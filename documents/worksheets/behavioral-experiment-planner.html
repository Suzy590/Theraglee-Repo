Test a Belief in the Real World

Arguing with a belief in your head rarely settles anything. This helps you design one small, safe experiment — a prediction, a test, a result — so real life gets to weigh in.

13 fields CBTAnxiety Basic
Some beliefs cannot be talked out of existence, but they can be tested. This is not an exam and there is no way to fail it: an experiment that proves your prediction right is just as useful as one that proves it wrong, because either way you learn something true. Keep the test small enough that you would actually do it this week, and write your prediction down before you start, so the result cannot be quietly rewritten afterwards.
1

The belief

The belief you want to test, written as a plain sentence — for example, “if I say no to a favor, people will be annoyed with me”
How much you believe it right now, in your own words — a little, mostly, completely
What you currently do to avoid finding out whether it is true
2

The prediction

If the belief is true, exactly what will happen during the test. Be specific enough that you could check it afterwards
What you fear most about doing the test, and how likely that feels
What would count as the belief being wrong, or at least less true than you thought
3

The test

The experiment itself: what you'll do, with whom, and where. Small enough to do this week
Day and time you'll run it, and anything you need to set up first
One way to make it a little safer without making it pointless — a shorter version, a kinder audience, a backup plan
4

The result

What actually happened, described the way a reporter would — what was said, what people did, how long it lasted
How you felt during it, and how you felt an hour afterward
5

What it means

Put the prediction next to the result. Where did they match, and where did they differ
How much you believe the original sentence now, and one thing you'd like to test next
