Worksheet

The People-Pleasing Pattern

Saying yes when you mean no usually feels like kindness in the moment and resentment later. This worksheet looks at one recent yes, what you actually wanted, what you feared, and one honest sentence to try.

12 fields Self-EsteemShame Basic
This is not a test, and wanting people to be happy with you is not a flaw. It is a skill that once kept you safe or loved. The trouble starts when it runs on its own and costs you things you never agreed to give. Look at one recent example with curiosity rather than blame. There are no right answers, only patterns worth knowing.
1

The recent yes

Something you said yes to recently that you did not want to do — a favor, a plan, extra work, a conversation
How you said it. The exact words if you can: “Sure, no problem”, “Of course”, “I’d love to”
How fast you answered, and what your body was doing while you said it
2

What you actually wanted

What you actually wanted to say, in plain words, to nobody but yourself
What you gave up by saying yes — time, rest, money, a plan of your own, a bit of self-respect
3

What you feared

Finish this: “If I had said no, I was afraid that they would…”
Finish this: “…and if they did that, it would mean that I am…”
Has that feared thing ever actually happened when you said no? What really happened instead?
4

The pattern

Where you learned that keeping people happy was your job. Who taught it, and what happened when you didn’t.
Who you say yes to most automatically, and who you can already be honest with. What is different about them?
5

One honest sentence

Write the honest sentence you could have said instead. Short, warm, no long excuse: “I can’t take that on this week.”
A request likely to come up in the next seven days, and the sentence you will use. Afterward, note what they actually said.
