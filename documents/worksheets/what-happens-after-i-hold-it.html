What Happens After I Hold It

The fear of holding a boundary is usually built on a prediction that has never been tested. This experiment has you write the prediction first, hold the line once, then record what actually happened.

13 fields Boundaries Basic
This is an experiment, not a test, and there is no result that counts as failing. You will pick one boundary you have been reluctant to hold, write down in detail what you expect to happen if you hold it, then hold it once and come back to write what actually happened. The two pieces of writing are the whole point. Predictions made in fear tend to be very specific and very wrong, but you can only find that out by checking.
1

The boundary

The boundary, stated in one plain sentence — “I leave at nine”, “I don't lend money”, “I'm not discussing my weight”
With whom, and in what kind of moment it usually comes up
How many times you've let it go rather than hold it, roughly, and what letting it go looks like
2

The prediction

Write exactly what you expect them to say or do, word for word if you can. Don't soften it
How you expect to feel in the first ten minutes, and how confident you are in that prediction (1–10)
What you expect to be true about the relationship a week later
The worst version of the outcome, in one sentence, and how likely it honestly feels (1–10)
3

Hold it once

When and where you'll hold it, and what you'll say. Date, place, the actual words
Something you'll do for yourself in the hour afterward, whatever happens
4

The result

What they actually said or did, word for word if you can remember it
How you actually felt in the first ten minutes, and how you felt a day later
Put the prediction and the result side by side. What did your fear get right, and what did it get wrong?
5

What you'll carry

One sentence you'll remember the next time this boundary comes up, based on what actually happened rather than what you feared
