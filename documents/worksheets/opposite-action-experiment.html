Acting Opposite to the Urge

When a feeling doesn't fit the facts, or is bigger than the moment calls for, doing the opposite of what it urges can shrink it. You'll pick one feeling, plan the opposite action, and test it.

11 fields Emotion RegulationDBT Basic
This is an experiment, not a test, and there is no right answer. Every emotion comes with an action it wants: fear wants to avoid, anger wants to attack, shame wants to hide, sadness wants to withdraw. Sometimes that action is exactly right. But when the feeling is out of proportion, or acting on it would make things worse, doing the opposite — all the way, with your whole body — can turn the feeling down. You are not pretending. You are giving your body different information.

The feeling and its urge

The feeling you want to work with, and a recent moment when it showed up — the situation in a sentence or two
What the feeling urges you to do — avoid the call, slam the door, apologize six times, stay in bed, go silent
Does the feeling fit the facts, and is its size in proportion to what happened? Say honestly what you think
If you acted fully on the urge, what would probably happen next, for you and for anyone else involved?

Planning the opposite

The opposite action, spelled out — not just “don't avoid” but “walk in, say hello, stay ten minutes”
What the opposite looks like in your body: posture, face, tone of voice, where your eyes go, the pace you move at
Your prediction. What you expect to feel while doing it, and what you expect the feeling to do afterwards (1–10)
