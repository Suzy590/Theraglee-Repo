The Guilt After a Boundary

Guilt after saying no does not mean you did something wrong. It means a rule got broken. This sheet helps you find the rule, work out where it came from, and decide whether it is actually yours.

12 fields BoundariesRelationships Basic
There is no right answer here and nobody grading your conscience. Guilt is a signal that some rule has been broken, and the only question worth asking is whose rule it was. Some of the rules we carry were handed to us by people who needed us to be easy, and we kept them long after those people stopped watching. This sheet is a slow look at one bout of guilt, so you can decide what to keep.
1

The guilt

The boundary you held, and who it was with
The guilt as it actually arrived — a thought, a stomach drop, a need to text and take it back? Describe it plainly
How long it lasted, and what you did with it — apologized, undid the boundary, sat with it?
2

The rule

Finish this sentence: “A good person would have…”
Finish this sentence: “If I say no, then I am…”
Now write the rule you broke as a single flat sentence, the way it would sound on a sign — “Never let anyone be disappointed in you”
3

Whose rule is it

Who first taught you this rule, and what they needed from you at the time
What happened, back then, when you broke it
Would you hand this rule to a child you love? If not, what would you tell them instead?
4

Rewrite or keep

If the rule is yours and you'd keep it, write it in your own words. If it isn't, write the rule you'd rather live by
The next time this guilt arrives, one sentence you'll say to it — not to argue, just to name whose voice it is
Something you'll notice in the next week about how long the guilt lasts when you don't act on it
