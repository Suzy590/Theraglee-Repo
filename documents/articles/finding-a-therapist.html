Finding a Therapist

You have decided to get help and now there are two hundred names and no way to choose. How to narrow it down, what to ask before you book, and how to tell early whether it is working.

Decision MakingWorry Visitor 947 words

You have got as far as deciding. Then you open a directory, and there are two hundred photographs, a dozen sets of letters after people's names, and no way to tell any of them apart. So the tab stays open for three weeks and nothing happens.

The deciding was the hard part emotionally. This part is mostly logistics, and logistics respond to a method.

Start with the three things that rule people out

Most of the two hundred are not actually options, and finding that out first makes the choice much smaller.

Money. What can you pay per session, and how often. Then look specifically for sliding scale, training clinics attached to universities, community mental health services, employee assistance programs through work, and anything your insurance covers. Ask about the lowest fee they offer rather than assuming the listed one is fixed.

Practicality. In person or video. Which evenings or hours you can actually make, every week, for months. A brilliant therapist at a time you cannot keep is worse than a good one you can.

The thing you want help with. Panic, a bereavement, something that happened to you, a relationship, drinking, low mood that will not lift. Say it in your own words. You do not need the right term.

Then look for the match, not the best one

There is no best therapist, only a good fit for this problem and this person. Two things are worth matching on.

Experience with your particular thing. Someone who works with trauma, or with eating difficulties, or with couples, or with grief. This matters more than any general impression of warmth.

The approach, roughly. Cognitive behavioral therapy and its relatives are structured, practical, and strong for panic, phobias, low mood and worry. Trauma-focused approaches are what you want if something happened. More open-ended talking therapy suits long-running patterns and questions about who you are. If you have no idea, say the problem and ask them what they would suggest.

Check they are properly qualified

Titles are protected differently in different places, so look for a license or registration with a professional body rather than a certificate from a course. Where you live will have a register you can search.

That one check matters more than the website, the photograph or the writing.

Email or call three, not one

Pick three and contact all of them. Most will offer a short free call, and that call is the real selection process.

Three, because the first one may have no space, the second may not feel right, and lining them up at once stops this taking three months.

Ask these on the call

Have your questions written down, because people forget them when nervous.

Do you work with this a lot. What would the plan roughly look like. How long do people usually come for. What is the fee, and do you have any lower-cost spaces. What is your cancellation policy. Where is your waiting list at. How will we know whether it is working.

Then one that tells you most: what should I do if I do not think it is helping.

A good answer to that is warm and specific. Someone who seems uncomfortable with the question is answering it.

Expect to feel awkward on the call, and ignore that

The call is not an audition and you do not have to explain everything. Two or three sentences about the problem is plenty. If the thought of speaking at all is the obstacle, write an email instead, and say that you would rather start by email.

Judge it on the right things after a few sessions

The first session is mostly history and practicalities and often feels strange. By around the third or fourth, you can start to tell.

Good signs: you feel heard rather than assessed, there is some sense of a direction, you leave with something to think about or try, and you can imagine telling this person something embarrassing.

Reasons to raise it or change: you spend the session managing their reactions, nothing seems to be going anywhere after a couple of months, they talk about themselves a great deal, you feel judged, or you dread it in a way that is about them rather than about the work.

Not every discomfort is a bad sign. Therapy that goes anywhere is uncomfortable in places. The difference is whether the discomfort is the work or the person.

It is allowed to not work out

A fair number of people see two or three therapists before they land somewhere useful, and that is a normal part of this rather than a failure. You can say, plainly: I do not think this is the right fit, thank you for your time. You do not owe a reason, and a professional will not be wounded by it.

While you wait

Waiting lists are real and can be long. In the meantime: ask to go on more than one list, ask about cancellation slots, look at group programs which often start sooner, and ask your doctor what is available locally that does not need a referral.

When to get help

If you are struggling to get started on this at all, your doctor is the simplest first step: they can refer you, tell you what exists locally, and treat the things medication helps with while you wait. Say plainly how bad it is rather than the tidier version, because what is available often depends on that. If you are in crisis, do not wait for an appointment, and if you ever feel you cannot go on, please reach out to an urgent service today rather than a waiting list.

If you are in crisis, call or text 988 (Suicide & Crisis Lifeline, US) or your local emergency number. Theraglee is a wellbeing tool, not a substitute for professional care.
