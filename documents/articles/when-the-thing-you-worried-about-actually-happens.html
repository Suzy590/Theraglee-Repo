When the Thing You Worried About Actually Happens

The bad news arrives and your first thought is that you were right. What the worry actually bought you, why being right feels so strange, and how to work the real problem now.

WorryThinking Traps Visitor 762 words

You worried about it for weeks. Then it happened. And underneath the shock there is a small, unpleasant note of recognition, as though some part of you is saying it knew.

This is a confusing position to be in, because everything you have ever read about worry assumes it was unnecessary. Here it was not. That changes the conversation and it is worth having properly.

What the worry actually bought you

Start here, because this is where people draw the wrong conclusion and carry it for years.

Worry is not what made you ready. Whatever preparation you did was done by the practical steps, the conversations, the money set aside. The hours of turning it over in bed did not contribute to any of those.

Being right about the outcome is not the same as having predicted it. You worried about a long list of things, and one of them happened. The others did not, and you will not count those.

And you were not spared anything by the rehearsal. People expect that having imagined it will take the edge off when it arrives. Almost nobody finds that it does. The shock lands anyway, on top of the weeks you already spent.

That is the real arithmetic. You paid in advance and the price did not come down.

Why being right feels so strange

Several things are happening at once, which is why it is hard to name.

There is a small satisfaction in it, and it feels shameful. It is not. Being right is a familiar comfort in a moment where nothing else is familiar, and your mind reaches for it.

There is the sense of a confirmed world view, which is the costly part. Worry presents the outcome as proof that worrying works, and that is the mechanism by which this gets worse rather than better.

And there is often guilt, which is the heaviest and the least deserved. Knowing something might happen is not causing it, and not preventing it is not the same as failing.

Working the real problem now

The thing has happened, which means it has stopped being a worry and become a situation. Those are handled completely differently.

Write down what is now actually true, in plain terms, without the forecast attached. Worry is about an unknown future. A situation has facts, and the facts are nearly always narrower than the dread was.

Then write what the next step is, not the whole path. The thing that has to happen this week. This is the point where people try to solve the entire aftermath in one evening and get nowhere.

Notice which parts of this are yours to do and which belong to somebody else, because a fair amount of it usually does. Carrying both sets is what exhausts people in the first weeks.

And let other people take the specific jobs they offer. Vague help is hard to accept and a specific offer is easy, which is why naming a task for somebody is a kindness to both of you.

Keeping the lesson from going wrong

This is the part that decides how the next year goes.

The conclusion is not that worry works. It is that a thing you feared can happen and be survivable, which is different and far more useful.

Notice if you start worrying harder now, as a sort of insurance. This is extremely common after an outcome like this and it is worth catching early, because the next few months set the habit.

Give attention to the things that actually helped, which were almost certainly concrete. Who showed up, what preparation mattered, what you did in the first week. That is the material for next time.

And expect a wobble around reminders and anniversaries. That is not a relapse, it is the ordinary shape of this.

When to bring somebody in

Worry that has an outcome attached to it can set hard, and that is worth taking seriously rather than waiting out.

It is worth talking to somebody if you cannot stop replaying what you should have seen, if you have become watchful in a way that is costing you sleep, if the worry has spread to things it never touched before, or if you are avoiding ordinary parts of life to keep it from happening again. A therapist who works with anxiety handles this directly and the work is usually shorter than people expect. Your doctor is a good first stop if you are not sure where to look, and asking early makes it simpler, not more serious.
