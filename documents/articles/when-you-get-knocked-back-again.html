When You Get Knocked Back Again

The fortieth application, the fourth audition, another no. How to keep going through repeated rejection without deciding it is a verdict on you.

ResilienceSelf-Esteem Visitor 877 words

The email starts with thank you for your interest. You already know. That is the ninth one, or the thirtieth, and this one you actually thought you had.

The first few were disappointing. Somewhere around the twentieth, something changed, and it stopped feeling like bad luck and started feeling like information about you.

What repetition does to the meaning

One rejection is an event. A long run of them turns into a story, and the story is almost always about your worth rather than about the process.

That shift happens quietly. I did not get that job becomes they can tell, becomes there is something wrong with me that other people can see and I cannot.

It helps to know this is what volume does to a person, rather than what the evidence supports. The same forty applications would produce the same feeling in anybody.

Separate the numbers from the verdict

Most of these processes have ratios that nobody tells you about.

Hiring is largely a matter of fit, timing and internal candidates. Auditions are cast for a look and a tone you cannot control. Dating is a numbers business with chemistry in it. Funding, publishing and grants reject the overwhelming majority of good applications.

So the honest reading of thirty nos in those systems is usually: this is what thirty attempts looks like. Not: thirty people assessed my value and agreed.

Writing down the actual ratio — how many attempts a result usually takes in your field — is worth more than any pep talk.

Ask for one piece of feedback, then stop asking

Feedback is useful once. After that it tends to become a way of searching for the flaw.

So ask one or two places, specifically: is there anything I could do differently next time? Most will not answer. The ones who do often say something small and fixable, and occasionally something that reframes the whole thing.

Then act on what is actionable and leave the rest. If three people say the same thing, that is a pattern worth work. One person's remark is one person's remark.

Count what you control

The outcome is not in your hands, so measure the parts that are.

Applications sent. Approaches made. Hours practiced. One new contact a week. A better version of the covering letter. A different route entirely.

Keep a tally of those and let it be the scoreboard, because a scoreboard of outcomes in a high-rejection process is designed to make you feel like a failure.

And set a quota rather than a goal: ten a week, three a day, whatever is sustainable. Quotas survive a bad week; hopes do not.

Protect the hour after a no

The damage usually happens in the hour afterward, not in the email.

So have a plan. Read it once, note anything useful, close it. Then do something physical for twenty minutes: a walk, the stairs, something heavy. Tell one person, briefly, who will not try to fix it.

Do not reply at length, do not reread your application looking for the mistake, and do not send the next one while raw, because it will show.

And give it a time limit. Twenty minutes of being properly fed up is healthy. Three days of it is where the story takes hold.

Keep something in your life that is not this

Long rejection runs take over, and that makes every single no heavier, because there is nothing else in the day to weigh against it.

So keep something with reliable returns in the week: exercise, a class, a craft, volunteering, time with people who like you for reasons unrelated to this. Something where effort and outcome are connected, which is exactly what the search is not.

And protect the relationships. People in a long stretch of this tend to withdraw out of embarrassment, which removes the thing that holds them up.

Reassess on a schedule, not on a bad day

There is a real question in here: how long do I keep going, and should I change the approach?

Answer it deliberately, every month or two, with somebody sensible, rather than at eleven at night after a rejection. On a bad night the answer is always that you should give up, and that answer is not information.

And consider that changing the approach is not quitting. A different sector, a different city, a different level, a course, a different kind of application: those are moves, not surrender.

When to get help with it

Please talk to a doctor or a therapist if the low mood has been there for weeks rather than days, if you are not sleeping or eating properly, if you have stopped applying altogether, or if the nos have hardened into a settled belief that you are worthless.

That is extremely common in long rejection stretches, it is treatable, and it is much easier to keep going once it lifts. A counselor can also help with the particular habit of reading every outcome as a verdict on you, which is specific, well understood and workable.

And if it has taken you somewhere dark, and you are not sure you want to be here, please tell a doctor or someone you trust today rather than carrying it alone.
