The First Weeks on a New Medication

Side effects before benefits, a waiting period nobody warned you about, and a lot of second-guessing. What the first few weeks are usually like, and what to ask your prescriber.

DepressionAnxiety Visitor 895 words

You started the tablets on Monday. By Wednesday you felt slightly sick and oddly wired, by Friday you were sleeping badly, and you still feel exactly as low as you did two weeks ago.

So you are sitting there wondering whether it is working, whether you are getting worse, and whether to stop.

The order of events surprises people

The thing nobody explains clearly: side effects usually arrive first and benefits usually arrive later.

That means the first week or two can feel like a bad deal — nausea, headache, a jittery feeling, strange sleep, a dry mouth — while the thing you took it for is unchanged.

Most of those early effects settle over the first couple of weeks. The benefit, for many medications used for mood and anxiety, builds slowly over several weeks, and often shows up in other people's observations before your own.

Knowing that shape in advance is the difference between riding out a hard couple of weeks and abandoning something that had not started yet.

What to ask before you take the first one

Five questions, and it is reasonable to ask every one of them.

What should I expect in the first week? How long before I should notice something? What are the side effects that mean I should call you rather than wait? What should I avoid — alcohol, other medicines, driving while I settle? And how long will I be on it?

Ask your pharmacist too. They have more time than most prescribers and they know interactions extremely well.

Write the answers down, because the version you remember a week later will be the frightening one.

Keep a very short record

One line a day is enough: sleep, side effects, mood out of ten, anything notable.

This matters because memory is unreliable when you feel bad. Three weeks in, a one-line record will often show a pattern you cannot feel: waking less, a slightly better afternoon, fewer bad hours.

It also gives your prescriber something real to work with at the review appointment rather than a general impression.

Do not stop on your own

If it is not suiting you, the answer is a phone call rather than stopping, because some medications need to be reduced gradually and stopping abruptly can make you feel much worse.

Equally, do not double a dose, skip days to make it last, or decide to take it only when you feel bad, unless that is what you were told to do. Steady is what makes most of these work.

And resist changing two things at once. Starting a tablet, stopping drinking, beginning therapy and changing your sleep in the same week leaves you with no idea what is doing what.

Things that make the first weeks easier

Take it at the same time every day, attached to something you already do.

Take it with food if it makes you feel sick, unless you were told otherwise. If it makes you sleepy, ask whether you can take it at night; if it winds you up, ask about mornings.

Go easy on caffeine and alcohol while things settle, because both amplify most of the early effects.

Tell one person you have started something, so there is somebody who can say you seem a bit better, which is often the first evidence available.

When to ring rather than wait

Some things should not be sat out, and you are not being a nuisance by calling.

A rash, swelling, fever, or anything that looks like an allergic reaction. Severe agitation or being unable to sit still. Sickness or diarrhea that will not stop. Confusion, a racing heart, or a high temperature with sweating and shaking. Thoughts of harming yourself, or feeling much worse rather than better.

That last one matters particularly in the first weeks, and particularly for younger people. It is a known and specific reason to contact your prescriber straight away rather than waiting for the review.

Medication is one part of it

It is worth saying plainly: a tablet does not replace the rest.

Sleep, daylight, movement, food, company and therapy all do real work, and the medication often makes those possible rather than unnecessary. The people who do best generally use both.

And if this one does not suit you, that is common and it is not the end of the road. There are many options, and finding the right fit is frequently a process of two or three attempts rather than a verdict.

When to get help

Please talk to your doctor or prescriber if the side effects are not settling after a couple of weeks, if there has been no change at all by the time they said to expect one, or if anything on the list above happens; that conversation is a normal part of treatment rather than a complaint.

Ask for a review date before you leave the appointment, and take your one-line record with you. A pharmacist, a doctor or a nurse prescriber are all reasonable people to ask about a dose, a time of day, or an interaction.

And if you are feeling worse rather than better, and you are not sure you want to be here, please tell a doctor or someone you trust today rather than waiting for the next appointment. In the US you can call or text 988 at any time.

If you are in crisis, call or text 988 (Suicide & Crisis Lifeline, US) or your local emergency number. Theraglee is a wellbeing tool, not a substitute for professional care.
