Going to Something Alone

The class, the talk, the party where you know one person. How to walk in on your own without the twenty minutes of standing by the wall deciding to leave.

Social AnxietyLoneliness Visitor 975 words

You have the details. You said you would come. And now it is forty minutes before, you are thinking about the part where you walk in and do not know anyone, and the evening at home is looking better by the minute.

Going somewhere alone is a specific hurdle, separate from whether you want to be there. Plenty of people who like the thing still do not go, because the entrance is the hard part and there is nobody to do it with.

What is actually frightening

It is rarely the event. It is the first ten minutes.

Walking in and having nobody to stand beside. Not knowing where to put yourself. Being visibly alone, which feels like being visibly unwanted. And the fear of the specific moment where you are standing with a drink, looking at your phone, aware of yourself.

Notice that all of that is about the arrival. Once something has started — the class, the talk, the game — there is a structure and you are fine. So the whole problem is about twenty minutes, and twenty minutes can be planned for.

Arrive at the useful time

Most people arrive right on time, which is the worst moment: the room is full, groups have formed, and you have to break into one.

Arriving five or ten minutes early is much easier. The room is half empty, nobody is established, the only other people there are also standing around, and talking to someone next to you is the obvious thing rather than an intrusion.

The organizer is usually there early too, and they are the single easiest person to talk to, because welcoming people is their job for the evening.

Pick something with a structure

If going alone is hard, the kind of event matters enormously.

Classes, courses, teams, choirs, volunteering, run clubs, workshops, book groups: all of these give you something to do, a reason to be there, a shape to the time, and natural conversation about the activity. They also repeat, which means you do not have to achieve anything tonight.

Open-ended parties and mixers are the hardest version, because the only activity is socializing. If you are building up to things, do not start there.

Have your first three sentences ready

The dread is mostly about not knowing what to say, so decide in advance and stop rehearsing.

An opener that works almost anywhere: is this your first time here. Then, how did you find out about it, and have you done this sort of thing before.

Those three cover the first few minutes with anyone, and they share one useful feature: they are about the situation you are both in, which is the easiest subject available.

If you prefer a job to a script, take one. Offer to help set up chairs, hand out papers, carry something. Doing anything at all removes the standing-around problem completely, and it puts you next to people with a reason to talk.

Give yourself an exit and a minimum

Two decisions, made before you go, that make the whole thing easier.

A minimum: I will stay thirty minutes. That is short enough to agree to and long enough that you almost always stay longer.

An exit: how you will leave, what you will say, whether you need to tell anyone. Knowing you can go, cleanly, is often the thing that lets you walk in at all.

And decide both in advance, because deciding at the door always comes out as going home.

What to do in the awkward gap

There will be a stretch where you are standing alone. Expect it rather than treating it as a failure.

Put the phone away, because it is the one thing that makes you unapproachable. Instead: get a drink, read the notice board, look at whatever is on the walls, go and look at the equipment or the books or the food. Moving with a small purpose reads completely differently from standing still.

Then join the nearest pair rather than a group of five. Two people talking is easy to stand near; a circle of five is a closed shape.

And know that almost nobody is watching. The feeling of being conspicuous is the least accurate feeling in the room.

Afterward, score it honestly

The temptation is to come home and audit your own performance. That is what makes the next one harder.

So judge it on two things only: did you go, and would you go again. Not whether you were charming, not whether you made a friend.

Going four times to the same thing beats one brilliant evening somewhere you never return to. Familiarity is what eventually turns a room of strangers into people who say your name when you arrive, and it only comes from repetition.

Make the second time easier than the first

Book or commit to the next one before you leave the building, if you can. Momentum after a reasonable evening is worth a great deal.

And if you spoke to one person you liked, it is completely normal to say: I will probably come again next week, are you coming. That sentence is how most adult acquaintances start.

When the barrier is bigger than nerves

If the thought of walking in alone brings real dread, if you have been turning down invitations for months, or if you have stopped going to anything where you might be observed, that is worth treating rather than willing yourself through.

Social anxiety responds very well to practical therapy, often in a fairly small number of sessions, and a therapist can help you build up to the hard version rather than failing at it repeatedly. If avoiding things has left you isolated, or if the loneliness underneath has started to feel heavy, a doctor or a therapist is the right place to start.
