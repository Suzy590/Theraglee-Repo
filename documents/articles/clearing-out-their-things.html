Clearing Out Their Things

The wardrobe, the shed, the handwriting on a shopping list. Why sorting a person's belongings is harder than anyone warns you, and how to do it at a pace you can bear.

LossSelf-Compassion Visitor 986 words

You have opened the wardrobe four times and closed it again. There is a coat in there that still smells of them, and a shopping list in their handwriting on the kitchen counter that you cannot throw away.

Everyone treats this as a practical job. It is not a practical job. It is one of the hardest parts of losing someone, and almost nobody is warned about it.

Why objects hit so hard

Grief does not arrive on schedule; it arrives through the senses.

A coat holds a smell. A mug has the chip they made. A diary has next week's appointments in it, written by someone who expected to be there. Those details land in a way that a conversation about the funeral never does, because they are evidence of an ordinary life that was still going.

And the small things are often worse than the big ones. People brace for the photographs and are undone by a half-used tube of hand cream.

There is no deadline, except the ones that are real

Most of this can wait. If the house does not have to be cleared, there is no reason at all to do it by a particular date, and doing it too early is something people regret more often than doing it late.

Where there is a real deadline — a rented property, a care home room, a sale — find out exactly what it is and how much flexibility exists, because the panic in your head is usually tighter than the actual terms.

If the room has to be emptied fast, you do not have to sort as you go. Box it, label it, store it, and do the deciding months later when you can. That is not avoidance; it is separating two jobs that do not have to happen at once.

Do it in short shifts, with someone there

Two hours is plenty. Four will flatten you for days.

Have someone with you if you can, and choose them carefully: not the person who wants to be efficient, but the one who can sit while you hold a jumper and say nothing. Their job is to be there, make tea, and carry boxes to the car.

Eat beforehand, drink water, and plan something gentle afterward. Do not schedule anything that needs you to be competent for the rest of the day.

And agree a signal for stopping, out loud, in advance: when I say I am done, we stop. Then stop.

Start with the easy shelf

Work from the least loaded thing toward the most, not in the order the room suggests.

Cleaning products, old paperwork, the contents of the bathroom cabinet, food in the cupboards. Those can mostly go without much feeling, and getting through them builds a little momentum.

Leave for last the things you already know are difficult: the clothes, the handwriting, the letters, the toolbox, the handbag. And give yourself permission to leave one drawer closed for a year.

Keep less than you want to, and more than feels sensible

People swerve into one of two ditches: keeping everything, which turns a garage into a shrine nobody can face, or clearing it all in a week and grieving the loss twice.

A middle path works better. Keep a small number of things, on purpose, and choose them for use rather than for storage: the mug you drink from, the jumper you wear, the tools in your shed, one picture out where you can see it.

Photograph what you are letting go of, if that helps. Many people find the picture holds the memory perfectly well once the object is gone.

And one or two things can simply be kept for no reason at all. A shopping list in their handwriting is allowed to live in a drawer for the rest of your life.

Giving things away is easier when you know where they go

It is much less painful to pass something to a person or a place than to a skip.

A friend who would wear the coat. The charity shop they used. Tools to someone starting out. Books to the library. A quilt or a cushion made from a shirt, if that is the sort of thing you like.

Children and grandchildren often want something small and specific rather than furniture. Asking them is usually better than guessing.

When other people are involved

Families fall out over objects, and it is almost never really about the objects.

Say the real thing where you can: I am not fighting you for the clock, I am frightened of having nothing left of him. That sentence prevents more arguments than any fair-division system.

Practically: agree the few items that matter to each person first, take turns rather than negotiating item by item, keep anything contested in a box rather than deciding while everyone is raw, and let somebody outside the family help if it has already gone wrong.

When to get some help with this

Please talk to a doctor or a therapist if months have passed and you cannot enter the room at all, if the house has become impossible to live in because nothing can be moved, or if the grief has not softened at all with time and is keeping you from working, eating or sleeping.

Grief counseling exists for exactly this, and bereavement services will often see you without a referral; clearing a house is one of the most common things people bring, and nobody there will think you are being sentimental. A therapist can also help with the guilt that tends to come with it, which is nearly universal and almost never deserved.

And if the loss has taken you somewhere dark, and you are not sure you want to be here, please tell a doctor or someone you trust today. In the US you can call or text 988 at any time.

If you are in crisis, call or text 988 (Suicide & Crisis Lifeline, US) or your local emergency number. Theraglee is a wellbeing tool, not a substitute for professional care.
