Being With Someone Who Asks the Same Question

They have asked about the appointment eleven times since lunch. Why correcting does not work, what the question is usually really about, and how to get through an afternoon without snapping.

CaregivingMindfulness Visitor 905 words

What time is the appointment. You answer. Four minutes later: what time is the appointment. You answer again, a little flatter. By the eighth time your voice has an edge in it that you can hear and hate.

Then the guilt, because none of this is their fault, and you know that, and you are still at the end of your patience.

Why repeating happens

When short-term memory is failing, the question does not feel repeated to the person asking. Each time is the first time. They are not being difficult, not testing you, not refusing to listen. The information went in and did not stay.

So the instinct to say I have told you three times does nothing useful. They have no memory of the three times, so what they hear is only that they have done something wrong, and what is left afterward is the feeling of being scolded without knowing why. That feeling lasts long after the facts have gone.

Answer the feeling, not only the question

Most repeated questions are carrying something underneath them, and the something is usually anxiety.

What time is the appointment often means am I going to be all right, or is someone taking me, or am I going to be left on my own.

So answer both parts. Eleven o'clock, and I am driving you, so you do not need to do anything. Then: shall we have a cup of tea while we wait.

When the underneath part gets answered, the question frequently slows down, because the worry was what kept generating it.

Make the answer external

If the information is written down somewhere they can see, the question has somewhere else to go.

A whiteboard in the kitchen with today's date, the day, and the one or two things that are happening. A note by the chair. A clock with the day on it. A card in their pocket.

Then your answer can become a gesture rather than a sentence: it is on the board, look, eleven o'clock. People manage this far better than being told, because it gives back a little independence instead of needing you every time.

Keep your answers identical and short

Varying the wording makes it harder, and explaining more makes it harder still. Choose one short sentence and use the same one every time, in the same tone.

Short also protects you. A nine-word answer twelve times is survivable. A paragraph twelve times is not.

Do not argue with the version of reality they are in

If they believe their mother is coming, or that they still live in the old house, a factual correction is usually a small cruelty with no benefit. It cannot be absorbed, and it lands as an argument.

Go with the feeling instead. You are missing her. Tell me about her. That is not lying to them, it is answering what is actually present, which is the longing rather than the schedule.

Change the subject with your body, not your voice

When a loop will not break, moving usually works better than talking. Stand up, hand them something, go and look out of the window, put the radio on, go outside, start a task together, offer food.

A loop is partly an attentional groove. New input is more effective at breaking it than better phrasing.

Give yourself a way out of the room

You will lose your patience sometimes. Everybody does. Having a planned exit makes it far less likely to come out as an edge in your voice.

Decide in advance: when I notice the tightness in my jaw, I go and put the kettle on. Two minutes in another room, cold water on your wrists, one slow breath out, then back.

Repetition is genuinely hard on an adult brain. The irritation is not a sign you love them less.

Mind the afternoons

Confusion and repetition usually get worse later in the day, when everyone is tired. If you can, put the demanding parts of the day in the morning, keep the late afternoon quiet and well lit, and lower what you are trying to achieve after four o'clock.

And look at the obvious physical things, because they make everything worse and are fixable: poor sleep, dehydration, constipation, an infection, hearing aids not in, glasses lost, a new medication.

Let yourself be a person, not only a carer

The people who manage this for years are not the ones with the most patience. They are the ones who get breaks, who have someone to be honest with, and who have stopped expecting themselves to be endlessly calm.

Say the hard thing to one person: today I snapped at my father for asking the same question and I feel awful. You will almost always hear a version of it back.

When to get help

Please talk to their doctor if the repeating is new, has got worse quickly, or came with confusion, falls or a change in how they are generally, since infections, medication and other treatable things cause exactly this and get missed. Ask about a memory assessment if there has not been one, and ask about a carer's assessment for yourself, which is a real thing you are entitled to in most places. If you are not sleeping, low, or dreading the days, please tell your own doctor or a counselor, because carers get ill quietly and nobody checks unless you say so.

If you are in crisis, call or text 988 (Suicide & Crisis Lifeline, US) or your local emergency number. Theraglee is a wellbeing tool, not a substitute for professional care.
