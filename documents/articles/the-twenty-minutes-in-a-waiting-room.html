The Twenty Minutes in a Waiting Room

The appointment is not the hard part. The waiting is. Why that room does what it does, what to do with the time, and how to stop the dread from spreading to the next one.

PanicStress Visitor 795 words

You got there early because you always do. Now there are nineteen minutes, a row of chairs, a television nobody is watching, and a door that opens occasionally for somebody else.

Plenty of people who manage the appointment itself without much trouble find this part is where everything goes wrong. That is not strange, and there are reasons for it.

What that room does

The waiting room is almost perfectly designed to work against you, without meaning to be.

You have nothing to do, which leaves your attention free to go looking. A mind with no task and a worry available will find the worry every time.

You cannot leave, or feel you cannot. That alone raises the temperature, and it is the single biggest factor for most people.

You do not know when it will end. A known twenty minutes is far easier than an unknown one, and the door opening for somebody else resets the clock each time.

And your body is already slightly up. Getting there, parking, being on time, the lobby. By the time you sit down the baseline is raised before anything has happened.

Add the quiet, the other people, and the general sense that something is about to be done to you, and the room does most of the work.

What to do with the time

The aim is not to be calm. The aim is to get through twenty minutes without the dread building, and that is a much easier target.

Give yourself a task before you sit down. A specific one, decided in advance. A chapter, a crossword, a list, something you are actually doing rather than something you are using to distract yourself.

Keep your eyes on something far away rather than on your phone. Reading close up with your head down is the shape that makes the chest tightness worse.

Breathe out longer than you breathe in. Not deep breaths, which can make things worse, just a slow out-breath and a pause. Four or five of them is usually enough to turn the dial down a notch.

Press your feet into the floor and your back into the chair and notice both. This is dull, unimpressive, and it does more in a waiting room than anything else on this list.

And go outside if you need to. Tell the desk you are stepping out for a minute and do it. Almost nobody does this and it is completely allowed.

If it starts climbing anyway

Sometimes it moves faster than the twenty minutes and you feel it coming.

Name what is happening, in those words. This is my body doing the alarm thing. Naming it does not stop it and it reliably keeps it from doubling.

Remember that the feeling peaks and comes down on its own. It cannot climb indefinitely. Waiting it out is not giving in, it is the actual mechanism by which it ends.

Cold on the back of your neck or your wrists helps more than it sounds like it should, and a restroom faucet is usually close.

And tell the desk if you are struggling. Receptionists in medical settings see this constantly and will usually find you somewhere quieter or move you up.

Keeping it from spreading

This is the part that matters beyond today.

Do not cancel the next one. Avoidance works brilliantly for an afternoon and makes the following appointment harder, and that is how a difficult room becomes a difficult year.

Book early in the day when you can, since both the waiting and the dread grow with the hours before.

Bring somebody if that helps, and do not be embarrassed about it. An adult sitting with an adult in a waiting room is ordinary.

And ask whether you can wait outside or in the car and be called or texted. Many places will do this if asked and almost nobody asks.

When to say something about it

If this is happening at every appointment, it is worth naming rather than enduring.

Tell the clinician you are seeing. A sentence as plain as I find waiting rooms very hard is useful information to them, it is more common than you would guess, and it often changes how your appointments get booked.

It is worth asking for more help if the dread is now starting days in advance, if you have put off or canceled care because of it, or if what happens in the room is a full panic rather than discomfort. That is well-understood ground and the treatment for it is specific, short and works well. Your doctor can refer you, and a therapist who works with panic will have seen exactly this many times. In the US you can call or text 988 at any time.
