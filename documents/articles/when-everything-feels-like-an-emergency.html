When Everything Feels Like an Emergency

Answering email at eleven at night about something that could wait a week. Why urgency is a feeling rather than a fact, how to sort by consequence, and the pause worth building in.

DBTStress Visitor 767 words

The email could be answered tomorrow and you answer it now, at eleven at night. The leak will not get worse before Monday and you are calling plumbers on a Sunday. Somebody mentions a problem and you are already standing up.

Nothing is actually on fire. But the feeling says everything is, all the time, and living at that speed costs you more than the tasks ever did.

Urgency is a feeling, not a fact

This is the distinction that does the most work, and it is easy to lose.

A deadline is a fact. A gas leak is a fact. The sensation of must-be-done-now is a feeling, generated by your body, and it attaches itself to things with no regard for their actual timescale.

Which means the useful question is not what do I do first. It is: is this urgent, or does it feel urgent. Those have different answers surprisingly often.

Sort by consequence, not by feeling

So ask the plain question about each thing: what actually happens if this waits until tomorrow, or until next week.

Most answers are nothing. Some are a small cost you can name. A few are real: a deadline today, a medication, a child, a genuine safety matter.

Write the three categories if the week has got away from you. Real deadline today. Costs something to delay. Nothing happens. Most people find the third list is the longest and is where their evening went.

Learn what the feeling is actually telling you

Urgency almost always arrives from somewhere, and knowing the source makes it easier to discount.

Anxiety is the common one. Doing something gives relief, so the pull to act is really a pull to stop feeling uncomfortable, and it does not matter much what the action is.

Sometimes it is somebody else's panic landing on you, and their emergency has become your afternoon without either of you noticing.

Sometimes it is history: if things were genuinely unpredictable for you once, acting fast was sensible, and the habit outlived the circumstances.

And sometimes it is that the important thing is hard, so an urgent small thing is a relief. That is the version that eats careers.

Put a delay between the feeling and the hand

The skill here is not deciding better. It is slowing the gap between the urge and the action.

So pick a standard pause and use it for anything that is not actually urgent. Ten minutes for a message. Overnight for a decision. A day for a reply you have feelings about.

Write the thing and do not send it. Draft the list and look at it in the morning. Say I will come back to you tomorrow, which is almost always available and almost never refused.

The feeling fades on its own in that gap far more often than people expect, and what is left after it fades is the real priority.

Do one thing at the speed it deserves

A braced body makes everything fast and shallow, which is why urgent days produce so little.

So take one task and deliberately do it at its proper pace. Sit down for it. One tab. Finish it. Notice how long it actually took, which is usually less than the scattered version.

That is also how you find out that the day did not require the speed. It required three things done properly.

Watch the edges

Living at this pitch has costs that show up away from the task list.

Not being able to sit through an evening. Snapping at people over nothing. Checking constantly. Feeling bored and uneasy when there is nothing pressing, and then creating something pressing.

That last one is the giveaway, and it is worth catching, because it means the urgency is not coming from your circumstances.

And remember the plain arithmetic: treating everything as urgent is the same as having no priorities, because when all of it is first, nothing is.

When to get help

Please talk to a therapist if you cannot rest without a task, if the sense of emergency is there most days, or if slowing down produces real distress rather than relief, because that pattern is common, is usually anxiety doing the driving, and responds well to treatment. Skills for exactly this come from dialectical behavior therapy, and learning them with a professional works better than reading about them. See your doctor if you are not sleeping, if your heart races through ordinary days, or if you have been running at this speed for months, since the physical bill for that is real and worth checking.

If you are in crisis, call or text 988 (Suicide & Crisis Lifeline, US) or your local emergency number. Theraglee is a wellbeing tool, not a substitute for professional care.
