How to Support a Friend Who Is Grieving

Someone you care about has lost someone, and you do not know what to say. Most people do not. What actually helps, what quietly hurts, and how to keep showing up after everyone else has moved on.

GriefRelationships Visitor 933 words

Your friend's mother died. Or their partner, or their child, or their best friend. You found out, and your first feeling after the shock was a kind of helpless panic: what do I say? What do I do? Everything that comes to mind sounds either too small or too much.

That panic is common, and it is the reason many grieving people end up surrounded by silence. Friends who care deeply stay away because they are afraid of getting it wrong. So here is the most important thing first: getting it wrong and showing up is almost always better than getting it right by staying away.

Say the simple thing

You do not need the perfect words. There are none. What a grieving person needs to hear is that you know, that you are sorry, and that you are here. I am so sorry. I am thinking of you. I do not know what to say, but I love you. That is enough. It is more than enough.

Say the name of the person who died. People often avoid it, worried it will cause pain, but the pain is already there. Hearing the name says that the person mattered and is not being erased.

Things that sound kind and are not

Some phrases land badly, even when they come from love. They are in a better place. Everything happens for a reason. At least they are not suffering anymore. At least you had so many years. God needed another angel. I know exactly how you feel.

Each of these tries to shrink the loss or explain it away, and the person grieving does not want it shrunk. They want it witnessed. If you catch yourself about to say at least, stop. Say I am so sorry instead.

Do not ask what you can do

Let me know if you need anything is offered with the best intentions, and it almost never works. A grieving person cannot think of what they need, and even if they could, asking for it takes energy they do not have.

Instead, offer something specific and small. I am dropping dinner on your porch Thursday; you do not need to answer the door. I can take the kids Saturday morning. I am going to the store; text me a list. I will sit with you and we do not have to talk. Then do it. Specific offers can be accepted or declined with a single word, and that is all they have in them.

Let them be however they are

Grief does not look one way. Your friend may cry constantly, or not at all. They may want to talk about the person for hours, or not be able to say the name. They may laugh at something and then feel guilty about laughing. They may be angry, at the doctors, at the person who died, at you.

None of this needs correcting. Your job is not to move them through it or to cheer them up. It is to be with them where they are. If they want to tell the same story for the fifth time, listen for the fifth time. If they want silence, sit in it.

Keep showing up after the casseroles stop

In the first two weeks there are flowers, food, and messages. Then the funeral is over and everyone goes back to their lives, and that is when the hardest part often begins. The house is quiet. The phone stops. The world expects the grieving person to be finished, and they are nowhere near finished.

This is where you can matter most. Put dates in your calendar: one month, three months, six months, the person's birthday, the anniversary of the death. Send a message on those days. I am thinking of you and of your dad today. Keep inviting them to things, even when they keep saying no; the invitation itself says they are still wanted.

Do not make it about you

You may have your own grief, or a story about a loss you went through. Save most of it. A brief I lost my mother too, and I remember how it felt can help; a long account of your own experience shifts the weight onto them to comfort you. Let your friend be the one who is grieving right now.

Watch for when it becomes something more

Grief is not an illness, and it does not need fixing. But sometimes it becomes something a person cannot carry alone. If, months on, your friend is not eating or sleeping, cannot work, has withdrawn from everyone, or says things that suggest they do not want to be here, take it seriously. Ask them directly and gently whether they are thinking about hurting themselves. Asking does not plant the idea. In the US you can call or text 988 at any time.

Take care of yourself too

Supporting someone through grief is heavy. You can feel helpless, drained, and sad, and you may be grieving as well. That is allowed. Lean on other people so you are not the only one carrying it, and let your own feelings be real.

If your friend is struggling beyond what love and casseroles can reach, encourage them to talk to a therapist or a grief counselor, and offer to help them find one. And if the weight of being there for them is more than you can hold, you can talk to a professional too. Helping someone grieve is one of the kindest things a person can do, and you do not have to do it alone.

If you are in crisis, call or text 988 (Suicide & Crisis Lifeline, US) or your local emergency number. Theraglee is a wellbeing tool, not a substitute for professional care.
