When You Cannot Tell Hunger From Anxiety

The same hollow feeling could be hunger, nerves or tiredness, and reading it wrong sends the day sideways. Why the signals overlap, and a few ways to tell them apart in the moment.

BodyGrounding Visitor 757 words

There is a feeling in your middle. It could be that you have not eaten since seven. It could be the conversation you have at four. It could be that you slept badly. You genuinely cannot tell, and whichever one you guess, you will act on it.

Guess wrong and you eat when you were nervous, or you push through a meeting with nothing in you since breakfast, and the afternoon goes worse than it needed to.

Why the signals overlap

This is not a failure of attention. The two states are genuinely similar from the inside.

They use some of the same equipment. Both produce changes in the stomach, both can make you shaky and light-headed, and both make it harder to concentrate.

They also happen together. Being anxious often means not eating, and not eating makes anxiety worse, so by the afternoon you usually have both and no way to tell which is louder.

The reading gets quieter with disuse. People who have spent years eating by the clock, or ignoring the signal to get through a shift, end up with a less distinct sense of it, and that is reversible rather than permanent.

And a few things blur it directly. Caffeine on an empty stomach produces something almost identical to both. So do several medications, poor sleep and a long stretch of stress.

Things that tell them apart

None of these is perfect and together they do fairly well.

Check the clock before the feeling. If it has been four or five hours, hunger is probably in the mix regardless of what else is going on. This one small fact resolves most cases.

Notice where it is and what it does. Hunger tends to be lower, hollow, and comes in waves that build. Anxiety tends to be higher in the chest and throat, more constant, and often arrives with a thought attached.

Look for the thought. Anxiety usually comes with a subject, even a vague one. Hunger does not have an opinion about this afternoon.

See whether it responds to a small amount of food. A few bites of something plain settles hunger noticeably within ten minutes and does very little for anxiety. That is a useful test rather than a meal.

And ask what time of day it is. Mid-afternoon is when these two most commonly get confused, because it is when both are most likely to be present at once.

What to do when you still cannot tell

The answer is usually to treat the hunger, because it is the cheaper mistake.

Eat something small and plain anyway. If it was hunger, it is dealt with. If it was anxiety, you have lost nothing and you are now better equipped for it. Facing an anxious afternoon with nothing in you is the version that goes badly.

Then do one short physical thing. Stand up, go outside, cold water on your wrists. If something was anxiety, this is where it shows, and it separates the two faster than thinking about it.

And give it ten minutes before concluding anything. Both of these change over that span and in different directions, which is itself the clearest signal you will get.

Getting the reading back

If this has been blurred for a long time, it does come back, and the method is dull and effective.

Eat at roughly regular times for a few weeks, even without a clear signal. Steady intake makes the real hunger signal easier to pick out against the background.

Pause briefly before eating and note what you notice, without changing the decision. You are collecting readings rather than enforcing anything.

And keep an eye on caffeine, which is the single most common thing muddying this for people who cannot work out why they feel hollow and jittery at eleven.

When it is worth raising with somebody

This is ordinary and extremely common, particularly in people who are stressed or busy.

It is worth mentioning to a doctor if you have lost weight without meaning to, if eating is regularly painful or uncomfortable, or if the shakiness and light-headedness are severe or happen even after eating, since there are physical causes worth ruling out.

And it is worth taking to a therapist if the confusion has become a source of anxiety in itself, if you are regularly going whole days without eating, or if decisions about food have started to feel loaded and difficult. Both of those are workable, and the earlier they are raised the simpler the work tends to be.
