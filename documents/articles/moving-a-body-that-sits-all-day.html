Moving a Body That Sits All Day

Nine hours in a chair leaves you stiff, achy and oddly tired. What sitting actually does, and the small movements through the day that undo most of it.

MovementBody Visitor 934 words

You get up from the desk at six and your hips do not want to straighten. Your neck aches on one side. Your lower back has that dull tightness that was not there at nine in the morning, and despite having done nothing physical you feel wrung out.

Then the advice arrives: you should exercise more. Which may be true and is not the main thing going on here.

What sitting does

Long stretches of sitting are not harmful because sitting is bad. They are a problem because nothing changes. Muscles held in one position get stiff and shortened, the ones that should be working switch off, circulation slows, and your spine stays in one shape for hours.

The result is predictable: tight hips and hamstrings, a rounded upper back, a neck doing the work of holding your head forward, and a lower back that aches without ever being injured.

And the tiredness is real. Being still for nine hours leaves most people less alert than moving does, which is why the afternoon slump is partly a movement problem rather than only a sleep one.

Frequency beats intensity here

This is the key point. An hour at the gym after work is good for you, and it does not undo nine hours of stillness. What undoes stillness is interrupting it.

Standing up and moving for one or two minutes every half hour or so does more for stiffness, circulation and alertness than one long session. Any excuse will do: fill the water glass, take the call standing, walk to the printer, go to a different floor for the bathroom, stand up whenever the phone rings.

Set something to prompt you, because nobody remembers. A repeating alarm, a smartwatch nudge, or a glass of water small enough that refilling it is the reminder.

The four movements worth knowing

If you only do a handful of things, these address what sitting actually does.

Open the front of the hips. Stand up, step one foot back, keep yourself upright and press the back hip forward gently. Twenty or thirty seconds each side. This is the single most useful one for a stiff lower back.

Open the chest and shoulders. Stand in a doorway, forearms on the frame, step through until you feel the stretch across the chest. Or clasp your hands behind your back and lift them slightly.

Move your spine. Sitting or standing, round the lower back and then arch it slowly, eight or ten times. Then turn gently to each side.

Chin and neck. Instead of stretching the neck, slide your chin straight back as though making a double chin, hold a few seconds, repeat ten times. That counters the forward head position.

None of this requires a mat, a change of clothes, or anyone watching.

Set the chair up once, properly

A ten-minute setup beats years of adjusting your body to bad furniture.

Feet flat on the floor or a box, knees roughly level with hips. Screen at eye height so you are not looking down, which is where neck pain comes from. Keyboard close enough that your elbows stay by your sides. The back of the chair actually supporting your lower back, with a rolled towel behind it if it does not.

If you use a laptop all day, raise the screen and add a separate keyboard. It is the cheapest fix available for neck and shoulder pain.

Standing is not the answer by itself

Standing desks help some people and standing still for six hours brings its own aches, in the feet, knees and lower back. The useful version is alternating: a while sitting, a while standing, moving between the two.

If you have one, change position every half hour rather than committing to a whole morning upright.

Walk at the edges of the day

A walk before work and a walk after it do more than people expect, partly for the stiffness and partly because they bracket the day. Ten minutes each is enough to matter.

Stairs are the most efficient thing available to most desk workers: one flight, twice a day, gets you breathing harder than a corridor walk ever will.

Build the strength that sitting takes away

Over years, sitting leaves the hips, back and shoulders weak as well as stiff, and weakness is what turns into pain. Two short sessions a week of simple strength work changes that: sitting down and standing up from a chair slowly, pushing away from a wall, going up onto your toes, holding a plank position.

Ten minutes, twice a week, with no equipment. The point is not fitness, it is being able to sit all day without hurting.

Treat pain as information rather than something to push through

Aches that come and go with position are usually about stillness and setup. Pain that is worsening, that wakes you at night, that travels down an arm or a leg, or that comes with numbness, weakness or pins and needles is different and should be looked at rather than stretched.

When to get help

Please see your doctor or a physical therapist if back, neck or shoulder pain has lasted more than a few weeks, is getting worse, wakes you at night, or comes with numbness, tingling or weakness anywhere. A physical therapist can look at how you sit and give you the three movements that fit your particular body, which beats any general list. If your employer has a workplace assessment available, ask for one, since getting the desk right is usually cheaper and more effective than treating what it does to you.

If you are in crisis, call or text 988 (Suicide & Crisis Lifeline, US) or your local emergency number. Theraglee is a wellbeing tool, not a substitute for professional care.
