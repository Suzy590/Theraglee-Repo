Turning an Age That Bothers You

Thirty, forty, fifty, and a discomfort you feel silly admitting to. What a round number actually does, how to sort the real losses from the borrowed ones, and how to handle the day.

Life TransitionsValues Visitor 859 words

The number is coming. Thirty, forty, fifty, sixty, whichever one it is for you. And instead of the mild indifference you expected, there is something uncomfortable in the weeks beforehand that you are not sure you are allowed to mention, because it sounds vain, or ungrateful, or ridiculous.

It is none of those. Round numbers do something specific to people, and understanding what makes it much easier to pass through.

What the number actually does

Nothing changes on the day. What changes is that the number makes you take stock, and stock-taking is where the discomfort lives.

A round age invites a comparison: here is where I am, against where I thought I would be. Almost nobody comes out of that comparison neutral, because the expectations were set by a younger person with a different set of facts.

It also moves you, in your own head, into a different category. Not the age so much as what the age is supposed to mean, which is largely borrowed from people who were that age when you were a child.

And it quietly rules some things out. That is the real weight in it, and it is worth naming rather than talking yourself out of.

Separate the real losses from the borrowed ones

Some of what arrives is genuine and deserves acknowledging rather than arguing with.

There are things that will not happen now, or not in the form you imagined. There are doors that close with each decade, and pretending otherwise does not help.

But a great deal of it is borrowed: ideas about what you should have achieved by now, what a person your age does and does not do, what you are too old for. Those came from somewhere else and most of them are not even accurate anymore.

So write the two lists. Actually lost. Only assumed. The second list is usually much longer, and crossing things off it is where the relief is.

Check what you are comparing against

The comparison is rarely with your own actual expectations. It is with other people's visible outcomes, which is a rigged contest.

You are measuring your whole life, including the parts only you can see, against the parts of theirs that are on display. That produces a reliably bad result for everybody doing it.

And the people whose lives look settled at this age are often as unsettled as you, which is a thing you only find out by asking somebody honestly.

Ask what you actually want now

This is the part that turns the birthday from a verdict into something useful.

The question is not am I where I should be. It is: knowing what I now know, what do I want the next stretch to contain.

And the honest answers at forty or sixty are often different from the ones at twenty, in ways that are a gain rather than a loss. Fewer, closer people. Work that matters more than work that impresses. Time rather than money. Something you want to be able to do with your body in ten years, which is a reason to start now.

Pick one thing from that list and start it in the month of the birthday. Not a reinvention. One thing.

Handle the day itself deliberately

A great deal of the dread is about the event rather than the age.

So decide what you actually want, rather than what the number demands. A party if you like parties. Dinner with four people. A day away on your own. Nothing at all, which is a legitimate choice people feel oddly guilty about.

Be careful about who you let organize it, and say what you want out loud, because the standard big-birthday production is exactly wrong for a lot of people.

And notice that the weeks before are usually worse than the day. Most people find the birthday itself unremarkable and the lead-up heavy, which is the normal shape of this.

Then let it be a milestone rather than a judgment

The most common report, afterward, is that it was fine. The number stops being a threshold within a week or two and becomes just the number you are.

What tends to last is whatever stock-taking you did, which is why it is worth doing properly rather than avoiding.

When to get help

Please talk to a therapist if the stock-taking has turned into weeks of regret you cannot put down, or if a birthday has set off a serious reconsideration of your work, your relationship or your life that you are frightened of, because that is a good use of a few sessions and better than acting on it suddenly. See your doctor if your mood has been low for more than a couple of weeks, if you are not sleeping, or if you are drinking more in the lead-up, since those are treatable and not just a mood about a number. And if the age is bringing a real loss with it, such as the end of being able to have children, that is a genuine grief and deserves support rather than being dismissed as being about a birthday.
