When You Have to Keep Someone Else's Secret

Someone trusted you with something and now you are carrying it everywhere. Why it weighs so much, what to clarify with the person who told you, and where keeping it stops being the right thing to do.

BoundariesSelf-Care Visitor 884 words

Someone told you something and asked you not to repeat it. A diagnosis. An affair. Money that has gone. A plan to leave. Something they did years ago.

You said of course. You meant it. And now you are carrying something that does not belong to you, in rooms full of people who do not know, and it is heavier than you expected a piece of information to be.

Why a secret weighs

It is not only the effort of not saying it, though that is real.

You have to edit yourself continuously. In every conversation with the people it concerns, part of your attention goes to steering, and steering is tiring in a way that talking is not.

It changes the relationships around it. You now know something about a third person that they do not know you know, and that quietly alters every ordinary exchange with them.

You carry the consequences without any of the say. If it goes badly, you watched it coming. If it comes out, you were part of how long it stayed hidden.

And you usually cannot talk about the thing that is bothering you most, which removes the main way people get through anything.

The question nobody asks at the time

When someone says can you keep this to yourself, almost everyone says yes before knowing what it is. That is a decent instinct and it is also how people end up holding things they would never have agreed to.

It is allowed to ask first. Before you tell me, is this something I will have to keep from anyone in particular. That one sentence prevents most of the trouble, and it is much easier to say than people think.

And it is allowed to say no afterward, carefully. I want to support you and I do not think I can keep this from my wife. That is not a betrayal. It is honest, and it is far better said now than discovered later.

Working out what you actually agreed to

Secrets are rarely defined, and most of the strain comes from the undefined parts.

Ask what the boundary is, plainly. Who specifically am I not telling. Is there an end to this. Can I say that something is going on without saying what.

Ask what they want from you, which is a separate question. Some people want advice, some want it witnessed, and some want it held and never mentioned again. Guessing wrong makes you both uncomfortable.

And establish whether you can refer back to it. Many people find they are allowed to carry a thing and not allowed to speak about it even with the person who told them, which is the loneliest version.

Looking after yourself while you hold it

There are a few things that genuinely reduce the weight, and none of them require breaking anything.

Separate the facts from the feelings. You cannot share the facts, and you can absolutely say to someone close to you that you are carrying something difficult and would rather not explain. That is not a breach, and it ends the pretending that you are fine.

Talk to someone entirely outside it if you need to say the whole thing out loud. A therapist, a helpline, a counselor at work. This is one of the main reasons those exist, and using one is not an indulgence.

Put the worry somewhere. People holding a secret tend to run it in the background constantly. Deciding to think about it at a particular time, for a set stretch, works better than trying not to.

And notice if you have started managing the situation rather than holding the information. Arranging things so it does not come out, steering conversations, covering gaps. That is a different job, and it is far heavier than the one you agreed to.

Where keeping it stops being the right thing

Not every secret should be kept, and it is worth being clear about the line in advance rather than in a crisis.

If someone is in danger, if a child is involved, or if what you were told is still happening to someone who does not know, confidentiality is not the governing concern, and it is reasonable to tell somebody who can act.

Where that is the case, say so to the person if you safely can. I cannot keep this one, and here is what I am going to do. People sometimes handle that far better than you expect, and saying it is fairer than acting silently.

And if you are not sure, get advice from someone who deals with that kind of situation rather than deciding alone at midnight. Being unsure is not a reason to default to silence.

When it has become too much to hold

If this has you sleeping badly, if you dread seeing certain people, if you feel complicit in something you did not do, or if you have started avoiding the person who told you, that is a sign the weight has outgrown the arrangement.

Talking it through with a therapist is both allowed and confidential, which makes it one of the few places the whole thing can be said out loud. Your doctor can point you to what is available if you do not know where to start.
