When Nobody Believed You at the Time

Being doubted is a second injury with its own weight. Why disbelief does lasting damage, what it does to your own account of what happened, and how to make that account solid again.

TraumaSelf-Compassion Visitor 889 words

You told someone what happened. A parent, a teacher, a manager, a police officer, a doctor, a friend. And the response was a version of are you sure, or that does not sound like them, or you are making a lot out of nothing.

Maybe you tried twice. Maybe you never tried again. Either way, something happened to you, and then something else happened on top of it, and the second thing has its own weight.

Why not being believed does its own damage

It is tempting to treat this as a footnote to the original event. It is not. It is a separate injury with its own effects.

It takes away the thing that would have helped. Being believed is most of what makes a bad experience bearable, and being doubted removes the one repair that was available.

It teaches you not to say anything. People who were disbelieved once often stop reporting anything at all, including things that have nothing to do with it, because the cost of being doubted turned out to be higher than the cost of staying quiet.

It attacks your own account. If somebody you trusted said it did not happen that way, part of you starts checking, and that checking can run for decades.

And it is usually silent. There is often nobody who knows about the second injury at all, because the first one was never established.

The doubt you turn on yourself

This is the part people find hardest to say out loud, and it is extremely common.

You go over it looking for the exaggeration. You cannot find one and you keep looking, because somebody once implied there was one.

You notice that your memory has gaps, and you treat the gaps as evidence against yourself. Memory of a frightening event is normally patchy, and that is a feature of how fear and memory work rather than a sign that the memory is false.

You compare it to worse things and conclude you are making a fuss. Measuring your experience against somebody else's is a habit that was taught to you, usually by whoever did not believe you.

And you find yourself needing somebody to confirm it before you are allowed to feel anything about it, which keeps the whole thing in other people's hands.

Why people did not believe you

Worth knowing, because the reasons are mostly about them, and most people assume they are about themselves.

It was easier not to. Believing you would have meant acting, and acting would have cost them something. That is bleak, and it accounts for a great deal of it.

It did not fit what they thought they knew. People hold on to their picture of a person or a place, and new information gets rejected rather than absorbed.

They were frightened, or out of their depth, or protecting someone, or following a process that did not allow for what you were saying.

None of that is a judgment on whether you were telling the truth, and that is the point. Disbelief is evidence about the listener.

Making your own account solid again

You are allowed to settle this without anyone's permission, and there are things that genuinely help.

Write it down, once, as plainly as you can, including the parts you are unsure about. Marking uncertainty as uncertainty is what an honest account looks like, and seeing that on paper undercuts the idea that doubt means invention.

Notice the difference between remembering imperfectly and being wrong. Those are not the same thing, and the people who doubted you probably ran them together.

Tell one person who will receive it well, if there is anyone. One good response does a surprising amount, and many people find it works even decades later.

And give yourself the sentence you did not get. Something happened, it affected me, and I am allowed to treat it as real. Say it rather than think it. It feels strange and it does something.

If you are considering telling someone now

Sometimes people want to report it formally, or tell a family member, years after the fact. That can be worth doing, and it is worth preparing for.

Decide what you want from it before you start, because the answer shapes everything. Acknowledgment, a record, safety for somebody else, and consequences for a person are four different goals with four different routes.

Expect it to be hard regardless of the outcome, and line up support for afterward rather than only for the day itself.

And get advice from an organization that handles that kind of report rather than going in alone. They know what the process does and does not offer.

Talking to someone who will take it seriously

A therapist is trained to receive an account without auditing it, which is precisely what was missing, and this is well-worn ground rather than an unusual thing to bring.

If this has been sitting with you for years, if you find yourself unable to trust what you remember, or if talking about it brings a wave of something you cannot ride out alone, that is a reason to see someone rather than keep managing it privately. Your doctor can point you to services, many of them free. In the US you can call or text 988 at any time.
