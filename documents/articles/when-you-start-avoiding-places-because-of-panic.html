When You Start Avoiding Places Because of Panic

One bad episode somewhere, and then you stop going. How avoidance spreads without anyone deciding to let it, and how to work back outward in steps small enough to hold.

PanicGrounding Visitor 866 words

It started with one place. The store where it happened, or the highway, or the theater where you had to get out partway through.

You avoided that one, which was reasonable. Then the next one that felt similar. And at some point you noticed that the list of places you go has been getting shorter for a while, and nobody decided that, it just happened.

Why avoidance spreads on its own

Avoiding something you are afraid of works immediately. The dread drops the moment you decide not to go, and that relief is a powerful reward, delivered instantly, every single time.

What it also does is remove the chance to find out that it would have been all right. So the fear is never contradicted. It stays exactly as large as it was, and because you have now treated that place as genuinely dangerous, it gets slightly larger.

Then it generalizes. Not the store but all stores. Not that road but driving. Not that theater but anywhere with rows and a middle seat. Your mind is doing pattern matching, and the pattern widens each time.

And the escape routes become part of it. Sitting near the door, driving only the route you know, going only with one particular person. Those feel like coping, and they work, which is the problem: they keep the fear intact by quietly confirming that the place was only survivable because of the precaution.

What is actually being feared

Usually not the place. What people are avoiding is having an episode there, and specifically having one somewhere they cannot leave, or where people would see.

That is worth saying plainly, because it changes what the work is. The task is not getting comfortable with supermarkets. It is learning, in your body rather than your head, that the sensations can rise and fall without anything catastrophic happening and without you needing to flee.

Working back outward

The way back is going toward things, deliberately, in steps small enough that you can stay until the feeling comes down rather than leaving at the peak.

Make a list and put it in order, from mildly uncomfortable to the worst. Be specific: not stores, but the small market on a weekday morning, then the same place at four in the afternoon, then the large one.

Start lower than feels respectable. If standing in the parking lot is a four out of ten, start there. People who start at the top usually have one bad attempt and stop for a year.

The key is staying long enough for the curve to turn. Anxiety rises, levels, and comes down if you stay, and it is the coming-down part that teaches your body something new. Leaving at the peak teaches the opposite, so a shorter trip you complete beats a longer one you flee.

Repeat the same step until it is boring, which usually takes several goes rather than one. Then move up one.

Drop the precautions one at a time, on purpose, once the step itself is manageable. Sit further from the door. Go without the phone in your hand. Go without the person who always comes. These matter more than people expect, because while they are in place the step has not quite been done.

What to do when it rises while you are there

Expect it, rather than hoping it will not happen. The plan is not to feel calm. It is to stay.

Let the sensations be there without fighting them. Pushing against a racing heart adds alarm on top of alarm. Noticing it, and continuing with what you came to do, does not.

Slow the out-breath, longer than the in-breath, and keep your eyes on the room rather than on your chest. Inward attention feeds it.

And do not leave to prove you can leave. If you must step outside, come back in before you go home, so the trip ends with staying rather than with escaping.

What progress actually looks like

Not the absence of fear. The ability to go anyway, with the fear present and getting smaller over months.

Expect it to be uneven. A bad day after a good week is ordinary and it is not a reset. The useful measure is the range of places you go, not how you felt on a particular afternoon.

And keep going to the places you have reclaimed. Avoidance regrows quietly if a place drops off the list for a few months.

When to get help with it

This is one of the most treatable things people bring to a therapist, and the approach is specific, structured and usually short. If your world has narrowed, if you are turning down work or arranging your week around what you cannot do, or if trying this alone has stalled, a therapist who works with panic will get you much further and faster than doing it unaided.

It is also worth a conversation with a doctor, partly to rule out the ordinary physical causes of the same sensations, and partly because there are treatments that make the first steps possible for people who are stuck. In the US you can call or text 988 at any time.

If you are in crisis, call or text 988 (Suicide & Crisis Lifeline, US) or your local emergency number. Theraglee is a wellbeing tool, not a substitute for professional care.
