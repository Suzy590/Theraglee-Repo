Telling Your Manager You Are Struggling

You are barely holding the week together and nobody at work knows. What to say, how much to say, and how to ask for something specific rather than sympathy.

BurnoutCommunication Visitor 907 words

You have been getting through the days on adrenaline and lists. Something has slipped. You are lying awake at three, and on Monday there is a meeting where you will be asked how it is all going.

And the honest answer is not good, which you have no intention of saying, because then it is out there and you cannot take it back.

What usually stops people

Three fears, and all of them are understandable.

That it will count against you: that you will be seen as unreliable, passed over, first on the list in a restructure.

That it will become a performance matter rather than a support one, with meetings and forms.

And that saying it out loud makes it real. While nobody knows, you can keep believing you are managing.

Against those, weigh what silence costs. A manager who does not know thinks you are slow or careless rather than overloaded, and they cannot adjust anything they have not been told about.

Decide what you want before you decide what to say

This is the part people skip, and it changes the whole conversation.

Ask yourself what would actually help in the next month. Fewer projects. A deadline moved. A day working from home. Not being on call. Someone taking one task. A week of leave. A phased return. Clear priorities so you can stop trying to do everything.

Pick one or two specific things. A conversation where you ask for something concrete goes somewhere; a conversation where you describe how hard everything is usually ends in sympathy and no change.

How much to tell them

You decide this, not them, and you can say a great deal less than the whole truth.

The minimum version is about capacity: I am overloaded and something has to give, here is what I suggest. No diagnosis, no personal detail.

The middle version adds a reason without specifics: I am dealing with something health-related at the moment, I am getting support for it, and I need a couple of adjustments for the next few weeks.

The fuller version is for a manager you trust: naming what is actually going on. That is a reasonable choice with the right person, and it is not required to get help.

Say it plainly, keep it short, and do not narrate your whole year.

How to open it

Ask for the meeting rather than raising it in passing: can we have twenty minutes, I want to talk about my workload.

Then lead with the facts and the ask rather than the feelings: I am carrying X, Y and Z, it is more than the hours allow, and the quality is starting to suffer. I would like to hand Y over or push it to next quarter. Which would you prefer?

Have it written down, and leave the list with them. Managers forget conversations and remember documents.

And say what you are still doing well. This is not a resignation; it is a request to keep working at a sustainable level.

Know what exists before you go in

Most workplaces have more than people realize, and almost nobody asks.

Occupational health, which is there to advise on adjustments rather than to assess you. Employee assistance programs, usually with free counseling attached. Flexible working requests. Sick leave, phased returns, unpaid leave. A human resources team who will know the policies even when your manager does not.

If what is going on is health-related, reasonable adjustments may be something you are entitled to rather than a favor: changed hours, a lighter load, fewer meetings, more notice of changes.

A union or a staff association, if you have one, will tell you exactly where you stand before you say anything.

Keep a quiet record

Not because you expect trouble, but because it protects everybody.

After the conversation, send a short email: thanks for the chat, as agreed I am handing over Y and the deadline for Z moves to the fifteenth. Two lines.

That turns a sympathetic conversation into an actual change, and it means nobody is relying on their memory in three months.

If the answer is no

Sometimes it is. Then you know something important, and you have options.

Ask what they suggest instead, in writing. Escalate to human resources or occupational health if it is a health matter. Go to your union. Work your contracted hours and let the queue be visible rather than absorbing it silently.

And if the answer is consistently no and the load is not survivable, start looking. That is not failure; it is information about the job rather than about you.

When to get help

Please talk to a doctor if you are not sleeping, getting ill repeatedly, waking at three, dreading Monday from Friday onward, or drinking more to switch off. Those are the standard warning lights, and arriving with them early is far better than arriving when you cannot work at all.

A doctor can also sign you off if that is what is needed, which is sometimes the only thing that stops a spiral, and a therapist is worth it if you find it almost impossible to put work down or to say no.

And if the thought of going in has become dark rather than simply heavy, please tell a doctor or someone you trust today rather than carrying it to Monday. In the US you can call or text 988 at any time.

If you are in crisis, call or text 988 (Suicide & Crisis Lifeline, US) or your local emergency number. Theraglee is a wellbeing tool, not a substitute for professional care.
