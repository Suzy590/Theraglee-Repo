The First Weeks Back at Work After Something Hard

Someone died, or you were ill, or your life came apart. Now you are back at your desk and everyone is being normal. How to get through the return without pretending you are fine.

WorkGrief Visitor 916 words

You buried your mother eleven days ago. Or you came out of hospital, or your marriage ended, or something happened that split your life in two. And this morning you put on work clothes and went back, and by ten o'clock someone was asking you about a spreadsheet.

The return to work after something serious is strange in a way nobody prepares you for. The world carried on. Your inbox filled up with things that do not matter. And you are expected, more or less, to be the person you were before.

Expect to be much less capable than usual

Grief, illness, and shock all take a large amount of the machinery you use for work. Concentration, short-term memory, decision-making, and patience are all reduced, often dramatically, and often without you noticing until you have read the same email four times.

This is temporary and it is not a character failure. Plan for it rather than being surprised by it: write everything down, do not trust your memory, check your own work, and do the hardest thing early in the day if you can.

Go back gradually if there is any way to

Half days, three days a week, two weeks of reduced responsibility. If your workplace allows it, take it, even if you feel able to go back fully. Almost everyone who returns at full speed hits a wall in week two or three.

If it cannot be gradual, at least keep the first weeks narrow: fewer meetings, no new projects, nothing you volunteer for.

Decide in advance what you will say

The hardest part for many people is the thirty conversations. Someone catches you at the coffee machine and says how are you, and you have to decide in that second how much to give.

Have two answers ready. A short one for people you barely know: thank you for asking, it has been a hard few weeks, I am glad to be back. And a fuller one for the two or three people you actually want to tell. Practice the short one until it comes out without effort, because it is the one you will use most.

You are allowed to say I would rather not talk about it here, and to say it pleasantly and repeatedly.

Let someone go first for you

If you can, ask one person, your manager or a colleague you trust, to tell people what happened and what you want. That saves you from delivering the news thirty times. Many people find this the single most helpful thing anyone did.

Tell them what you need too, specifically: please do not ask me how I am every morning. Please do put things in writing. Please do carry on talking about normal things around me.

Expect it to hit at unpredictable times

You will be fine through a difficult meeting and then be undone by a song in the elevator, or by a colleague mentioning their own mother. The wave arrives without warning and often at the most inconvenient moment.

Have a plan for it: where you can go for ten minutes, how you leave a room without explaining, what you say if someone sees. Something as simple as I need a minute, back shortly is enough. Nobody needs a reason.

Let work be a relief when it is

Many people feel guilty when work turns out to help, when a few hours of ordinary problem-solving gives them a break from the thing. That is not disloyalty or denial. Being absorbed in something is genuinely restorative, and the grief will be there when you finish.

Protect the rest of the day

The return uses more energy than the work itself. Do less in the evenings than you normally would, and expect to be exhausted in a way the hours do not explain. This is the part people underestimate: they manage the working day and then have nothing left for the people at home.

Know your rights, roughly

Bereavement leave, sick leave, and flexible arrangements vary a great deal, and many people take much less than they are entitled to because they never asked. It is worth one conversation with your manager or human resources about what is actually available, including a phased return, which is more common than most people think.

Watch the three-month mark

The support tends to fade around the time it gets harder. In the first weeks people are kind and ask after you. By month three they have moved on, and you may be at your lowest, because the shock has worn off and the permanence has arrived. Expect that shape rather than reading it as evidence that you are failing at recovery.

When to get more help

Please talk to your doctor or a therapist if, after a couple of months, you cannot function at work, if you are not sleeping, if you are drinking more to get through, if you feel nothing at all, or if the thought of going in produces dread every single day. Many workplaces have a confidential employee assistance program that you can use without telling anyone, and a bereavement or trauma counselor can help you carry this while you work. And if you ever find yourself hopeless or thinking of harming yourself, please reach out that day. In the US you can call or text 988 at any time. You are doing something difficult with far less than your usual capacity. That deserves patience, most of all from you.

If you are in crisis, call or text 988 (Suicide & Crisis Lifeline, US) or your local emergency number. Theraglee is a wellbeing tool, not a substitute for professional care.
