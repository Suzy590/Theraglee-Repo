The Week After You Lose Your Job

However it happened, the first week is its own problem. What to do in the first few days, what to leave alone, and why this hits harder than the money explains.

LossLife Transitions Visitor 1074 words

It is Tuesday morning and there is nowhere to be. You have told a few people and not others. You are oddly calm, and then you are not, usually somewhere around four in the afternoon.

Losing a job is treated as a financial event. It is that, and it is also a loss of structure, status, daily company and a large part of how you answer the question of who you are. All four arrive at once.

Why it hits harder than the arithmetic

The money is often the smallest part of the first week.

What goes with a job is the shape of your day, the people you spoke to without arranging it, the sense of being useful, and the plain fact of being chosen. Losing those at the same time is why people feel shaky even when they have savings, and even when they disliked the job.

If it was a dismissal or a restructuring where you were picked and others were not, there is usually shame on top, and shame makes people hide exactly when they most need other people.

The first week: do these, and little else

Protect the shape of your days. Get up at your usual time, get dressed, go outside once. That sounds trivial and it is the single thing that most reliably keeps the first two weeks from unravelling.

Handle only the urgent paperwork. File for unemployment benefits or whatever applies where you live, because those have waiting periods and the clock starts when you file. Find out exactly what you are owed: notice, unused leave, final pay, any severance.

Do not decide anything large. Not moving, not a career change, not a confrontation. Decisions made in the first week are made by somebody who has just been winded.

And tell two or three people properly. Not a broadcast, two or three. The urge to wait until you have a plan is strong and it costs you the support you need to make one.

Get the facts on paper

Vagueness is what produces the three-in-the-morning version of this, so spend an hour on the numbers.

What is coming in: final pay, any severance, benefits you are entitled to, anything from a partner. What is going out that cannot change. How many months that gives you, roughly.

Most people find the runway is longer than the panic implied. Some find it is short, which is unwelcome and far better known than guessed, because short runways have specific actions attached: calling the lender, asking about hardship arrangements, cutting the big recurring costs first.

Be careful what you do with the time

Two opposite errors dominate the first weeks.

Filling every hour with frantic applications, usually badly targeted, which burns you out by week three and produces little. Twenty tailored applications will beat two hundred generic ones, and you cannot write twenty good ones in a week.

Or disappearing: staying in, sleeping late, losing the days. That feels like rest and behaves like a slide, because the structure is what holds your mood up.

The version that works is boring: a few hours of job work a day, in a fixed slot, and the rest of the day deliberately used for something else, including something physical and something with people in it.

What to say to people

You will be asked constantly, so decide one sentence and reuse it. I was made redundant in a restructure and I am looking for something in X. Or: my role was cut, and I am taking a few weeks to work out what is next.

Short, neutral, no apology, no case for the defense. You do not owe anyone the full account, including family.

If you were dismissed in a way you believe was unfair, keep the details for the people who can actually help: a lawyer, a union, a labor board. Explaining it repeatedly to acquaintances tends to leave you more upset and no further forward.

Expect the mood to move around

The first week is often numb, or even oddly light. The hard part frequently arrives in weeks two to four, when the novelty has gone and the first rejections land.

Rejections are the part people are least prepared for, because job hunting has a terrible ratio and each no arrives personally addressed. It helps to count applications rather than outcomes, and to decide in advance how many nos a week is simply the cost of looking.

And notice what the thoughts do. Unemployment is extremely good at producing sentences like I am unemployable, nobody will take me at my age, I have wasted ten years. Those are mood talking, and they get louder in the afternoon and quieter after a walk.

Use the one advantage you have

For most people this is the first unstructured stretch in years, and it is worth not spending all of it on applications.

Three things repay the time: one honest conversation with someone in a field you are curious about, one skill or certificate that is actually required by the jobs you want, and a serious look at whether the work you were doing is the work you want to go back to.

Not as a reinvention project. Just enough that the next job is a choice rather than the first thing that said yes.

Keep the people, deliberately

The job provided your social contact, and losing it quietly removes most of it.

So put two or three fixed things in the week that involve other people: a standing coffee, a class, a volunteer shift, a sport. Volunteering in particular does double duty, because it provides company, structure and something true to say about what you have been doing.

When to get help

If you are not sleeping, drinking more, unable to get out of the house, or finding that the low mood has not lifted at all after a few weeks, please take it to a doctor or a therapist. Job loss is one of the more common reasons people end up in treatment, and that is not a sign of weakness; it is one of the larger losses adults face.

Free employment services, nonprofit debt advice and benefits advisers all exist and are badly underused. And if things get dark enough that you are thinking about not being here, please tell a doctor or someone you trust that day. In the US you can call or text 988 at any time.

If you are in crisis, call or text 988 (Suicide & Crisis Lifeline, US) or your local emergency number. Theraglee is a wellbeing tool, not a substitute for professional care.
