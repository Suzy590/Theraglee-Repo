The First Ten Minutes of Your Day

You reach for the phone before your eyes are open and arrive at breakfast already behind. A small, practical change to the start of the day, and why it is worth the effort.

HabitsMindfulness Visitor 877 words

The alarm goes off, which is also your phone, so the first thing in your hand is a screen. Within ninety seconds you have seen a headline, two work emails and something irritating from somebody you went to school with.

You have not got out of bed yet and the day already has a tone.

Why the first few minutes carry weight

Whatever you look at first sets the frame for everything after it, mostly because waking up leaves you unusually open.

For a short while after waking, your attention has not settled and your mood has not formed. Something sharp — a demanding message, bad news, a notification about money — lands with more force than it would at four in the afternoon, and the alertness it produces tends to last.

And it starts the day in a reactive position: responding to other people's requests before you have had a single thought of your own.

That is not a reason to be mystical about mornings. It is a reason to be slightly deliberate about ten minutes.

Get the phone out of the first stretch

The whole change, in practice, is this: do not look at the phone until you have been up for a bit.

Buy a cheap alarm clock and charge the phone in another room, which is the version that actually works. Failing that, put it across the room, in a drawer, face down with notifications off overnight.

If you need it reachable for emergencies, keep it on do not disturb with a couple of people allowed through. That covers the genuine case and removes the other hundred things.

And decide what counts as the end of the protected stretch: after you are dressed, after the first drink, after the dog. A line, so you are not negotiating at six in the morning.

Put something in the gap

Removing the phone leaves a space, and an empty space tends to get filled by the phone again. So have something ready.

Water, then whatever hot drink you like, made slowly. Standing outside for two minutes, which does more for waking up than anything on a screen. Opening the curtains and letting daylight hit your eyes. Stretching badly for a minute. Making the bed. Feeding an animal. Writing three lines in a notebook.

None of this needs to be a routine with a name. One or two things, in the same order, is plenty.

The point is that the first thing you do is something you chose.

Decide the one thing before the world asks

If you want a single addition beyond the physical, make it this: before you open anything, decide what matters today.

One line, out loud or on paper: today I want to get the report done, or today I want to be patient with the children, or today I only have to get through it.

That takes twenty seconds and it changes what the emails feel like, because you arrive at them with a priority rather than receiving one.

Expect the first week to be uncomfortable

If you have started your days on a screen for years, the quiet will feel strange, and the urge to check is strongest in exactly the moments you have just freed up.

Notice the urge, let it be there, and go and do the next small thing. It fades over a week or two, and the first thing most people report is not peacefulness but a feeling of having more time, which is simply the time the scrolling was taking.

And expect to fail some mornings. The aim is most days, not every day; one bad morning does not require a fresh start on the first of the month.

What about the news

The news will still be there at nine, and so will the emails.

For most people, moving both to after breakfast costs nothing real and removes a daily spike of alarm about things they cannot act on before work. If your job genuinely requires an early check, do that one thing on purpose, with a time limit, rather than drifting through everything else on the way.

Keep the end of the day in mind too

The morning is downstream of the night before.

Leaving the phone out of the bedroom fixes both ends at once, which is why it is the single change worth the awkwardness. It also tends to improve sleep, because the last ten minutes of a day set a tone in much the same way the first ten do.

And set out whatever the morning needs — clothes, bag, the kettle filled — so the first stretch has as few decisions in it as possible.

When mornings are harder than this

Please talk to a doctor if getting up has become genuinely difficult rather than unpleasant: if you are exhausted however long you sleep, if the first hours are flattened by dread, or if your mood is low and has been for more than a couple of weeks.

Those are common and treatable, and no amount of rearranging a morning routine substitutes for having them looked at. A therapist can also help with the specific pattern of waking up already braced, which often has something behind it worth knowing about.
