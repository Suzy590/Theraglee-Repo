Nightmares That Keep Coming Back

The same dream, or the same kind of dream, several nights a week. Why repeated nightmares happen, what helps at three in the morning, and what treatment exists.

TraumaSleep Visitor 912 words

You wake at three with your heart going, already sitting up, and for a few seconds you do not know which parts of it were real.

Then the worse part: the knowledge that this will probably happen again tomorrow, and the slow dread that starts building around bedtime the next evening.

Repeated nightmares are their own problem, separate from whatever started them. They cost you sleep, they make the bedroom feel unsafe, and they are exhausting in a way that is hard to explain to anyone who sleeps through the night.

Why the same dream comes back

Dreaming seems to be part of how the mind processes experience. When something has been frightening or overwhelming, the processing can get stuck in a loop: instead of working the material through and settling, the same scene returns, with the same ending, over and over.

Sometimes it is a replay of something that happened. Often it is not literal at all: being chased, trapped, unable to move or speak, losing someone, arriving too late. The emotion is the accurate part; the images may be nothing like the event.

Several other things make nightmares more frequent: alcohol, especially in the hours before bed, some medications, withdrawal from others, fever, broken sleep, and long stretches of high stress. Those are worth knowing because some are adjustable.

What to do when you wake

Get fully awake before you try to go back to sleep. Lying in the dark half-in-it is where the fear gets a second wind.

Sit up. Put a light on. Put your feet on the floor and feel it. Name where you are and the date out loud. Have a drink of water. Cold on the hands or face helps if your body is still going.

Then give your mind something ordinary and sensory rather than the dream: name five things you can see, or do a slow body scan from the feet up. You are not trying to analyze anything at three in the morning.

If you are not back to sleep after twenty minutes or so, get up and go to another room with low light and something dull to read, and come back when you are sleepy. Lying there losing the fight teaches your body that bed is a place of struggle.

Make the room less like the dream

Small changes to the setting help more than they sound like they would.

A low light you can reach without getting up. A door left as you prefer it. A fan or something quiet in the background if silence makes you listen for things. Something physical by the bed to hold.

And avoid the news or anything frightening in the last hour before sleep. Not because it causes nightmares exactly, but because what you take to bed with you has some say in the material.

Rewriting the ending, in daylight

There is a specific approach for repeated nightmares that is simple enough to describe and worth knowing about: you change the dream on purpose, while awake.

In the daytime, write the nightmare down in the present tense, but stop before the worst part. Then write a different continuation, one you choose: you get out, someone arrives, you turn and it is nothing, you can suddenly speak. It does not have to be realistic and it does not have to be brave.

Then read your new version through a few times before bed, picturing it as vividly as you can, for a couple of weeks.

This is the core of a treatment used for exactly this problem, and many people find the dream changes or loses its grip. It works best with a therapist guiding it, especially if the nightmare comes from something traumatic, because writing it down can bring more than you expected.

Be careful with the two common shortcuts

Alcohol is the most popular remedy for this and one of the worst. It gets people to sleep and then fragments the second half of the night, which is exactly when nightmares arrive.

Staying up until you collapse is the other one. It works for a while and then stops, and the resulting sleep deprivation makes everything about this harder, including how frightening the dream feels.

If you are doing either, that is understandable rather than shameful. It is also worth saying to a doctor, because there are better options.

What to tell the people around you

If you share a bed, tell them what helps. Some people want to be woken, some want to be left; some want to be told where they are, some just want the light on.

Agreeing that in advance saves a frightened, confusing few minutes in the middle of the night for both of you.

When to get help with it

Nightmares two or more nights a week, nightmares that have you dreading bedtime, or nightmares that follow something frightening in your life are worth treating, and they respond well. Treatment is usually short and practical; there are specific therapies for nightmares and for trauma, and a doctor can also look at medications, alcohol and anything physical that might be feeding it.

Please talk to a doctor or a therapist, especially if you are also having flashbacks or feeling numb much of the time, or if the exhaustion has got to the point where you are not sure how much longer you can keep going. In the US you can call or text 988 at any time.

If you are in crisis, call or text 988 (Suicide & Crisis Lifeline, US) or your local emergency number. Theraglee is a wellbeing tool, not a substitute for professional care.
