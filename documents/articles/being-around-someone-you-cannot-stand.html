Being Around Someone You Cannot Stand

A relative, a colleague, the other parent at pickup. Why liking them is not the goal, the three topics and the exit line to prepare, and how to keep your evening afterward.

BoundariesAnger Visitor 820 words

There is a person you have to be around. A colleague, a relative by marriage, somebody on a committee, the other parent at pickup. You are not going to be friends. You cannot avoid them. And a Tuesday with them in it is measurably worse than a Tuesday without.

This is an ordinary problem that gets very little practical attention, because the usual advice is either set a boundary or try to see the best in them, and neither helps much at a birthday party you cannot leave.

Decide what you are actually aiming for

Most of the frustration comes from an unclear goal. You are not trying to like them, change them, or be understood by them. Those are not available.

What you want is a dull interaction that costs you as little as possible, and an evening afterward that is not ruined.

That is a low bar on purpose. Aiming at it changes your behavior usefully, because it means a conversation where nothing happened was a success rather than a disappointment.

Have your three topics and your exit

Specifics beat resolve. Walk in with a plan and you will not be improvising while irritated.

Three safe topics, chosen in advance. The weather, a sports result, a neutral thing you both know about, something about the food. Boring is the point.

One line to get out. I am going to get a drink. I promised I would help in the kitchen. I need to catch up with somebody before they go.

And a time limit. Ten minutes, then you move. Knowing the end is near makes the middle much easier to be civil through.

Do not take the bait, in either direction

If this person provokes you, the provocation usually relies on you engaging, which is why not engaging works where arguing does not.

So keep your answers short and mild. That is one way to look at it. Maybe so. I will have to think about that. Then change the subject or move away.

Do not correct them, do not provide evidence, do not explain yourself. Being right in that conversation gets you nothing, and the attempt is what turns a dull five minutes into a bad evening.

And watch the other bait, which is more subtle. Some people draw you into complaining about them with somebody else, and an hour of that leaves you carrying them around all night.

Decide in advance which things you will say something about

Civility is not the same as accepting anything. The useful thing is to know your line before you are standing on it.

Most of what they do is not worth addressing. Rudeness about something that matters, or something said to a child or in front of one, might be.

Have one sentence ready, said flatly and without a speech. I would rather you did not say that. Let us not do this here. That is not something I am going to discuss.

Then leave it there. One flat sentence is far more effective than an argument, and it does not require them to agree.

Manage yourself rather than them

The part you control is your own state, and that does most of the work.

Do not go in tired or hungry, which sounds trivial and is the single biggest factor in how these go. Have an ally in the room if there is one. Stand near the people you like. Step outside for five minutes partway through.

And plan the hour after. A walk, a shower, a particular program, somebody to debrief with briefly. Then stop. A ten-minute vent is useful and an hour of it hands them your whole evening.

Let go of being understood by them

This is the quiet part, and it is where most of the lasting irritation lives. Somewhere in you is the hope that they will finally see it, admit it, or behave differently.

That hope is what keeps the sting fresh. People who make peace with these relationships usually describe a moment of deciding that this person is simply going to be like this, and that nothing is owed to them beyond politeness.

It is a small grief, oddly, and it comes with a good deal of relief.

When to get help

Please talk to a therapist if a particular person is affecting your sleep, your mood or your week, especially if the relationship goes back a long way, since the ones that get under our skin usually connect to something older. Talk to someone too if what you are dealing with is more than difficulty, such as bullying at work, or behavior that frightens you, because that needs a different kind of support than coping strategies. And see your doctor if dread of events where this person will be has started shrinking what you do, or if you are carrying a level of anger that is hard to put down.

If you are in crisis, call or text 988 (Suicide & Crisis Lifeline, US) or your local emergency number. Theraglee is a wellbeing tool, not a substitute for professional care.
