When the News Is Too Much

You want to be informed and you end up frightened, flat and unable to look away. Why the feed works on you that way, how much following is enough, and what to do with the worry it leaves.

WorryBoundaries Visitor 877 words

You pick up the phone to check what happened and surface forty minutes later knowing a great deal about something awful and nothing useful about what to do with it. The feeling afterward is specific: wound up and hopeless at once, braced for something, and strangely tired.

Then there is the guilt that arrives if you consider looking away. People are suffering, so watching feels like the least you can do, and switching off feels like a kind of abandonment.

Why it works on you this way

A news feed is not a newspaper that ends. It refreshes, and it is assembled to hold attention, which means the most alarming version of a thing reaches you first and most often.

Your alarm system cannot tell distance. It responds to a frightening image or a frightening headline roughly as though the danger were in your street, which is why your body gets ready for something you cannot act on. The readiness has nowhere to go, so it stays as tension, poor sleep and a short fuse.

Repetition makes it worse rather than better. Reading about the same event twenty times does not add twenty times the understanding, but it does keep the alarm running all day.

And the feeling that you have to keep watching is part of how the thing is built. Your attention is the product. Being unable to stop is not a flaw in your character.

Being informed and following every hour are not the same thing

It is worth asking what being informed actually requires. For almost everybody it means knowing roughly what is happening, well enough to vote, to talk to people, to give money or time where you choose to. It does not require a live feed.

Most people can get that from a longer read once a day, or even a couple of times a week, and will understand events better than someone who has watched them unfold minute by minute, because the summaries are written once the facts have settled.

Make it a thing you do, not a thing that happens

The difference between following the news and being soaked in it is mostly structure.

Pick a time, and a form. Twenty minutes in the late afternoon, from one or two sources you respect, rather than whenever your hand reaches for the phone.

Keep it out of the first and last half hour of your day. News first thing sets the whole day's weather. News last thing takes your sleep.

Read rather than watch, where you can. Text moves at your pace and does not come with footage that stays in your mind for weeks.

Take the apps off your home screen, or log out, so there is a step between the urge and the feed.

Mute the repeats. Most of what arrives after the first hour is reaction rather than news.

Give the worry somewhere to go

Alarm with no outlet turns into dread. One small action a week changes the feeling out of proportion to its size, because it moves you from watching to doing.

Give money, once and by standing order so you are not deciding each time. Write to a representative. Volunteer for something local, even something unrelated to the headlines. Help one actual person near you.

None of this fixes the thing on the screen. It does rebuild the sense that you are a person who acts, which is the part that constant watching takes away.

Talk about it with people rather than at them

Say the frightening thing out loud to someone you trust, rather than carrying it around or arguing with strangers online. Arguing with strangers reliably leaves you feeling worse and convinces no one. A conversation with a friend about how all of this is landing on you both does the opposite.

Watch for the signs it has gone too far

Pay attention if you are losing sleep to it, checking in the night, feeling unable to enjoy ordinary things, snapping at people, feeling hopeless about the future, or if your children are picking up your dread. Those are signs to narrow the intake sharply for a couple of weeks, not signs that you need to understand the situation better.

Caring is not measured in hours of exposure

The belief underneath a lot of this is that watching is a form of solidarity, and that looking away is a betrayal. It is worth saying plainly that your attention does not help anyone at a distance, and that an exhausted, frightened person is less use to the people around them than a rested one. Staying able to act is the part that matters.

When to get help

If the news has you unable to sleep, constantly on edge, or hopeless in a way that has lasted more than a couple of weeks, please talk to your doctor or a therapist, who can help you tell ordinary distress from anxiety or low mood that needs treatment. This is a common reason people seek help now, and nobody will think it is a small thing to bring. If any of it has left you feeling you cannot go on, please reach out today. In the US you can call or text 988 at any time.
