Talking to Your Parents About Their Money

Unopened letters and no idea what their finances look like. How to open it without sounding like you are taking over, what to ask instead of balances, and when it has become urgent.

MoneyCaregiving Visitor 861 words

You have noticed the unopened letters. Or the renewal that got paid twice. Or they mentioned, lightly, that somebody called about an investment. And you have no idea what their finances actually look like, because you have never asked and they have never said.

It is one of the hardest conversations in a family, and the reason it gets postponed is simple: it touches money, independence, and the fact that your parents are getting older, all at once.

Know why you are asking

Being clear with yourself first makes the conversation much easier, because the suspicion people have is that you are after the money or taking over.

The honest reasons are usually: so nobody has to guess in a crisis, so bills do not go unpaid, so somebody can help if they are scammed, and so their wishes get followed rather than improvised.

Say that out loud when you start. I am not trying to take anything over. I want to know enough that if something happened, I could help the way you would want.

Start with the smallest possible ask

The whole-picture conversation almost always fails on the first attempt. Something narrower works.

So ask for one thing. Where do you keep the important papers. Who is your doctor. Is there somebody who does your taxes. If you were in the hospital for a week, who would need to be called.

Then stop. Having asked one thing successfully is the whole goal of the first conversation, and it makes the second one far easier.

Pick a moment that is not a crisis and not a celebration. A quiet weekday, a car journey, a walk. Not a birthday, and not the day after a fall.

Use somebody else's situation as the door

The least threatening opening is almost never about them.

I have been sorting out my own will and it made me realize I have no idea about yours. My friend's father went into the hospital and nobody could find anything. I am doing this for myself and thought I would ask what you did.

That makes the subject a shared practical matter rather than an assessment of their competence, which is what they are braced for.

Ask about the system, not the balances

You almost never need to know how much they have. You need to know how it works.

Where the papers are. Which bank. Whether bills are on automatic payment. Whether anyone else can sign anything. Whether there is a will, where it is, and who the attorney or executor is. Who advises them, if anybody.

Framed that way it is a logistics conversation, and most people answer logistics questions willingly where they would refuse a question about amounts.

Respect the no, and leave the door open

If the answer is no, take it. Pressing turns a postponement into a refusal, and it can poison the subject for years.

So say the thing that keeps it alive. That is fine. Can I ask you again in a few months. In the meantime, is there one thing you would want me to know.

And know the difference between a parent who does not want to discuss it and a parent who cannot manage it. The first is their right. The second needs a different approach, and probably a doctor.

Watch for the signs that this has become urgent

Most of this can go at their pace. Some things cannot.

Unopened mail piling up. Services cut off for non-payment. Large or odd withdrawals. A new friend, tradesman or caller who has become very involved. Confusion about money they used to handle easily. Shame or secrecy that is new.

Financial exploitation of older people is common and is more often somebody known to them than a stranger. Be careful about saying so out loud too fast, since the usual response is defensiveness, and get advice before you confront anyone.

Mind the siblings

A great deal of the damage in this area is done between brothers and sisters rather than with the parent.

So tell them before you talk to your parent, not after. Agree who asks. Write down what you learn and share it. Avoid becoming the one who knows everything, which breeds suspicion however well you behave.

And keep your parent's wishes at the center rather than the family's preferences. The question is what they want, not what everybody would find convenient.

When to get help

Please encourage them toward their own professional rather than relying on family: an attorney for a will and a power of attorney, and an accountant or a financial adviser they choose themselves, which protects both of them and you. If you suspect a scam or exploitation, contact adult protective services or your bank's fraud team for advice before confronting anybody, since both deal with this constantly. See your parent's doctor if the money trouble comes with confusion, forgetting or a change in judgment, because that is a medical question first. And talk to a therapist yourself if this has become a source of dread or family conflict, since the role reversal underneath it is a real grief and worth somewhere to put.

If you are in crisis, call or text 988 (Suicide & Crisis Lifeline, US) or your local emergency number. Theraglee is a wellbeing tool, not a substitute for professional care.
