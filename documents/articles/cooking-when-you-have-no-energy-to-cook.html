Cooking When You Have No Energy to Cook

On a flat or exhausted day, cooking can be the thing that does not happen. A lower bar, a shorter list, and a way to eat that does not require you to be at your best.

FoodSelf-Care Visitor 897 words

It is eight in the evening. There is food in the house. You have been standing in the kitchen doorway for a while, and the gap between what is in the fridge and anything you could call a meal feels unbridgeable.

So you have toast, or you have nothing, and then you feel bad about that too.

This is one of the most common and least discussed parts of a hard week. Cooking is not just effort; it is a chain of about fifteen small decisions and actions, and on a depleted day a chain is exactly the thing you cannot hold.

What is actually hard about it

It helps to see the parts. Deciding what to eat. Checking what is there. Working out what is missing. Standing up. Chopping. Watching something. Timing two things. Washing up afterward.

Each one is minor. Together they are a project, and the deciding is often the heaviest part, not the cooking.

Which tells you where to aim. Most of what follows is about removing decisions rather than finding easier recipes.

Lower the bar to eating, not to cooking

The first move is to separate two goals that get tangled. One is to eat something. The other is to make a meal.

On a bad day, pick the first. Cereal counts. Toast with cheese counts. A tin of soup counts. Fruit and a handful of nuts counts. Food from a shop that someone else assembled counts.

There is no version of this where not eating is the better outcome. Getting something into you is the whole job, and it is worth doing badly.

Have three no-decision meals

Decide now, while you have the capacity, what you will eat on the days you cannot decide. Three is enough.

Pick things with four ingredients or fewer, all of which keep. Beans on toast. Pasta with butter and whatever cheese is in the fridge. Eggs and bread. Rice from a packet with a tin of something. Soup and a hunk of bread.

Write the three on a piece of paper and put it inside a cupboard door. The list is not for inspiration; it is so that on a flat evening you do not have to produce an idea from nothing. You just read it.

Then keep those specific ingredients in, permanently, as a kind of insurance. A cupboard with the makings of your three meals is worth more on a bad week than a fridge full of ambitious vegetables.

Make the easy version easy to reach

Small physical arrangements help more than resolve.

Keep the tin opener somewhere obvious. Buy the pre-chopped onions or the frozen vegetables without apology. Have one pan you like using. Fill the kettle after you use it. Keep a bowl and a fork clean rather than in the pile.

Every one of those removes one step from the chain, and on the days you have four steps in you, that is the difference between eating and not.

Cook once, eat twice

If you have one good evening in the week, use it to make tomorrow easier rather than to make tonight special.

Double whatever you are cooking and put half aside. Boil extra rice. Roast a tray of something that can sit in the fridge. The point is to put a meal into the future, where your tired self will find it already decided.

A portion in the freezer is one of the kindest things you can do for someone, including yourself.

Mind the washing-up trap

Plenty of people cannot start cooking because the sink is already full, and the full sink is a bigger obstacle than the meal.

Two options. Wash only what you need for this one meal and leave the rest; one pan and one plate is a small enough job to do. Or use something disposable for a few days and let that be fine.

Neither is a failure of standards. It is choosing eating over tidiness on a day when you only get one of them.

Eating alone, and the other part of this

For a lot of people the flatness is not really about the cooking. It is about making a meal for one person when it feels like it is not worth the trouble.

If that is the shape of it, change the setting rather than the recipe. Eat with something you like on in the background. Put it on a real plate and sit somewhere other than the kitchen counter. Eat on the phone with someone once a week. Keep one thing in the house that you genuinely look forward to.

You are allowed to make the experience nicer without making the food harder.

When it has gone on a while

Not being able to feed yourself is often one of the first signs that you are running very low, and it tends to travel with other things: sleeping badly, not seeing anyone, everything feeling like too much.

If you have been living on toast for weeks, if you are skipping meals without noticing, if food has become something you are frightened of or fighting with, or if you have lost interest in eating altogether, that is worth saying to a doctor or a therapist. It is a concrete, practical thing to bring up, and it usually says something useful about how heavy the rest of your load has become.
