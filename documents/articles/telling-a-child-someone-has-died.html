Telling a Child Someone Has Died

You have to say it and you do not know how. The words that work, the ones to avoid, and what children of different ages tend to need afterward.

LossParenting Visitor 965 words

You are standing in the hall working out how to walk into the room. Somebody has died, the children do not know yet, and you are the one who has to say it.

There is no version of this that does not hurt. There is a version that gives them something solid to stand on, and that is what you are aiming for.

Do it soon, and do it yourself

Children almost always know something is wrong before they are told. They hear phone calls, they see faces, and in the gap they usually invent something worse or decide it is their fault.

So tell them early, in a quiet place, with the television off, from somebody they trust rather than from a relative they barely know.

If you can, have one other adult there who can take over if you cannot speak. And take two minutes beforehand to steady yourself: not to hide your feelings, but so you can get the sentence out.

Use the plain words

This is the part adults most want to avoid, and the one that matters most: say died.

Gone away, passed, lost, asleep, gone to a better place. Young children take all of those literally, and the results are real: looking for the person, being frightened of sleep, waiting for someone to come back from a trip.

Something like this works: I have something very sad to tell you. Grandad died this morning. His body stopped working and he will not be coming back. I am so sorry.

Then stop talking. Let the silence sit, however uncomfortable it is, and let them react however they do.

Expect any reaction at all

Children grieve in bursts, which startles adults who expect sustained sadness.

They may cry. They may say nothing. They may ask whether they can go and play, or what is for dinner, and then be devastated at bedtime. They may be furious. Many ask something that sounds callous, like who gets the dog, which is simply a child trying to work out what changes for them.

None of that means they do not care. A child can only hold a big feeling for a short while, so they dip in and out of it for weeks.

Answer the questions, including the hard ones

Children ask directly, and they need honest, short answers.

Will you die too? Everybody dies one day, and I expect to be here for a very long time. That is honest and reassuring without a promise you cannot make.

Was it my fault? No. Nothing you did, said or thought caused this.

How did they die? Give a true, simple version at their level, and expect to repeat it. If the death was sudden, violent, or by suicide, say the simple true thing — his body was too badly hurt, or his illness made him so unwell that he ended his own life — and get advice from a bereavement service about how much to say at their age.

And it is fine to say I do not know. Children trust that more than invented certainty.

Say what happens next

Most of a child's fear is about the ordinary machinery of life, not the philosophy.

So tell them the practical things. Who will take you to school. Where you will sleep. Whether the house changes. Whether you still go on the trip. What a funeral is, and whether they can come, which is usually better offered than imposed.

Repeat it over the days that follow. Children absorb this in pieces, and they will ask the same question four times.

Expect it in their behavior

Grief in children often shows up as behavior rather than words: clinginess, going backward in sleep or toileting, tummy aches, tantrums, trouble at school, falling asleep in class, suddenly being a perfectionist.

Teenagers tend to go to their rooms, to their friends, or to their phones, and may look unaffected while being nothing of the kind.

So keep routines going, because predictability is what holds children together. Tell the school on day one. Keep bedtime the same. Keep the expectations roughly the same, with more patience built in.

Let them see you upset

Children learn what to do with feelings by watching.

If you hide all of it, they conclude they must hide theirs too. If they see you cry and also see you make dinner, they learn that sadness is survivable.

What they should not carry is responsibility for your grief. So cry in front of them and get your own comfort from other adults, not from your child.

And name the person often. Children worry that the dead are never mentioned again. Telling stories, laughing at a memory, keeping a photograph where they can see it: all of that helps more than careful silence.

When to get help

Please talk to your child's doctor or school if, after several weeks, your child cannot sleep, will not eat, is not playing or engaging at all, is frightened to leave you, has stopped going to school, or seems to be getting worse rather than slowly settling.

Ask early rather than late if they are harming themselves, talking about not wanting to be here, or saying they want to be with the person who died; speak to a doctor the same day.

Child bereavement services exist in most places, will see families without a referral, and are extremely good at exactly this. A counselor can also help you with the part of this that is yours, which is often where the real exhaustion sits.

And if you are the adult holding everyone together and it has taken you somewhere dark, please tell a doctor or someone you trust today. In the US you can call or text 988 at any time.

If you are in crisis, call or text 988 (Suicide & Crisis Lifeline, US) or your local emergency number. Theraglee is a wellbeing tool, not a substitute for professional care.
