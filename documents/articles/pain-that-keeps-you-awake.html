Pain That Keeps You Awake

It is worse lying down, worse at three in the morning, and the less you sleep the more it hurts. How to break that loop, and what actually helps the nights.

BodySleep Visitor 886 words

The day was manageable. Then you lie down and there is nothing else to pay attention to, and the pain gets the whole stage. You find one position that works for eleven minutes. You look at the ceiling. By three you are doing arithmetic about how much sleep is still possible.

And the next day everything hurts more, which is not your imagination.

The loop is real, and it runs both ways

Pain makes sleep worse, and poor sleep makes pain worse. Short sleep genuinely lowers pain tolerance, so the same amount of damage or inflammation hurts more after a bad night.

That is why nights matter so much in any long-running pain problem, and why working on the sleep is not a distraction from the pain. It is one of the few levers that moves both.

Night is also when there is nothing else to think about. The same signal that was background during the day becomes the only thing in the room, and attention amplifies pain. That is not weakness, it is how pain works.

Get the practical setup right

Small physical things make more difference at night than they sound like they should, and they are worth experimenting with properly rather than once.

Positioning and support: a pillow under or between the knees for back and hip pain, a thin pillow rather than a thick one for neck pain, a rolled towel where a joint needs filling, an arm supported rather than hanging.

Temperature: warmth before bed for stiff joints and muscles, cool for inflamed and throbbing pain. A hot shower or a wheat bag twenty minutes before you lie down does more than one taken at two in the morning.

Timing the pain relief you already have: ask your pharmacist whether yours can be taken so that it peaks when you are trying to fall asleep rather than wearing off then. People often take it too early.

Stop lying there losing the argument

Staying in bed awake and in pain for hours teaches your brain that bed is where you suffer, and that makes the next night harder.

So after a long stretch awake, get up. Somewhere dim, warm and comfortable, something undemanding, and go back when you feel heavy rather than when you think you ought to. That is not giving up on the night, it is protecting bed from becoming the place where pain lives.

Give your attention somewhere else to be

You cannot out-concentrate pain, and you can give your attention a job, which lowers how loud the pain is.

Audiobooks and spoken radio work better than music for most people, because words occupy the part of the mind that otherwise narrates the pain. Keep it dull and keep it going.

A body scan, moving slowly through the body and noticing without trying to change anything, helps a surprising number of people with pain, partly because it stops the fight with the sensation.

If you have been told to breathe through it and found that useless, try longer out-breaths rather than deep in-breaths, which is the part that actually settles the system.

Watch the two o'clock thinking

The thoughts that arrive in the small hours are reliably worse than daytime ones: this will never get better, I will lose my job, I cannot live like this.

Those thoughts make the pain worse in a measurable way, because distress and pain share machinery. It helps to know, in advance, that your three in the morning conclusions are not reliable and do not need answering tonight. Write the worry on a pad to deal with tomorrow, and put it down.

Keep the days from making the nights worse

Pushing hard on the good days and collapsing on the bad ones leaves most people in more pain and sleeping worse. Steadier, smaller amounts of activity spread through the week, even on the good days, generally produces better nights.

Gentle movement during the day also helps sleep, and so does daylight in the morning, which steadies the rhythm that decides when you get sleepy.

And look honestly at the obvious things: caffeine after lunch, alcohol in the evening, which puts you to sleep and then fragments the second half of the night, and daytime naps long enough to borrow from tonight.

Do not accept a decade of bad nights as the deal

A great many people with long-term pain are quietly surviving on four hours and have never been offered anything for it. There are specific approaches for sleep with pain, there are pain services, and there are medication changes that help one without wrecking the other.

When to get help

Please talk to your doctor if pain is regularly keeping you from sleeping, and say it plainly in numbers: woken this many times, awake this long, for this many months. Ask about timing your medication for the night, about a referral to a pain service or physical therapy, and about treatment for insomnia alongside the pain, since the two are usually treated together. Go sooner if the pain is new, severe, worse at night in a way it was not before, or comes with fever, weight change or weakness, which are reasons to be seen rather than to manage. If the nights have left you hopeless, tell your doctor that part too.

If you are in crisis, call or text 988 (Suicide & Crisis Lifeline, US) or your local emergency number. Theraglee is a wellbeing tool, not a substitute for professional care.
