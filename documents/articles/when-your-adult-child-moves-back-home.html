When Your Adult Child Moves Back Home

Everyone said yes warmly and nobody agreed the rules. What to settle in the first weeks, how to talk about how long, and why mixed feelings about it are normal rather than a failure.

FamilyLife Transitions Visitor 912 words

They are twenty-six, the lease ended or the relationship did or the job did, and their boxes are in the hall. Everyone said yes quickly and warmly, and now you are three weeks in and nobody is quite sure what the rules are.

You love them and you want to help. You are also aware of a feeling you do not much like, somewhere between crowded and resentful, and you are not sure you are allowed to have it.

Say out loud that this is a change for everybody

The usual mistake is treating it as a non-event. Of course you can stay, it is your home too. Kind, and it skips the part where a household rearranges itself around a new adult.

Both sides lose something real. You lose the quiet, the ease of a house with fewer people in it, and the particular freedom of a late middle age. They lose independence, privacy and the sense of being a full adult, which is often more painful than they let on.

Naming that in one conversation does more than any rule you agree afterward. This is good and it is also an adjustment for all of us.

Agree the practical things early, while everyone is still polite

Three weeks in is the right time. Six months in, after a slow build of unspoken irritation, is much harder.

Money. Whether they pay anything, how much, and starting when. Something is usually better than nothing, even a small amount, because it makes them a contributing adult rather than a guest.

Chores. Not helping out, which means nothing, but specific jobs that are theirs: the trash, the kitchen on these days, their own laundry, the shopping once a week.

Space and noise. Which rooms are shared, what happens after eleven, whether a partner stays over and how often, whether you knock.

Food. Who buys it, who cooks, and whether meals are together or separate. This is the one that generates the most daily friction and the one people are most embarrassed to raise.

Write it down, lightly. Not a contract, just an agreed list, because memory is partial and shared memory is worse.

Talk about how long, even if the answer is uncertain

Nobody wants to ask, and not asking is what turns a stay into an impasse.

You do not need a date. You need a direction and a review. Let us talk again at the end of February and see where things are. What would need to be true for the next step to be possible.

If their plan depends on saving money, say what that looks like in practice, because a vague intention to save is not a plan and both of you will be disappointed.

Treat them as an adult, which is harder than it sounds

The house remembers them at fifteen, and so do you, and old roles reassemble themselves within days.

So notice the pull: asking where they are going, commenting on their hours, their diet, their job search, tidying their room, waiting up. Almost all of it is affection, and it all lands as a demotion.

The useful rule is the one you would use with any adult housemate you were fond of. You would not ask what time they would be home. You would ask them to text if they were not coming for dinner you had cooked.

They have their own work to do here too: being in the house they grew up in while feeling like a failure is a particular kind of hard, and withdrawal to a bedroom is usually that rather than rudeness.

Protect the relationship as well as the arrangement

Living together is not the same as being close, and the first can quietly eat the second.

So do something together that is not logistics. A meal out, a walk, a show you both watch. It stops every exchange being about the dishwasher.

And keep your own life running. Your plans, your friends, the room you use for your things. A parent who dissolves into a service is not a better parent, and the resentment that follows is corrosive and hard to admit to.

If there is a partner in the house, protect that too. Two of you being on the same page before a conversation prevents most of the ways this goes wrong.

Expect the feelings to be mixed

You can be glad they are safe and tired of them being here. You can be proud of them and frustrated at the pace. Both at once, every day, is normal rather than a sign that you are failing at this.

Resentment is information, not a verdict. It usually means a specific thing has been left unsaid for too long, and it shrinks quickly once the specific thing is said.

When to get help

Please talk to a family therapist if the arrangement has become a standoff, if conversations about it now turn into the same argument, or if you suspect something harder underneath the move, such as depression, a drinking problem, debt, or a relationship they have not told you about. A few sessions can get a conversation unstuck faster than another year of trying to have it over dinner. And see your own doctor if this has flattened your mood or your sleep, or if you are carrying a worry about your child that you cannot put down, because that is worth proper attention rather than quietly managing it.

If you are in crisis, call or text 988 (Suicide & Crisis Lifeline, US) or your local emergency number. Theraglee is a wellbeing tool, not a substitute for professional care.
