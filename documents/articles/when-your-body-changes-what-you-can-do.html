When Your Body Changes What You Can Do

An injury, an illness or just getting older, and suddenly the stairs are a decision. How to grieve what your body used to do while getting on with the life you have.

Life TransitionsResilience Visitor 928 words

You used to run for the bus. Now you plan around whether there is somewhere to sit, and you have started looking up how far the walk is from the station.

Whether it arrived overnight with an injury or crept in over five years, this is one of the larger changes a person goes through, and it is treated as a practical problem when most of it is not.

It is a loss, and it is allowed to be one

People around you will talk about adaptations and aids and being positive. Underneath that, something has gone, and it deserves to be named.

Not only the activity itself but what came with it: being the capable one, the person who carries things, the one who says yes to the hill walk. Spontaneity goes too, replaced by planning, which is its own quiet grief.

And it is not a single loss you get over. It turns up again at each new thing you discover you cannot do, which is why people are surprised to feel wretched about it years later.

Letting it be sad is not giving up. People who refuse to acknowledge the loss tend to get stuck fighting their own body, which is exhausting and does not win.

Find out what is actually true now

Fear fills in the gaps, so get the real information rather than the version you have assumed.

Ask the specific questions: what is likely to change, what will not, what makes it worse, what is safe, what helps. A physical therapist is often more useful here than anyone, because their job is exactly what you can do and how to do more of it.

And ask how other people with the same thing manage, including the practical tricks. There is usually a body of ordinary knowledge that nobody tells you and that a patient group or a charity can hand over in an afternoon.

Separate what is caused by the condition from what is caused by stopping

This matters more than almost anything else here.

When something hurts or is hard, people reasonably do less of it, and then lose strength and stamina on top of the original problem. A good deal of what feels like decline is deconditioning, and that part is reversible.

So find the floor and work up from it slowly, with help if you can get it. Ten minutes, three times a week, of something gentle, done consistently, does more than a heroic effort once a month, and it is the opposite of the push-until-it-hurts-then-rest-for-a-week cycle most people fall into.

Pacing is the skill, and it is genuinely a skill: doing a bit less than you could on a good day so there is still something left tomorrow.

Use the aids early

People treat a stick, a shower rail, a scooter or a blue parking permit as a defeat and put them off for years.

What those things actually do is buy back range. A stick is the difference between a walk and no walk. A rail is the difference between showering standing up and dreading it. A scooter on the long bits means you still get to the market with everyone else.

The question is not whether you need it. It is what you get to do if you have it.

Rebuild the life around what remains

The temptation is to keep doing the old things badly and then stop doing them. The better move is to work out what you were getting from a thing and find another route to it.

If it was being outdoors, that might become a flatter route, a bench halfway, a boat, a garden. If it was company and effort, perhaps swimming, seated classes or coaching the team you used to play for. If it was feeling capable, something with your hands, or the kind of competence that has nothing to do with legs.

Keep the parts that still work busy. Identity rebuilds around use rather than around what you used to be.

Handle the other people

You will get unwanted advice, false cheer, and the relative who insists you would be fine if you tried harder.

Have one short line ready, and do not argue: this is how it is now, and I have a good team looking after it. Repeat it and change the subject.

Tell the people close to you what actually helps and what does not. Most of them want to get it right and are guessing. Asking before helping, not moving your things, carrying the bag without a comment, slowing down without announcing it: those are specific and easy for someone to do.

And say yes to the modified version of things more often than feels dignified. Being at the party sitting down is better than not being at the party.

When to get help with the mood, not just the body

Please talk to a doctor or a therapist if the low mood has been there for weeks, if you have stopped doing the things you still could do, if pain and sleep are feeding each other, or if you are drinking more to get through the evenings.

Low mood alongside a change in health is extremely common, treatable, and not something to wait out; mentioning it is also how you find the services that exist for exactly this, from pain programs to rehabilitation and peer groups. A counselor who works with health changes is a good place to put the grief, and the grief is the part people most often try to carry alone.
