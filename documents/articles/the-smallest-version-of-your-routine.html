The Smallest Version of Your Routine

On a bad stretch the usual routine is out of reach and dropping it entirely makes things worse. How to build a stripped-down version in advance, and what belongs in it.

DepressionHabits Visitor 876 words

There is a version of your day that works. You get up at a reasonable hour, you eat something, you go outside, you do the things.

And then there are the stretches when none of that is available, and what actually happens is that the whole structure goes at once. No getting up, no eating properly, no leaving, no washing, and three days later the lack of structure is making everything heavier than it had to be.

The problem with all or nothing

Most routines are built for a good week and have no reduced setting. So when you cannot do the full thing, there is nothing to fall back to, and you end up doing none of it.

That is costly, because the parts that hold a day together are doing more work than they look like they are. Daylight, roughly regular meals, roughly regular sleep and wake times, and some movement are not self-improvement. They are the scaffolding, and when they go the low stretch gets both deeper and longer.

The fix is not more discipline on bad days. It is deciding in advance what the stripped-down version is, so that on the day itself there is no decision to make and no standard to fail.

Building the small version

Write it now, while you are able to think, and keep it somewhere you will find it. Three or four items, no more, each small enough to be possible on a day when getting up is the hard part.

Make each one genuinely tiny. Not a walk, but stand outside the front door for two minutes. Not a meal, but eat one thing. Not shower, but wash your face and change your shirt.

Anchor them to something that already happens rather than to a clock. After I use the bathroom in the morning I open the curtains. When I get up I drink a glass of water.

And write it as a list of what counts, not what you should do. On a bad day the difference between those two phrasings decides whether the list helps or becomes one more source of guilt.

What belongs in it

A few things earn their place more than the rest.

Daylight, early if possible. Even through a window. It does more for the shape of the day than anything else on the list.

Water and one thing to eat. Not nutrition, just not running the day on nothing.

Getting up and getting dressed, in any clothes. Not for appearances. Because the body is a cue, and being dressed changes what the day feels like it could contain.

Some movement, however small. Stairs twice. Standing up and stretching. Walking to the end of the street.

And one point of contact with another person, which can be a single message. Low stretches shrink your world fast, and this is the item that keeps a door open.

What to leave out

Anything that produces a number. No weighing, no step counts, no tracking that will hand you evidence you are doing badly.

Anything performative. Elaborate self-care plans are for better weeks. On a bad day they are another thing to not do.

And anything you only included because it sounds like what a person should do. The small version has to be honest to be usable.

Using it without turning it into a test

The point of the list is to lower the floor, not to add a target.

Two of four items is a good day on a bad week. Say that to yourself in advance, so the partial version counts as using the list rather than failing it.

Never add to it on a bad day. The urge to do more once something is working is understandable and it is how people end up back at nothing.

And come off it gradually. When the stretch lifts, add one ordinary thing back at a time rather than resuming the full routine on a Monday, which usually lasts three days.

What this is and is not

It is a way of keeping the basics going when capacity is low, and that genuinely matters, because the basics affect how the low stretch goes.

It is not a treatment, and it will not lift a long low patch by itself. Anyone who tells you that routine alone fixes this is selling something.

So use it for what it is: the thing that keeps the week from getting worse while the actual help is arranged.

When to get help

If a stretch like this has lasted more than a couple of weeks, if things you used to care about have stopped registering, if sleep or appetite have changed noticeably, or if getting through the day is taking everything you have, that is the point to talk to someone rather than manage it with a shorter list.

Start with a doctor or a therapist. Low mood that persists responds well to proper treatment, often better and faster than people expect, and bringing it up does not commit you to anything in particular. If things feel darker than low, or you are having thoughts of harming yourself, do not wait for an appointment. In the US you can call or text 988 at any time.

If you are in crisis, call or text 988 (Suicide & Crisis Lifeline, US) or your local emergency number. Theraglee is a wellbeing tool, not a substitute for professional care.
