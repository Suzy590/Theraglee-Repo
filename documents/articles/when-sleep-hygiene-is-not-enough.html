When Sleep Hygiene Is Not Enough

You have cut the caffeine and dimmed the screens and you are still awake at 2 am. Here is what the sleep advice usually leaves out, and what to try instead.

SleepAnxiety Visitor 814 words

Everyone who has struggled to sleep has heard the list. No caffeine after noon. No screens in bed. Keep the room cool and dark. Go to bed at the same time every night. It is good advice, and for many people it is enough. If you are reading this, it probably was not enough for you. Here is why, and what tends to help when the basics are already in place.

Sleep hygiene fixes the room, not the relationship

The list treats sleep as a matter of conditions: get the environment right and sleep will follow. That works when the problem is the environment. It does not work when the problem is that bed has become a place where you lie awake feeling frustrated. After enough nights like that, the bed itself becomes a cue for wakefulness, the way the smell of a dentist's office can make your jaw tighten. No amount of blackout curtain fixes a learned association.

The first thing to change, then, is what you do when you are not sleeping. If you have been awake for what feels like twenty minutes and you are alert and annoyed, get up. Go to another room, keep the lights low, and do something quiet and slightly boring until you feel drowsy. Then go back. It feels counterintuitive to leave the bed when the whole point is to be in it, but you are teaching your body that bed is for sleeping, not for lying awake.

Trying harder makes it worse

Sleep is one of the few things that retreats when you chase it. The effort of trying to fall asleep is itself a form of arousal, and arousal is the opposite of sleep. People who sleep well are not doing anything. They are simply not preventing it.

If you notice yourself straining, try the reverse. Lie comfortably, keep your eyes gently open in the dark, and quietly try to stay awake a little longer. This is an old trick and a surprisingly reliable one. It takes the pressure off, and without pressure, sleep tends to show up on its own.

The 2 am mind is not a fair witness

Waking in the night is normal. Most adults surface briefly several times and do not remember it. What turns a brief surfacing into an hour of wakefulness is what the mind does with it. At 2 am, small worries look enormous, and the arithmetic starts: if I fall asleep now I will get four hours, if I do not I will be useless tomorrow. That calculation is itself a stimulant.

Two things help here. The first is to have decided, in daylight, that a bad night is survivable. It is. You have had them before, and you got through the next day. The second is to give the worries somewhere to go. A notepad by the bed, and a rule that anything written down is handled until morning, saves a lot of people a lot of hours.

The daytime half of the problem

Sleep is regulated by two systems: a clock, which is set mostly by light, and a pressure, which builds the longer you are awake. Sleep hygiene mostly addresses the clock. The pressure gets ignored, and it is often the real issue.

Pressure builds with wakefulness and activity and drains with rest, including the rest you take without meaning to. A long lie-in after a bad night, a nap on the couch, an evening spent half-asleep in front of the television: each of these lets pressure out before bedtime, so there is less of it to carry you under. The fix is unglamorous. Get up at the same time every day, including after a bad night, and stay properly awake through the day. Morning light outdoors, even ten minutes, sets the clock more firmly than anything you do at night.

If you are spending nine hours in bed to get six hours of sleep, it can also help to shorten the window. Go to bed later, keep the wake time fixed, and let the pressure build until sleep is fast and solid. Then widen the window slowly. This is the core of the therapy that works best for long-term insomnia, and it is worth doing with a therapist trained in it rather than alone, because the first week is hard.

What to stop doing

Stop checking the clock at night; turn it to face the wall. Stop reading about sleep in bed. Stop treating one bad night as a sign that something has gone wrong. And stop trying. Sleep will come when you get out of its way.

If poor sleep has lasted more than a few months, comes with loud snoring or gasping, or leaves you fighting to stay awake during the day, talk with a doctor. Some sleep problems are medical, and they are very treatable once they are named.
