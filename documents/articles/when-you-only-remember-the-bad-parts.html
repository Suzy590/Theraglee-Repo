When You Only Remember the Bad Parts

Somebody asks how the job was and the bad version comes out. Why memory stores the hard parts more strongly, how to go looking for the specifics, and what a line a day fixes.

CBTSelf-Compassion Visitor 790 words

Somebody asks how the job was, or the trip, or those years in the old house, and what comes out is the bad version. The difficult boss. The week it rained. The arguments.

And it is true, as far as it goes. It is also not the whole account, and the fact that your mind reaches for that version first is doing something to how you feel about your own life.

Why the bad parts come up first

This is not a flaw in you. It is how memory works, and it works that way for everyone.

Difficult events are stored more strongly, because the system that lays down memory pays more attention to threat than to a pleasant Tuesday. So the bad bits are sharper, more detailed and easier to find.

Mood then filters it further. When you are low or stressed, the material that matches your mood comes up readily and the rest stays filed. Ask the same question on a good day and you get a noticeably different answer.

And the story smooths out. Once the bad version has been told a few times, it becomes the official account, and the parts that do not fit stop being mentioned until you no longer remember them either.

Go looking for the specifics

The general picture will not shift by arguing with it. What shifts it is detail, because detail is what the general picture is missing.

So pick the period and go hunting for particulars. One ordinary good day. One person you liked. One thing you learned. One thing that made you laugh. One photograph.

You are not trying to conclude that it was fine. You are trying to recover the parts that have been edited out, so the account is accurate rather than cheerful.

Use the things that remember better than you do

Your own recall is the least reliable source available, and there are better ones lying around.

Photographs, especially the dull ones rather than the posed ones. Old messages. A calendar from that year. Anything you wrote at the time. Somebody who was there.

People are often startled by this. The year you have written off as miserable turns out, in the photographs, to contain a good deal of ordinary life, and the messages from the time sound nothing like your memory of it.

Keep a record going forward

The better fix is prospective rather than archaeological, and it is cheap.

One line a day, written at the time. Not a journal, not feelings, just what happened. Had coffee with Sam. Finished the report. The tree outside is turning.

Then on the days when the whole year looks like a write-off, you have evidence from somebody who was actually there, which is much harder to dismiss than a cheerful thought.

Many people say a line a day is the single most useful small habit they have kept, precisely because it corrects this.

Let both be true

The aim is not to replace the bad version with a good one. That lands as denial and it will not hold.

The accurate version usually has both in it. That job was badly managed and I learned the thing I do now. Those years were hard and I was happy on Saturdays. He was difficult and he was kind twice when it counted.

Practice saying it with the and rather than the but, because but deletes whatever comes before it.

Watch where it costs you most

This matters more in a few places, and it is worth noticing them.

Decisions, because a one-sided memory of the last time distorts what you expect from the next one. Relationships, because a running bad account of somebody becomes the relationship. And your own self-assessment, where remembering only the failures makes an honest look at your life impossible.

If you notice you cannot bring to mind a single good stretch across years, that is worth taking seriously rather than treating as an accurate report.

When to get help

Please talk to a therapist if the one-sided account covers most of your life rather than one chapter, if you cannot recall anything good at all, or if looking back reliably leaves you flat for days, because this is one of the central things cognitive therapy works with and it responds well. See your doctor if your mood has been low for more than a couple of weeks, since a low mood is doing some of the filtering here and treating it changes what you can remember. And if what you are looking back at is something frightening or harmful rather than simply difficult, a professional who works with trauma is the right person rather than a general effort to think about it differently.
