Doing One Thing at a Time

You are eating while scrolling while half-listening while planning tomorrow. What all that splitting actually costs, and how doing one thing at a time quiets more than you would expect.

MindfulnessStress Visitor 834 words

Think about the last thing you did. Were you only doing it? Or were you eating and scrolling, walking and on a call, listening to your child while composing a reply in your head, watching a show with a second screen going?

Most people spend most of their day doing two or three things at once, and have come to experience a single unaccompanied activity as slightly unbearable. Eating without a screen feels like waste. Walking without a podcast feels like a missed opportunity.

This is worth examining, because the cost is higher than it looks and the fix is strangely pleasant.

What splitting costs

Attention does not actually divide; it switches, rapidly, and each switch has a cost. So you do both things worse, and you finish more tired than either would have made you.

You also do not fully experience any of it. Meals eaten while scrolling are barely tasted. Shows watched with a phone in hand are not really watched. A large fraction of the day gets consumed without being lived, which is one reason weeks can feel both exhausting and empty.

And it keeps you in a low hum of stress. A mind that is always partly elsewhere is never quite settled, and never gets the small rests that come from being absorbed in one thing.

What one thing at a time does

The unexpected part is how much calmer it feels almost immediately. Attention that is not being split has somewhere to rest. The thing itself becomes more interesting: food has taste, a walk has weather, a conversation has a person in it.

This is most of what mindfulness practically means. Not sitting cross-legged, not emptying your mind. Doing the thing you are doing, and noticing it.

Start with one meal

Pick one meal a day, or even one snack. No phone, no screen, nothing to read. Sit down. Notice the first three bites: temperature, texture, taste. Then let your mind wander if it wants, and come back when you notice.

It will feel uncomfortable, possibly boring, and that discomfort is information about how unused to it you are. Most people find that by the second week it becomes the part of the day they look forward to.

Then pick a second one

A walk with nothing in your ears, once a week. A shower where you notice the water instead of planning the day. Washing the dishes as the thing you are doing rather than an obstacle to the evening. The commute without a podcast.

Not all of them. One or two, chosen on purpose, so the rest of the day can stay as it is.

When you are with a person

This is where the return is largest. Put the phone somewhere else, out of sight, not face down beside you. Listen to what they are saying without assembling your reply while they talk. Ask one question about what they just said before you offer your own thing.

People can tell, more than we assume, whether they have your whole attention. Giving it is one of the more generous ordinary acts there is, and it costs nothing but the habit.

Doing one thing at work

Batch rather than blend. One task with the tabs closed and notifications off for twenty-five minutes does more than an hour of switching, and leaves you far less depleted. Turn off the badges and the previews, which exist to fracture your attention, and check messages at set times.

Expect the pull

When you try this, you will feel the urge for the second input within a minute or two. The hand goes to the phone. That urge is worth watching rather than obeying: notice it, notice that it passes, and stay with the one thing. Each time it passes without being fed, it gets a little weaker.

The boredom is part of it

Underneath the constant second input is often a low-grade avoidance of being alone with your own mind. If sitting with a meal and nothing else makes you restless or uneasy, that is common, and it is worth knowing. Sometimes what comes up in the unfilled minutes is exactly what has been needing your attention.

Keep it small

This is not a program. You are not going to live your whole life single-tasking, and you do not need to. A few protected activities a day, done whole, changes how the day feels out of proportion to the effort.

When one thing at a time is not enough

If your mind is so busy that you cannot stay with anything, if you feel restless and unsettled even when nothing is demanding, or if the quiet brings anxiety or memories that are hard to be alone with, please talk to a therapist. Constant distraction is sometimes a habit and sometimes a way of keeping something at a distance, and knowing the difference matters. Either way, your attention is the most valuable thing you own, and it is reasonable to want it back.
