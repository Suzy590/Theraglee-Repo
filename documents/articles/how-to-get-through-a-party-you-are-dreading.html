How to Get Through a Party You Are Dreading

The invitation has been sitting there and the day has arrived. A practical plan for before, during, and after, so the evening costs you less than the dread suggests it will.

Social AnxietyGrounding Visitor 872 words

The party is tonight. You said yes weeks ago when it was abstract, and now it is real, and you have spent the afternoon constructing reasons not to go. You picture standing alone holding a drink. You picture running out of things to say. You picture everyone else finding this easy.

Here is a plan. Not for becoming a person who loves parties, but for getting through this one with less cost than the dread promises.

Before you go

Decide how long you will stay. An hour is a real amount of time and a complete appearance. Knowing there is an end makes the whole thing possible, and you can always stay longer if it turns out fine.

Eat something first. A lot of social anxiety is worse on an empty stomach, and it keeps you from relying on a drink to take the edge off, which tends to cost more later.

Ask the host one useful question: who else is coming, and is there anything you can help with. Knowing one name changes the room. Having a job, taking coats, refilling something, manning the grill, is the single most effective trick there is, because it gives you a reason to move and a reason to talk to everyone.

Arrive early rather than late. This feels wrong and it is right. Walking into a room of six people is far easier than walking into a room of forty, and if you are early you become one of the people already there.

Getting in the door

The hardest moment is the first three minutes. Have a plan for them. Find the host and say hello. Then find the kitchen, or the drinks, or anything with a task attached.

If you feel the physical spike, fast heart, hot face, the urge to leave, give it a minute. That spike almost always peaks and falls within a few minutes of arriving. Try putting your feet flat on the floor and naming five things you can see. It sounds too simple. It pulls your attention out of your own body and into the room, which is exactly what is needed.

Once you are in

Ask questions. This is the whole of it, really. People enjoy talking about themselves and will remember you as good company if you let them. How do you know the host. What are you working on. Where were you before this. Have you seen anything good lately.

Then listen to the answer and ask one more thing about it. Most conversations fail because nobody follows up, not because nobody was clever.

If you run dry, you can say so cheerfully: I am terrible at parties, tell me something interesting. Most people find this a relief.

Approach pairs and small groups rather than the big loud one, and look for the other person standing alone. They are not judging you. They are hoping someone comes over.

Give yourself exits

You are allowed to leave a conversation. I am going to get a drink, it was good to meet you. Nobody is offended; people do this constantly.

You are also allowed to take a break. The bathroom, the porch, a walk to the car. Two minutes of quiet resets a lot. This is not failing at the party. It is how many people get through any long social event.

When your mind starts narrating

At some point you will catch yourself running commentary: that was a stupid thing to say, they are bored, everyone can tell. Notice that this is the anxiety talking and not a report from the room. Nobody at a party is analyzing you; they are busy being self-conscious themselves.

If you can, put your attention back on the other person. Curiosity and self-consciousness cannot occupy the same space, and the easiest way out of your own head is genuine interest in someone else's.

Leaving

Go when you said you would, or when you have had enough. Say goodbye to the host and go. You do not need a reason, and you do not need to apologize for leaving a party.

Afterward

This is the part that catches people. The replaying, sometimes for days: what you said, how it landed, the moment you misheard someone. Know it is coming and treat it as the last stretch rather than a verdict.

When it starts, try naming it: this is the post-party replay, it always happens, and it is never accurate. Then do something absorbing. It fades faster when it is not fed.

And notice the part that went fine, because your memory will not offer it unprompted. You went. You talked to people. The catastrophe you rehearsed did not occur.

When it is bigger than nerves

If dread of social events is shaping your life, if you turn down most invitations, if the aftermath eats days, or if you can only face people with a drink in hand, that is worth taking seriously. Social anxiety is common and it responds very well to treatment, usually in a matter of months rather than years. A therapist can help you work through it in steps that are actually manageable. You should not have to spend the afternoon before every gathering talking yourself into it.
