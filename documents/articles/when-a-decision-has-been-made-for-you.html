When a Decision Has Been Made for You

The job is going, the move is happening, the answer was no. Why losing your say hurts more than the outcome, where the anger belongs, and which margins are still yours.

Decision MakingEmotion Regulation Visitor 839 words

The decision has been made. The job is going. The treatment is the one they recommend. The move is happening because of somebody else's job. The sale went through. The answer was no and there is no appeal.

You were not consulted, or you were consulted and overruled, and now you have to live inside an outcome you did not pick. That is a specific kind of difficult, and it is not the same as making a hard choice.

Name what is actually hard about it

People in this position often cannot work out why they feel so bad, because on paper the new arrangement may be fine.

It is rarely the outcome. It is the loss of say. Being able to influence what happens to you is something people need at a fairly deep level, and losing it produces a reaction out of proportion to how bad the outcome is.

So you can be genuinely all right about the thing itself and still angry, flat and unsettled for weeks. That is not ingratitude or inconsistency. It is a response to having had no vote.

Let the anger be about the right thing

The anger will find a target, and left alone it usually picks the nearest person rather than the right one.

So be specific with yourself. I am furious that this was decided in a meeting I was not in. I am angry that I found out by email. I am angry that nobody asked what it would do to my week.

Said precisely, it stops leaking onto your partner, the person who delivered the news, or the colleague who had nothing to do with it.

And allow that it may be legitimate. Not every imposed decision is reasonable, and the fact that it is final does not make it right. You can think it was handled badly and still have to live with it.

Separate the parts that are still yours

Almost no decision takes everything. The useful work is finding the margins that remain, because that is where your sense of agency comes back.

Not whether you move, but which neighborhood, and what you take. Not whether the job ends, but how you leave, what you say, what you do next. Not whether you have the treatment, but who comes with you, what you ask, what you want to know.

Write the two lists if it helps. Decided for me. Still mine. The second one is usually longer than it feels, and working on it does more for how you feel than any amount of arguing about the first.

Ask the questions you are entitled to

Even a final decision usually has information attached, and getting it is both practical and steadying.

Why this. Who decided. What happens next and when. What are my options within it. What would it take to revisit it, and is it genuinely closed.

Ask once, plainly, and in writing where that is appropriate. The answer is often less arbitrary than it looked, and sometimes the margins are wider than you were told.

Do not spend months on the appeal in your head

There is a stage where you argue the case endlessly to yourself, assemble the points you should have made, and replay the meeting.

That is normal for a week or two. Past that it costs you the energy you need for the part you can affect.

It helps to say the plain sentence out loud. This has been decided, I did not choose it, and I am going to spend my effort on what happens now. Not as acceptance exactly, more as a decision about where your attention goes.

If there is a real appeal available, make it properly and then put it down until there is an answer.

Mark the thing you lost

An imposed decision often takes something real: a house, a role, a plan, a version of the next five years.

That deserves an acknowledgment rather than being skipped over because the new arrangement is workable. Say what you are losing, to somebody. Walk the route one more time. Keep something.

People who do this move on better than those who go straight to being reasonable about it, because the reasonableness arrives faster when the loss has been admitted.

When to get help

Please talk to a therapist if the anger or the flatness is still running your weeks a couple of months on, if you are not sleeping, or if this has stirred up an older experience of having no say, which is very often what makes one of these land so hard. See your doctor if your mood has been low for weeks or the stress is showing up in your body, and say plainly that something was decided over your head, because it is a real cause rather than a trivial one. And if the decision is a medical one you are uneasy about, a second opinion is a reasonable thing to ask for rather than a complaint about the first.
