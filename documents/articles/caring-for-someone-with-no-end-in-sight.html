Caring for Someone With No End in Sight

Caregiving with no end date is a different problem from a hard few months. How to plan for a long stretch instead of a sprint, and what has to be built in rather than hoped for.

CaregivingResilience Visitor 918 words

At the start you were getting through it. People do that well. A crisis has a shape, you rise to it, and some part of you assumes it will resolve.

Then a year goes by, and another, and the situation is not resolving. It is just the way things are now, and may be for a long time.

That is a different problem from a hard few months, and most of the advice people are given is for the hard few months.

Why open-ended is harder than intense

An endpoint lets you spend yourself. You can run down your reserves if you know when they get refilled, and people routinely do extraordinary things for a fixed stretch.

Without an endpoint, there is nothing to pace against. So people keep running at crisis speed long past the point it is sustainable, because nobody told them to switch modes and no moment announces itself.

There is also the quiet arithmetic nobody says out loud. Any thought about this ending is a thought about somebody you love getting worse or dying, so the ordinary question of how long can I do this has nowhere safe to go.

And the support fades. People help loudly at the start. Two years in, almost nobody asks, because to everyone else this is simply your life now.

Switching from sprint to long haul

The useful shift is from getting through to building something that runs for years. That means a few unglamorous things.

Work out what is actually required, as opposed to what you have been doing. A lot of what caregiving becomes is habit and guilt rather than need. Writing out everything you do in a week, and marking what would genuinely go wrong if it stopped, usually finds some room.

Get other people into the structure, not just into emergencies. One person every Tuesday is worth far more than several people who would help if you called. A set schedule feels awkward to arrange and then does the work by itself.

Find out what you are entitled to, properly. Respite, home help, day programs, benefits, equipment, a needs assessment. Most caregivers are getting less than they qualify for, usually because the system is hard to navigate rather than because they were refused.

And put your own appointments in the calendar as if they were someone else's. Your own health is the single most important part of a long caregiving arrangement, and it is the first thing to go.

Rest that has to be built in

Rest cannot be what is left over, because nothing is left over.

Protect something small and regular, and make it specific. One evening. A morning walk. An hour on Saturday that is yours whatever happens. Small and reliable beats large and theoretical.

Take respite before you need it. People save it for when they are desperate, which means they are always desperate. Using it while things are manageable is what keeps them manageable.

And keep at least one thing in your life that is not about this. A class, a friend you talk to about other things, work you care about. Caregiving expands until it is a whole identity, and the people who last tend to be the ones who kept something else alive.

The feelings people do not admit to

Resentment is normal and nearly universal. It is not a sign you love them less, and it does not go away by being hidden. It gets smaller when it has somewhere to be said out loud.

So is wishing it were over, in whatever form that takes. Almost everybody in a long caregiving arrangement has had that thought, and almost nobody says so. It is a thought about being exhausted and it is not a betrayal.

So is grief while the person is still here. People change, and you can lose a great deal of someone long before they are gone. That is a real loss and it deserves to be treated as one.

None of this is improved by being carried silently, which is the main argument for a group of other caregivers, who tend to receive all of it without flinching.

Letting the arrangement change

Build in a review. Every few months, look at what has changed, what is no longer working, and what you would need. Arrangements set up for the person's needs two years ago are very often still running.

Decide in advance what your limits are, while you are calm. Lifting. Nights. Personal care. Money. Knowing your line beforehand makes it possible to arrange something else before the breaking point rather than after it.

And it is allowed to be a line. Needing more help, or a different arrangement entirely, is not a failure of love. It is usually what makes the care sustainable.

When to get help for yourself

If you are not sleeping, if you have stopped seeing anyone, if you are drinking more, or if you feel flat about things you used to care about, that is a sign to get support for yourself rather than to try harder.

Talk to a doctor, and say plainly that you are a caregiver, because it changes what they can point you toward. A therapist is worth having in your own right, and caregiver support groups exist nearly everywhere, often free. If you are frightened by how angry you get, or by thoughts of harming yourself or the person you care for, bring that to a professional now rather than carrying it.

If you are in crisis, call or text 988 (Suicide & Crisis Lifeline, US) or your local emergency number. Theraglee is a wellbeing tool, not a substitute for professional care.
