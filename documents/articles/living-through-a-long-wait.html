Living Through a Long Wait

Test results, a hiring decision, a court date, a visa: waiting for news you cannot influence has its own kind of difficulty. How to hold a life together while the answer is pending.

ResilienceStress Visitor 920 words

The test was on Monday. The results come next Thursday. Between those two points you have nine days, and no amount of thinking will move the answer by a single hour.

Waiting like this is its own experience, different from the thing you are waiting for. A diagnosis, a hiring decision, a court date, a visa, a loan, a call about someone in hospital. What makes it hard is not only the stakes but the combination: it matters enormously, and there is nothing to do.

Why an empty wait is so tiring

Most stress comes with an action attached. This one does not, and your mind does not handle that well.

So it substitutes activity for action. It rehearses both outcomes. It searches for information that does not exist yet. It reruns the appointment looking for clues in someone's tone of voice. It plans for a version of the future it cannot yet see.

All of that feels like preparing, which is why it is so hard to stop. But rehearsing an outcome does not prepare you for it; it just makes you live it repeatedly at full volume before you know whether it is real.

Decide what you are going to do with the not-knowing

The task in a wait is not to stop feeling uncertain. It is to stop spending your days as though the answer is already in.

One question helps more than any other: is there anything I can actually do about this today? Usually the honest answer is no, or it is one small thing: chase the appointment, send the document, ask when to expect the call.

Do that one thing early, and then treat the rest of the day as yours. Not because the wait does not matter, but because the alternative is handing over nine days to something that will take the same nine days either way.

Give the worry a time and a place

Trying not to think about it fails, and you have probably already discovered that. Deliberately corralling it works better.

Pick a time, fifteen minutes, same slot each day. That is when you are allowed to think about it properly: what you fear, what you would do, what you need to find out. When it arrives outside that window, note it and tell yourself it will get its turn at six.

This is not a trick to make the thoughts go away. It is a way to stop them being in charge of every hour, and most people find the worry loses some of its urgency when it has an appointment.

Do the obvious, unglamorous things

Waiting is physically taxing, and the basics matter more than usual.

Keep the shape of your days: get up at the normal time, eat at normal times, go outside once. Keep the plans you had, even at half strength; cancelling everything makes the wait the only thing in the room. Move your body, because unspent stress goes somewhere and movement is the cheapest place to put it. Protect sleep, and if your mind spins at night, get up for ten minutes rather than lying there arguing with it.

Be careful with searching. Looking up the thing you are waiting on gives a moment of relief and then more to fear, and it never delivers the one piece of information you want, which is your answer.

Plan one step, not two futures

There is a useful middle between refusing to think about it and living in the bad outcome.

Make one short, specific plan: if the news is bad, who I will call, what I will ask, and what I will not decide that day. Write it down. Then close it.

That plan is usually enough to satisfy the part of you that insists on being ready, and it takes far less out of you than imagining the whole of a life you may never have to live.

What to tell people

Decide in advance who you are telling and what you want from them, because being asked about it repeatedly is its own strain.

Some people want company and no questions. Some want to be distracted entirely. Both are fine, and both are easier to get if you say which one you are: I do not want to talk about it, I would like to go for a walk, is a complete and reasonable request.

If it is a wait other people are also inside, say your version out loud rather than assuming you are handling it the same way.

When the wait gets longer

Sometimes the date moves, or there is no date at all. That is harder, and it needs a different setting.

Set your own checkpoints instead: a day each week when you chase for news and update your plan, and the rest of the week left alone. Open-ended waits swallow people who keep checking hourly, and the weekly version is survivable for months.

When to get help with it

If the waiting has stopped you eating or sleeping, if you cannot work or be with people, if panic is arriving regularly, or if the wait is about something that would change your life completely, there is no reason to do it alone. A therapist can help with the spinning, and a doctor can help if sleep or panic has become the bigger problem. Many hospitals and clinics also have counselors attached to the service you are waiting on, and asking whether one is available is a perfectly ordinary request.

If you are in crisis, call or text 988 (Suicide & Crisis Lifeline, US) or your local emergency number. Theraglee is a wellbeing tool, not a substitute for professional care.
