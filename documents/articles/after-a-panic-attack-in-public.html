After a Panic Attack in Public

It happened in the supermarket, or the meeting, and people saw. How to handle the hours afterward, and what to do so the place does not become somewhere you avoid.

PanicShame Visitor 918 words

You were in line at the store, or halfway through the presentation, and your body decided this was an emergency. Someone fetched you a chair. Someone else asked if you wanted water.

It passed, as it always does. And now you are in the car park replaying it, and the worst part is not the panic. It is that people watched.

Two different problems

The panic itself is one thing, and it is over.

The second thing is the aftermath: the hot shame, the certainty that you made a scene, and the urge to never go back to that shop, that office, that train.

Those need separate handling, and the second one is what decides how much of your life this costs you.

What people actually saw

Almost always less than you think.

Panic is enormously loud from the inside and surprisingly quiet from the outside. What people generally see is somebody going pale, breathing fast, sitting down, maybe leaving. Not the roaring in your ears, not the conviction that you were dying.

And what they mostly felt was concern, briefly, followed by getting on with their day. The person who brought the chair has told the story once, as a kind thing they did, and forgotten it.

That is not reassurance for its own sake. It is a correction to the version your mind is running, which is filmed from the ceiling with everyone staring.

Handle the hours afterward deliberately

Panic leaves a physical hangover: shaky, exhausted, faintly ill, with a mind that wants to replay.

So treat the next hour as recovery. Water, something to eat, somewhere to sit, a walk if you can manage one. Do not go straight into something demanding and do not drive while still trembling.

Then put a limit on the replaying. One pass, with a pen if it helps: where it happened, what led up to it, what you did, how long it lasted. That is the useful information. The twentieth pass is not analysis, it is punishment.

What to say to people

The dread of the conversation is often worse than the conversation.

Short and matter-of-fact works best: sorry about yesterday, I had a panic attack, they are horrible and harmless, I am fine. Then change the subject.

You do not owe anyone an explanation of your history, and you do not have to apologize at length for having a body. If somebody was kind, thank them once.

With a manager or a colleague, you can keep it to capacity: I was unwell for a few minutes, I am all right, and I may need a couple of small adjustments. That is enough.

Expect the shame to lift before the fear does

Most people find the embarrassment fades within days, especially once they have had one ordinary interaction with somebody who was there.

The fear of a repeat lasts longer, and it is the thing that shapes behavior. Watch for it: taking the long way round, going at quiet times, needing somebody with you, sitting near the door, carrying water you do not drink, keeping your phone in your hand.

Each of those gives relief and quietly teaches your alarm that the place was genuinely dangerous.

Go back sooner than you want to

This is the part that decides the next six months, and it is uncomfortable.

Go back to the same place, soon — within days rather than weeks — and keep the visit short. Then again. Then again, a little longer, at a busier hour, without the person who came with you.

Repetition is what updates the alarm. One brave visit followed by three weeks of avoidance teaches it nothing.

And drop the props gradually rather than all at once. The order usually goes: quiet hour with a friend, quiet hour alone, busy hour with a friend, busy hour alone.

If it happens again

Assume it might, and have a short plan so it is not a crisis.

Breathe out longer than you breathe in. Feet on the floor, hand on something solid. Say the flat sentence: this is panic, it peaks in a few minutes, it has never hurt me.

Stay where you are if you possibly can, because leaving at the peak is what teaches escape. Sitting down is fine. Letting somebody fetch you water is fine.

And if a stranger helps you, let them. Most people are glad to be useful, and accepting help is not a debt.

When to get help with it

Please talk to a doctor if you are not sure the symptoms are panic, particularly if you fainted, had chest pain with exertion, or felt confused; that deserves checking once.

And talk to a doctor or a therapist if panic is happening regularly, if you have started avoiding places, or if the dread of it is shaping where you go and what you say yes to. Panic responds very well to treatment, usually in a small number of sessions, and the method is largely what is above, done in order with someone keeping you honest about the avoidance.

If the shame is the loudest part — if you cannot face people who were there, or you have withdrawn since — say that plainly to the doctor or therapist you see. It is a common and treatable piece of this rather than a character flaw, and if it has taken you somewhere dark, please tell someone you trust today. In the US you can call or text 988 at any time.

If you are in crisis, call or text 988 (Suicide & Crisis Lifeline, US) or your local emergency number. Theraglee is a wellbeing tool, not a substitute for professional care.
