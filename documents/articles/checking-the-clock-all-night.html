Checking the Clock All Night

Doing the arithmetic on how much sleep is left makes the night worse. Why clock-watching keeps you awake, what to do with the numbers, and how to get through the following day.

SleepWorry Visitor 782 words

It is twelve forty. Then it is one fifty. Then you do the sum: if I fall asleep right now I still get five hours. Then it is three, and the sum has changed, and the sum is worse.

Looking at the clock feels like gathering information. It is closer to the opposite, and it is one of the few things about a bad night that is entirely within your control.

What the arithmetic does

Each check turns a vague night into a specific countdown, and a countdown is a deadline. Deadlines produce a small amount of alertness, which is the exact state you are trying to get out of.

It also converts the night into a performance with a result. Five hours becomes a score, the score gets worse each time you look, and watching your own score decline is not restful.

Then the forecasting starts. Tomorrow is ruined. I will be useless in the meeting. That is no longer about sleep at all, it is about tomorrow, and it keeps you awake far more effectively than the original wakefulness did.

And the checking is a habit with a reward. There is a flicker of hope each time that less time has passed than you feared. Occasionally that is true, which is precisely what makes the habit stick.

Getting the clock out of reach

This is the practical bit and it is unusually effective for how simple it is.

Put the phone across the room, or face down and out of arm's reach. If you use it as an alarm, that is fine, it just does not need to be beside your head.

Turn the clock around. A clock you have to get up to read is one you will check once instead of eleven times.

If you wake and want to know the time, the honest answer is that it does not matter. There are only two possibilities: enough time left to sleep, or nearly morning. Neither changes what you should do next, which is the same thing either way.

What to do instead when you wake

Do not try to force it. Lying still and willing sleep is the most reliable way to stay awake, because effort and sleep do not coexist.

If you have been awake for what feels like twenty minutes or more, get up. Leave the bedroom. Sit somewhere dim with something undemanding and go back when you feel heavy rather than when you think you should.

That advice feels wrong because it costs you time in bed. It is worth it, because the alternative teaches your brain that the bed is a place for lying awake and thinking, which is the thing that turns one bad night into a bad month.

If thoughts are the problem, get them onto paper rather than arguing with them. Keep something to write on outside the bedroom. Half of what is circulating at two in the morning is a list of things you are afraid of forgetting, and a list stops circulating once it is written.

Breathing out slowly, longer than you breathe in, for a couple of minutes, helps more than most of the elaborate routines people try.

The following day

How you handle the day after matters, because it decides whether tomorrow night goes the same way.

Get outside in the morning light, early if you can. It is the strongest signal for setting the next night straight.

Do not nap for long or late. Twenty minutes before mid-afternoon is usually fine. An hour at five will cost you the coming night.

Resist going to bed much earlier to catch up. Lying in bed longer while unable to sleep makes the pattern worse, not better. Keep your wake time steady even after a bad night, which is the single most useful habit for this.

And be careful with the story you tell about it. I slept badly and today will be harder than usual is accurate. I cannot function, the whole day is lost is the forecasting again, and it tends to produce the result it predicts.

When it is more than a few bad nights

If this has been most nights for a month or more, if you dread bedtime, or if the daytime is genuinely suffering, that is worth treating properly rather than enduring.

There is a specific, well-established approach for ongoing sleeplessness, usually short, which a doctor or therapist can point you to, and it works better than sleep medication for most people over time. A doctor is also the right stop if you snore heavily, wake gasping, or feel exhausted after a full night, since a few ordinary physical causes produce exactly that.
