When Your Goal Depends on Somebody Else

Moving, studying, a baby, the trip. How to sort what is yours from what is theirs, find out what the hesitation really is, and tell when a slow yes is actually a no.

GoalsCommunication Visitor 885 words

You have decided to move house, or try for a baby, or go back to school, or finally take the trip. And then you discover the part nobody mentions about adult goals: this one does not work unless somebody else is also doing it.

That is a particular kind of frustration. All the usual advice about breaking a goal into steps assumes the steps are yours to take, and here half of them belong to a person who has their own pace, their own doubts and their own life.

Work out which parts are actually yours

The first useful move is to sort the goal rather than push harder at all of it.

Write two columns. What I can do alone. What needs them. Be honest about the second column rather than hopeful.

Then do everything in the first column. Not as a way of pressuring them, but because it is the only part you control and because it tends to be more than you think: the research, the savings, the paperwork, the appointment, the course prospectus, your own fitness, your own share.

Doing your own half well also changes the conversation. It is very different to say I have done this part, rather than why have you not done yours.

Find out what their hesitation actually is

Most stalled shared goals are stalled on something unspoken, and the longer it stays unspoken the more it looks like indifference.

So ask, once, properly, and not in passing. What would have to be true for you to feel ready. What is the part that worries you most. Is it the thing itself, or the timing, or the money, or something you have not said.

Then listen without arguing. The point of that conversation is information, not persuasion, and a reason you did not know about is worth more than another round of enthusiasm.

Often it turns out to be concrete and solvable. A number they are frightened of. A commitment they did not know could be smaller. A fear about something unrelated.

Agree a next step and a date rather than a decision

Asking somebody to commit to a whole life change is a big ask that invites a no, or worse, a vague yes.

So shrink it. Will you come to one viewing. Will you read this one page. Will you have one conversation with the doctor. Shall we talk about it again on the first of next month.

A small step with a date on it moves things. An open-ended agreement that we will think about it does not, and both of you know it.

Say what it means to you, once and clearly

There is a version of this where you never actually tell them how much it matters, because you are being considerate, and they genuinely do not know.

So say it plainly. This matters a great deal to me. I do not want to pressure you and I do want you to know where I am.

Then stop. Said once, clearly, it lands. Said weekly, it becomes noise and then becomes a grievance.

Notice when it is not going to happen

This is the hard part, and the part most worth being honest about. Sometimes the answer is no, and no is not a slow yes.

The signs are usually clear in hindsight: the same conversation for a year with no movement, agreement in the moment followed by nothing, or a reason that changes every time you ask.

At that point the question stops being how do I get them to do this, and becomes what do I do about wanting something they do not want. That is a bigger question and it deserves a real answer rather than another two years of waiting.

Sometimes the answer is that it was less important than the relationship, and you let it go properly rather than resentfully. Sometimes it is that you do a smaller version alone. Occasionally it is that this is a genuine fork in the road. All three are survivable. The thing that is corrosive is leaving it undecided while the resentment builds.

Keep a life while you wait

If everything you look forward to is behind somebody else's decision, the waiting will eat the relationship from the inside.

So have things that are yours and happening anyway. A plan with a friend, a smaller version of the thing, something you are learning, a trip you take alone if that is possible.

People who do that wait better, decide more clearly, and are considerably easier to live with.

When to get help

Please consider a couples counselor if the two of you have had the same conversation many times without moving, since a neutral professional can get at the thing underneath in a way that two people who love each other often cannot. Talk to a therapist on your own if the waiting has turned into resentment you cannot put down, or if you are facing the possibility of giving up something that matters deeply to you. And see your doctor if this has been sitting on your mood or your sleep for weeks, or if the goal involves your health or fertility, where the timing is a medical question as well as a personal one and worth proper advice.
