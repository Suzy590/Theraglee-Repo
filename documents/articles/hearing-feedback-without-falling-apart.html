Hearing Feedback Without Falling Apart

One line of criticism in an otherwise good review can ruin a week. How to take in feedback as information, separate the useful part, and stop relitigating it at midnight.

PerfectionismSelf-Esteem Visitor 900 words

Your manager said four good things and one critical one. You remember the critical one. You will remember it for a month, and you are already composing the explanation you did not give at the time.

Meanwhile nobody else in the building is thinking about it at all.

This is not thin skin, and it is not vanity. For a lot of people feedback does not land as information; it lands as a verdict, and a verdict needs defending.

What makes it hit so hard

Two things, usually.

The first is that criticism gets filed under who you are rather than what you did. This draft is unclear becomes I am not good at this becomes they are going to work out that I cannot do the job. Each step feels like the same sentence, and only the first one was actually said.

The second is that for many people being good at things is load-bearing. If being competent is how you feel acceptable, then a flaw is not a flaw; it is a threat to the whole arrangement. That is why a small note can produce a reaction out of all proportion.

Knowing which of those is driving it tells you where to aim: the first needs a translation habit, the second needs more than a technique.

In the moment: take it in, do not process it

You cannot usefully evaluate feedback while your ears are ringing, so split the job in two. Receiving it is one task. Deciding what to do with it is another, and it can wait an hour or a day.

While receiving, do three things. Ask for a specific example, because vague criticism is unusable and examples shrink it. Write down what they actually said, in their words. Then say thank you, that is useful, I want to think about it.

Resist the urge to explain. The defense you give in the first thirty seconds is nearly always the one you regret, and it tends to make the other person repeat the criticism more firmly to be sure it landed.

Sort it later, on paper

When you are calmer, take what they actually said and sort it into three piles.

True and useful: things you can act on. Pick at most one or two and decide a concrete change. That is the whole value of feedback, and it is usually smaller than the emotional weight suggested.

True but not important: things that are fair and that you are choosing not to prioritize. You are allowed this pile. Not every accurate observation deserves a project.

Not accurate, or not theirs to judge: sometimes feedback is about their preference, their bad week, or a misunderstanding of what you were asked to do. Decide whether it is worth a conversation, and if not, let it be their view rather than a fact about you.

Writing it down matters. On paper, one note usually looks like one note. In your head it expands to fill the available space.

Notice the two unhelpful responses

Collapse is one: taking it all in, agreeing with everything, and spending a week feeling exposed. This looks like humility and is actually a way of making the criticism bigger than it was.

Dismissal is the other: they do not understand, this is unfair, nothing to learn here. That protects you from the sting and also from the useful part.

The middle is less dramatic and more effective: they have a point about this specific thing, I am going to change that specific thing, and the rest I am setting down.

Ask for it on purpose, in small amounts

The best way to make feedback less frightening is to have more of it, more often, in lower doses.

Ask one person for one thing: what is the one thing you would change about this. Ask midway through a piece of work rather than at the end, where the stakes are lower and a change is still cheap.

Frequent small feedback also stops the annual-review effect, where everything is saved up and arrives at once with your whole worth apparently attached to it.

The midnight relitigating

The worst part of criticism is often not the conversation; it is the version that runs for three nights afterward, where you win the argument repeatedly and feel no better.

If that starts, two things help. Write the response you want to give, properly, and then decide in daylight whether to send any of it; most people find they do not want to. And notice that replaying it is not processing, it is rehearsing, and rehearsing makes the memory stronger.

Then set a stopping rule: I have taken the useful part, and I am not going to review this again tonight. You will have to apply it more than once.

When it is bigger than feedback

If a single piece of criticism can take out a week, if you avoid work where you might be judged, if you cannot share anything unfinished, or if your sense of being acceptable rises and falls entirely with how your work is received, that is worth working on with someone.

It is common ground in therapy and it moves. A therapist can help with the part underneath, where being good at things and being worth something got welded together, and that tends to do more than any amount of better technique for receiving notes.
