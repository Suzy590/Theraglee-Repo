When You Are Touched Out

Being climbed on and leaned against all day leaves some parents unable to bear one more hand. What that reaction is, why it is not a failure of love, and what actually relieves it.

ParentingSelf-Care Visitor 818 words

By the end of the day you cannot bear one more hand on you. Not your child's, not your partner's. Somebody puts an arm around you and your whole body says no, and then you feel terrible about it.

Parents sometimes call this being touched out. It is extremely common, rarely talked about, and it frightens people, because it feels like love has gone missing.

What is actually happening

Touch is a sense, and senses can be saturated. A day of being climbed on, leaned against, pulled at and carried is hours of continuous input, most of it unpredictable and none of it chosen.

When input runs that high for long enough, the system stops tolerating more. What arrives is not a reasoned preference. It is closer to a flinch, and it fires before you have an opinion about it.

Two things make it worse. Unpredictability, because touch you cannot anticipate costs more than touch you can. And lack of control, because you cannot decline most of it when a two-year-old is involved.

Add not much sleep, which lowers the threshold for every kind of input, and the end of the day arrives with nothing left.

Why it is not a failure of love

Because it is a sensory state, not a feeling about the person. You can be entirely devoted to someone and have no capacity left for their hands.

It also explains the thing that confuses people most, which is that it falls hardest on your partner. By evening the tolerance is gone, and the one person whose touch you could decline is the one who gets declined. That reads as rejection and it is really an empty tank.

Saying that out loud matters, because the guilt makes it worse. People who think the flinch means something about their love spend the evening being touched while braced, which uses what little is left.

What actually relieves it

Short, total breaks, more often than feels justified. Ten minutes with nobody on you does more than an hour of being touched slightly less. Lock the bathroom door. Step outside. Sit in the car.

Introduce touch you choose. Hands in hot water, a shower, pressure against a wall, a heavy blanket. Chosen input is processed differently from imposed input, and it genuinely resets some of this.

Reduce other input at the same time. Noise, lights, screens, people talking. Touch is rarely the only channel that is full, and dropping the others lowers the whole load.

Change the kind of contact rather than ending it. Many people can manage still, predictable contact when they cannot manage being climbed on. Sitting beside someone, a hand held without movement, leaning back to back.

And where you can, redistribute. If someone else in the house can do the bedtime carrying, that is not an indulgence, it is maintenance.

Telling your partner without it landing as rejection

Say it before the evening rather than in the moment when it comes out as recoiling.

Name the cause, not the person. I have been touched all day and I have nothing left. It is not about you.

Offer the version you can manage. Can we sit next to each other instead. Give me twenty minutes and then I will be better. Still, not moving.

And name the other thing directly if it applies, because it usually does. A great many people in this state are avoiding physical closeness entirely out of fear that any contact will be read as a beginning. Saying I want to be close and I do not have anything left today prevents months of quiet misreading.

With the children

You do not have to accept all of it, and setting a limit is reasonable parenting rather than coldness.

Small, concrete, offered warmly works. I need my hands for a minute. You can sit right here next to me. Not on my neck, my hands are fine.

Giving them something else to do with the need helps more than refusing it. A cushion to squeeze, your hand instead of your face, sitting shoulder to shoulder.

And it is worth knowing this eases as they get older. Most of the people who feel this most intensely are in a stage that ends.

When it is more than a full day

If the aversion has spread beyond the end of the day, if you are dreading being near your family rather than being tired of it, or if touch has become unpleasant in a way that feels unfamiliar, that is worth raising.

Talk to a doctor if it came on alongside low mood, poor sleep or anxiety, since all three lower sensory tolerance and all three are treatable. A therapist can help with the parts that are about resentment, lost autonomy or a couple that has stopped touching, which are the most common things sitting underneath this and are far more workable than people assume.
