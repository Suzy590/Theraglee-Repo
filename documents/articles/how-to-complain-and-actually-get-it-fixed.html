How to Complain and Actually Get It Fixed

The broken heating, the wrong charge, the thing that was promised and never arrived. How to complain in a way that works, without rehearsing it for three weeks first.

AssertivenessCommunication Visitor 990 words

The heating has been broken for two weeks. You have mentioned it twice, politely, and nothing has happened. You are now somewhere between letting it go and writing an email you would regret.

Most people are bad at this, and not because they lack nerve. Complaining well is a specific skill with a shape to it, and nobody teaches it.

The good news is that the effective version is calmer and shorter than the version you have been dreading.

Decide what you actually want first

This is the step people skip, and skipping it is why complaints go nowhere.

Before you say anything, finish this sentence: what I want is… A repair by Friday. A refund of this amount. The charge removed. An apology and a note on my file. To be moved to a different team.

A complaint without a request is just a report of displeasure, and the person receiving it has nothing to action. A complaint with one specific, achievable request is a task they can close, which is what they would genuinely rather have.

Keep it to one request. Three asks dilute each other and give them a choice of which to answer.

Get the facts into four lines

Long complaints are weak complaints. The person reading it is busy, and the detail you have been rehearsing is mostly for your benefit.

What they need: what you bought or were promised, when, what happened instead, what you have already tried, and what you want. Dates, amounts, reference numbers. Nothing else.

Write it that short even when you are furious. A tight factual account is far harder to dismiss than an account of how it made you feel, and it also protects you from the thing that most often loses a complaint: being so angry that you become the problem in the room.

Be pleasant, be specific, be persistent

Those three in combination do most of the work.

Pleasant, because the person in front of you almost never caused this and can choose how hard to try for you. Specific, because vagueness is where complaints die. Persistent, because many systems rely on people giving up after the second attempt.

A line that works in almost every setting: I know this is not your fault, and I would really appreciate your help getting it fixed. It reliably moves people onto your side.

Put it in writing, and keep the record

Phone calls vanish. Writing accumulates.

So after any call, send a short message summarizing what was agreed and by when: just to confirm, you said the engineer will come on Thursday and the charge will be reversed. That sentence has resolved more disputes than any amount of shouting.

Keep a simple log: date, who you spoke to, what they said. If this goes further, that log is the whole case, and it takes thirty seconds a time to maintain.

Escalate on purpose, not in anger

If two attempts have failed, the next step is not a louder version of the same attempt. It is a different level.

Ask plainly: who else can help with this, and what is your complaints process. Those two questions open doors, because organizations have procedures that staff are obliged to follow once invoked.

Then go to the named route: the complaints team, the manager, the regulator, the ombudsman, the dispute process with your card provider, the local authority for a landlord. Many of these are free and far more effective than people expect.

And set a deadline in your own message: if I have not heard by Friday I will take this to the complaints team. Say it neutrally and then actually do it. A deadline you do not keep teaches them to wait you out.

What to do with the anger

By the time most people complain they have been carrying it for weeks, and the complaint arrives with all of that attached.

Write the furious version if you need to, and do not send it. Then write the four-line version from the facts in it. The anger is useful fuel for finally acting and almost never useful content.

If you feel yourself escalating mid-call, say you need to call back and do it later. A complaint made while shaking tends to end with you apologizing, which is the opposite of the goal.

Public pressure, carefully

A public post or a review sometimes works where private channels have failed, and companies do respond to visible complaints.

Keep it factual if you use it: what happened, what you asked for, what they said. Accurate and unemotional is more persuasive and much safer than a furious account.

Use it after the ordinary routes rather than instead of them, and keep it proportionate to the thing.

Knowing when to stop

Not everything is winnable, and some complaints cost more in attention than the outcome is worth.

So decide your limit in advance: I will give this two more attempts and then let it go. Letting go deliberately is completely different from losing by attrition; one ends it, the other keeps it running in your head for months.

And notice what it is really about. Sometimes a small complaint is carrying something larger: being treated as if you do not matter, by a system that has done it before. That is worth knowing, because it tells you whether a refund will actually settle it.

When this is the hard part

If complaining makes you shake, if you cannot do it at all and things go unresolved for years because of it, or if a past experience of not being believed has made the whole thing feel unsafe, that is worth more than a technique.

It is also very workable. A therapist can help with the part that makes speaking up feel dangerous, and in the meantime there is no shame in asking a friend or family member to make the call with you or for you.
