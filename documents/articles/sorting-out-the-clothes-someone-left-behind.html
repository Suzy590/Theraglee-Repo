Sorting Out the Clothes Someone Left Behind

The closet is the job everyone puts off, and for good reason. Why clothes are harder than paperwork, how to do it in a way you can live with, and what nobody warns you about.

LossGrief Visitor 980 words

The papers got dealt with. The bank was told. Somebody canceled the subscriptions. And the closet is still exactly as it was, because every time you open it you close it again.

This is the task people put off longest, and they are usually embarrassed about how long. There is no need to be. It is harder than the rest for reasons that are worth understanding.

Why clothes are the hard part

Paperwork is about a person. Clothes carry the person, and your body knows it before you do.

They hold smell, which goes straight past thinking. Of everything a person leaves, their clothes are the thing most likely to put them in the room with you, and that is both the reason to do it and the reason you cannot.

They hold shape. A coat keeps the way somebody stood in it. A worn heel keeps how they walked. None of that is in a bank statement.

They are full of small facts. The shirt they wore to the thing. The sweater they were given and never liked. Each item asks you to remember something specific, and there are hundreds of them.

And they are the last thing that is still arranged the way they arranged it. Taking it apart feels like undoing the last room that is still theirs, which is exactly what it is.

When to do it

There is no correct time, and anybody who tells you otherwise is describing their own grief, not yours.

Later is usually easier than sooner. Most people find the first few months are the worst possible moment, and that six months or a year in it is the same job with far less force behind it.

Do it before you are made to, if a deadline exists. A house being sold or a room being needed sets the date, and it is much better to choose a day inside that than to be cleared out in a rush at the end.

And know that waiting has a cost too. Some people keep a closet untouched for years and find it has become a room they avoid, which is its own kind of weight. There is no free option, only two different ones.

Doing it in a way you can live with

The practical shape of this matters more than people expect.

Do not do it alone, unless you are certain you want to. One person who is fond of you but not shattered themselves is the right company. They do the lifting and the bags and the questions, and you do the deciding.

Work in short sessions with a real stopping point. Two hours and then a meal, not a whole weekend. This is one of the few jobs where stamina is genuinely not the goal.

Start with the easy end on purpose. Socks, worn-out things, the stuff nobody has feelings about. You are getting your hand in, and the things that matter get decided when you are warmer, not cold.

Make a small keep box before you start, and keep it small deliberately. A few things, chosen, not a second closet. People who keep everything usually end up doing the whole job again in three years.

And allow yourself to not decide. A box labeled not yet is a perfectly good answer and it does not mean you have failed at this.

What nobody warns you about

A few things ambush almost everybody, and they are easier when expected.

Pockets. There will be a receipt, a note, a ticket, something with their handwriting on it. Decide now that you will stop when it happens rather than push on.

The things that are not theirs. Something of yours they kept, a gift you forgot giving. This one tends to land harder than the clothes.

How ordinary most of it is. Grief expects significance and finds laundry. The dullness is strange and it does not mean you are doing it wrong.

And that you may feel relief at the end, which arrives with guilt attached. Relief is not disloyalty. It is what finishing a hard job feels like.

Where things can go

It helps to know in advance, because deciding in the moment is tiring.

A charity you choose rather than the nearest bin is worth the extra trip, and many people find it is the part that makes the rest bearable.

Someone who will wear them. Offering items to friends and family is a real option, and most people are glad to be asked.

Something made from a few pieces is worth considering, if that appeals. A quilt, a cushion, a bear for a child. It is not for everyone, and the ones who want it tend to know right away.

And some things can simply go in the trash, including things that were loved. Worn out is worn out, and nothing is owed to a sweater with no elbows.

When it is more than a hard day

Grief that includes a job like this is ordinarily heavy, and most people come out the other side of it with support from the people around them.

It is worth taking to somebody if you have been unable to go near it for a long time and that is causing you real trouble, if the grief has not shifted at all in many months, if you are not sleeping or eating, or if the thought of the closet brings on something closer to panic than sadness. A therapist who works with bereavement does exactly this, your doctor can point you toward local services and many of them are free, and none of it means you are grieving wrongly. If it has gone further than that, and you are having thoughts of not being here, tell somebody today rather than waiting. In the US you can call or text 988 at any time.
