Cooling Down When a Feeling Peaks

At the top of a wave of panic or rage, nothing you think helps. A few physical moves change your body fast enough to get you back to being able to choose.

DBTBody Visitor 930 words

There is a point in a strong feeling where thinking stops working. You know the technique, you know the reframe, and none of it will load. You are at the top of the wave and all the advice is for the shore.

For those few minutes, the only reliable lever is physical. You change what your body is doing, your body changes the chemistry, and a minute or two later you are back to being someone who can choose what to do next.

These are the standard moves from dialectical behavior therapy, a practical kind of therapy built around skills for exactly this moment, and they work for panic, rage, overwhelming urges and the kind of distress that makes people do something they regret.

Cold, first

Cold on the face is the fastest of them. Splash cold water, hold a cold wet cloth over your eyes and upper cheeks, or hold a cold pack there for thirty seconds while you breathe slowly.

Cold water on the face, especially with your breath held briefly and your body leaned forward, triggers a reflex that slows your heart rate. It is the closest thing to an off switch that exists for a spike, and it works in under a minute.

If that is not available, cold anywhere helps: hands under a cold tap, an ice cube held in your palm, a cold drink, stepping outside in cool air. It is also entirely discreet, which matters when you are not alone.

A caution: if you have a heart condition, are pregnant, or are on medication that affects your heart rate, ask your doctor before using the strong cold version, because it genuinely does change your heart rate.

Then burn some of it off

Strong feelings arrive with energy for action. If you do not spend it, it keeps looking for an outlet.

So move hard for a short stretch. Run up a flight of stairs. Walk fast around the block. Twenty seconds of anything strenuous: jumping, press-ups against a counter, carrying something heavy across a room.

Short and intense is the aim, not a workout. Ten or fifteen minutes of brisk movement takes most of the edge off, and even one minute helps.

Tense and release

If you cannot leave the room, use your own muscles.

Press your palms hard together for ten seconds, then let go completely. Push against a wall. Grip the arms of a chair, hold, release. Clench your fists and your shoulders, hold for a count of five, and drop everything.

Then work through the body quickly: feet, legs, stomach, hands, shoulders, jaw. Tense, hold, release. The release is where the change happens, and the contrast makes it easier to actually let go rather than just trying to relax.

Slow the breath, and slow the out-breath most

Breathing is the one everyone knows and most people do wrong under pressure.

Do not take a big breath in. Breathe out longer than you breathe in: four in, six or seven out, and let the out-breath be slow and loose rather than forced. Ten rounds.

Pair it with a slower pace overall. Dropping your shoulders, unclenching your jaw and slowing your movements all send the same signal, and a body that has stopped bracing is a body coming down.

What to do with the two minutes you have bought

None of this solves anything. It buys you back the ability to decide, which is the thing that was missing.

So use it deliberately. Do not send the message. Do not have the conversation yet. Drink some water, sit down, and ask the plain question: what do I want to do in the next ten minutes, and will I be glad of it tomorrow.

If the feeling is still high, repeat the cold and the breathing. Most people need two rounds.

Practice them when you are calm

This is the part everyone skips and it is what decides whether you can use any of it.

A skill you have never tried will not arrive in a crisis. So run through the cold and the breathing a few times on an ordinary afternoon, until your hands know where to go.

It also helps to write a short list on your phone or a card: cold water, stairs, breathe out long. At the top of a wave, reading a list is much easier than remembering one.

Where these are not enough

These moves are for the peak. They are not a treatment, and using them several times a day every day is a sign that something upstream needs attention rather than more skills.

They are also not the answer if you are unsafe. If you are in danger from someone, the right move is getting away and getting help, not regulating yourself to tolerate it.

And if what peaks is an urge to hurt yourself, please treat that as a reason to tell someone today rather than something to ride out alone each time.

When to bring in help

Repeated spikes of panic, rage or distress that take over your day respond well to treatment, and the skills above are the first part of a longer kit a therapist can help you build. Dialectical behavior therapy in particular is designed for exactly this, and a doctor can also check whether anything physical, including sleep, pain, alcohol or medication, is making the spikes sharper.

If you are frightened by how strong these moments are, or by what you do in them, please talk to a doctor or a therapist rather than managing them on your own.
