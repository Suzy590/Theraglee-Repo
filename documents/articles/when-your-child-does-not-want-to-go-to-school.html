When Your Child Does Not Want to Go to School

Stomach aches at breakfast, tears at the gate, or a flat refusal to get in the car. What is usually going on, and what actually helps mornings get easier.

AnxietyParenting Visitor 957 words

It starts with a stomach ache on Monday. By Thursday there are tears at the gate, and by the following week you are standing in the hallway at eight fifteen negotiating with a child who will not put their shoes on.

This is one of the most stressful situations in family life, partly because it happens at the worst hour of the day and partly because every option feels wrong: pushing them in feels cruel, keeping them home feels like the start of something.

Rule out the obvious things first

Before treating it as anxiety, check what might be underneath.

Is something happening at school? Bullying, a friendship that collapsed, a teacher they are frightened of, work that has become too hard, something that happened in a changing room or a bathroom. Children often report none of this directly and all of it sideways.

Is there a physical explanation? Vision, hearing, sleep, constipation, headaches, an undiagnosed difficulty with reading or attention. Children who cannot keep up often become children who cannot bear to go.

Has something changed at home? A separation, a new baby, an illness, a move, a parent under strain. Reluctance to leave the house is often about what they are leaving rather than where they are going.

Ask about all of it, in a low-key way, not at eight fifteen.

What the stomach aches usually are

Most school refusal that is not about a specific event is anxiety, and anxiety in children is largely physical.

Stomach pain, nausea, headaches, needing the toilet, feeling hot. Those are real sensations produced by a real alarm response, not performances, and telling a child there is nothing wrong with them rarely lands because something plainly is.

The useful framing, for them and for you, is: your body is doing the frightened thing, and it is uncomfortable, and it is not dangerous.

Why avoidance makes it worse

Here is the mechanism that matters most. Each morning they stay home, the relief is enormous and immediate, and that relief teaches the alarm that school is genuinely dangerous and that staying home is the escape.

So the following morning is harder, not easier. A week off is often what turns a difficult couple of weeks into a months-long problem.

That is why the general principle, for ordinary anxiety with no safeguarding concern, is that going in some form beats staying home, even a reduced form.

Make going smaller rather than optional

The choice is not all-or-nothing, and partial attendance is far better than none.

Going in for two lessons. Going in late, after the crowded entrance. Spending the first twenty minutes in the office or with a particular teacher. Being collected at lunch for a few days. Sitting in the library during the lesson they dread.

Agree a specific, written plan with the school, with a date to review it. Most schools have seen this many times and have a point of contact for exactly it; asking what you do for children who are finding mornings hard usually produces more than you expect.

The morning itself

Keep it short, warm and matter-of-fact. The longer the negotiation, the worse the outcome, because the discussion itself becomes part of the anxiety.

Decide the night before: clothes out, bag packed, breakfast known. Reduce the number of decisions in the hour.

Do not interrogate how they are feeling at breakfast; it tends to turn attention inward and raise the volume. Talk about anything else.

At the gate, keep the handover brief. A long, tearful goodbye prolongs the hardest moment. Say the same short line every day: I know this is hard, you can do it, I will be here at three.

And if another adult can do the drop-off, let them. It is often dramatically easier with someone who is not the person they want to stay with.

What to say and what to avoid

Useful: your body feels awful and it will settle once you are in. You do not have to feel brave. I will always come back. Let us see what the first lesson is like.

Less useful: there is nothing to worry about, which contradicts what they feel. Everyone else manages, which adds shame. And promises you cannot keep, like you can come home if you hate it, unless that is genuinely the agreed plan.

Notice too how much of your own fear is in the room. Children read their parents closely, and a parent who is visibly dreading eight fifteen confirms that something dreadful is happening.

Afterward, point at the evidence

When they come home having managed it, say what happened plainly: you went in, it was hard at the start, and you got through the day. That is the sentence that does the long-term work, and it needs saying out loud rather than being assumed.

Keep it proportionate. Enormous celebration can make going to school feel like a feat, which is its own kind of pressure.

When to take it further

Please involve your child's doctor or school if this has gone on more than a couple of weeks, if they are missing whole days, if there are physical symptoms you have not had checked, or if their mood has dropped alongside it.

Anxiety in children responds very well to treatment, and the earlier it is addressed the shorter the work tends to be. A school counselor, a pediatrician, or a therapist who works with children are all reasonable starting points.

And treat it as urgent rather than ordinary if your child is being hurt or threatened at school, if they are harming themselves, or if they talk about not wanting to be here. In those cases speak to a doctor the same day rather than working on mornings.
