When Being Close Makes You Go Somewhere Else

Closeness is welcome and then suddenly you are watching from a distance, numb and far from your own body. Why intimacy can trigger that, what to say about it, and how to come back slowly.

BodyTrauma Visitor 941 words

You want to be close to this person. You chose this. And then, somewhere in it, you are not there anymore. You are a few feet above yourself, or behind your own eyes, watching. Your body keeps going. You answer, you move, you may even say the right things, and inside there is a flat quiet and a long distance.

Afterward there is usually a particular kind of loneliness, and often shame. You may feel you faked something, or that you let the other person down, or that something is broken in you. None of those conclusions is correct, and this is far more common than the silence around it suggests.

Why closeness, of all things

The going-away is a protection. It is the same mechanism that takes people out of car crashes and hospital rooms and arguments: when something is too much to be fully present for, the mind steps back and turns the volume down.

Closeness can set it off for several reasons, and more than one may be true for you.

It asks you to be undefended. Being physically close with someone means lowering the guard you keep up everywhere else, and a system that learned to stay guarded for good reasons does not always agree to that just because you have.

It involves the body, and the body keeps its own records. Touch, position, smell, a particular weight or warmth can be so specific that something old gets triggered in the background without any memory arriving in words. You can go numb without knowing why, and then feel irrational for it, when in fact something very logical just happened.

It has history for a lot of people. If closeness was ever unsafe, coerced, or came with obligation, then stepping out of your body may have been exactly how you got through it. That ability was useful and it does not retire when your circumstances improve.

And sometimes the cause is more ordinary: exhaustion, a long stretch of stress, grief, feeling evaluated, or being unsure whether you want to be there at all. Going away can be the body's way of saying something your words have not yet reached.

Stop pushing through

The most common instinct is to carry on and hope you come back. This teaches your system something unhelpful: that going away is the cost of closeness and nobody is going to stop. Over time, that makes it happen earlier and more easily.

Pausing is not a rejection of the other person. It is the thing that eventually makes closeness possible without leaving.

Say something plain

You do not have to explain your whole history, and not in the moment. A short, true sentence is enough.

I need to stop for a minute. I am going far away and I want to be here.

Can we just lie still. I am a bit disconnected and I need a moment.

This is not about you. My body does this sometimes and I am coming back.

It helps enormously to have had this conversation beforehand, in daylight, dressed, over coffee. Tell the person what happens, tell them what it looks like from outside, agree on what you will say and what they should do, which is usually: slow down, stay nearby, talk to me normally, do not interrogate me about it right now.

Come back through the senses, gently

Thinking does not get you back; sensation does. Sit up. Put your feet on the floor and press down. Something cool against your wrists or your face. A blanket with weight to it. Look around the room and name three objects out loud. Say the day and the year.

Then let the ordinary world back in: a lamp on, a glass of water, talking about something unremarkable for a few minutes. Coming back is usually gradual, and it goes faster when nobody, including you, is demanding an explanation yet.

Go slower than feels necessary

If this happens often, the useful direction is more contact with less intensity rather than the reverse. Time where closeness is the whole thing and nothing is building toward anything. Sitting against each other. Hands. Talking in the dark. Staying present in small, undemanding amounts teaches your system something new, and it is the part most people skip.

Pay attention to what comes before it, too. Being very tired, having drunk more than a little, arguing earlier in the evening, feeling obliged: each of these makes going away much more likely.

Take the shame off it

Dissociating during closeness is not dishonesty, and it is not a failure of love. It is a nervous system doing an old job at a moment you did not pick. You can grieve the fact that it happens and still be entirely reasonable about why it does.

If it gets very heavy

For some people this comes with memories arriving, or with a long stretch of feeling unreal and detached afterward, or a sense that it would not matter what happened to them. If thoughts like that are around, please tell someone today. In the US you can call or text 988 at any time.

When to get help

Please talk to a therapist who works with trauma and with intimacy, especially if this happens most of the time, if it began after a specific experience, or if it is hurting a relationship that matters to you. A doctor is worth seeing too, since pain, medication, and hormonal changes can all play a part. This is ordinary territory for a professional who does this work, and it is a problem with known routes out, not a fact about you.

If you are in crisis, call or text 988 (Suicide & Crisis Lifeline, US) or your local emergency number. Theraglee is a wellbeing tool, not a substitute for professional care.
