What If It Really Is as Bad as You Think

Reassurance does not touch some worries. The alternative is to follow the fear to the end on purpose and ask what you would actually do.

CBTWorry Visitor 951 words

You are worrying about the thing again. Someone has told you it will probably be fine, and the reassurance lasted about ninety seconds, because your worry was never asking for odds. It was asking whether you could survive the outcome.

For that kind of worry, there is a move that sounds backward and works much better than reassurance: instead of arguing that it will not happen, you follow it all the way to the end and ask what you would do.

Why reassurance bounces off

Most worry is not a mistake about probability. It is a belief about your capacity.

So telling someone the scan is probably clear, or that nobody will notice, addresses a question they were not really asking. The real question is: and if it is not, what then? Unanswered, that question keeps the worry running regardless of the odds.

Reassurance also has a short half-life, and seeking it repeatedly teaches you that you cannot settle your own mind without someone else. That is why the fourth reassurance works less well than the first.

Follow it down, one step at a time

Take the worry and keep asking the same question: and then what would happen?

Say the fear is a bad review at work. And then what? My manager thinks I am not good enough. And then what? I might be managed out. And then what? I would have to find another job. And then what? It would take a few months, money would be tight, I would have to tell people.

Keep going until you reach the bottom, which is usually not an event but a feeling: I would be humiliated. I would be alone. I would not cope.

That bottom is the actual fear, and it is almost never the thing you thought you were worrying about.

Then ask the useful question

Not how likely is it, but: if that happened, what would I do?

Specifically. Who would I call. What would the first week look like. What money would I have. What help exists. What have I survived before that was comparable.

Most people find two things here. The plan is shorter and more obvious than they expected. And the bottom of the fear turns out to be something they have already lived through a version of.

Write it down. A worry answered in your head returns tomorrow; a worry answered on paper can be reread.

Where it goes wrong, and how to do it well

This can turn into rumination if you are not careful, so two rules.

Do it once, on paper, with an end. Not in bed, not in circles. Fifteen minutes, written, finished.

And keep moving down rather than sideways. The trap is branching into every possible version: but what if it is this instead, or that. Pick the version that frightens you most, follow that one to the bottom, and ignore the branches. The bottom is usually the same whichever branch you take, which is itself useful to discover.

If you find yourself feeling worse rather than steadier, stop and come back to it with someone else.

What to do about the part that is real

Sometimes following it down reveals something you can actually act on, which is good news badly disguised.

If the bottom is money, the action is knowing your numbers and what help exists. If it is a medical fear, the action is the appointment. If it is losing the job, the action might be updating your resume and keeping one contact warm.

A small amount of real preparation does more for a worry than hours of argument. And notice the difference between preparing, which has a finish, and checking, which does not.

Also: some worries deserve to be taken to someone. If the honest answer is I genuinely could not manage that alone, then the next step is working out who would be there, and asking them now rather than in the emergency.

The part you have to leave uncertain

Some of it cannot be settled, and pretending otherwise is what keeps people searching for a better reassurance.

The version that holds is not it will be fine. It is: I do not know how this goes, and here is what I would do if it went badly. That sentence is boring, accurate, and liveable, which is more than certainty ever manages.

You can also say the uncertain part out loud rather than resolving it: I might not get the result I want. People often find that saying the feared sentence plainly takes more charge out of it than any amount of being told it will not happen.

For the things that cannot be prepared for

Some fears have no plan available: a child's safety, a diagnosis, something far outside your control.

For those, the honest answer is that preparation does not apply, and the work is different: putting the worry in one time slot a day rather than all of them, keeping your life populated with other things, and letting the uncertainty be uncertain.

That is harder and it is still a skill rather than a mood.

When to get help with it

If worry has taken over most of your day, if you cannot stop asking people for reassurance, if the physical knot never fully goes, or if following a fear to the end leaves you panicky rather than calmer, that is worth treating rather than managing.

This kind of worry responds well to cognitive behavioral therapy, often in a fairly small number of sessions, and a doctor or a therapist is the right place to start. Take your written list; it is more useful than trying to describe it from memory.

If you are in crisis, call or text 988 (Suicide & Crisis Lifeline, US) or your local emergency number. Theraglee is a wellbeing tool, not a substitute for professional care.
