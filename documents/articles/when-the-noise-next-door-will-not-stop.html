When the Noise Next Door Will Not Stop

Noise you cannot control costs more than sleep. Why it makes you so angry, what to do while it is happening, and the conversation with the neighbor that usually goes better than you expect.

StressAnger Visitor 954 words

It starts at eleven. Or it is the bass through the floor, or the dog, or the renovation that was supposed to take three weeks, or a television loud enough to follow the plot through a wall.

You are lying there listening to it, which is the worst possible activity, and the anger you feel is out of all proportion to the volume. That proportion is the part worth understanding, because it is not really about how loud the sound is.

Why noise you cannot control is so hard

The same sound is tolerable when you chose it and intolerable when you did not. That is not weakness. Control over a sound is most of what determines whether it bothers you.

Noise you cannot predict is worse than noise that is constant. A steady hum fades. A sound that might start again in the next ten minutes keeps your attention on it, because part of you stays on duty waiting.

At night it is worse again, because there is nothing else to put your attention on and the stakes are higher. Every minute you are awake, you are also calculating how bad tomorrow is going to be.

Then there is the other person. A noise with a human being behind it carries a question about whether they care, and that question is what turns irritation into something much hotter.

The listening is making it worse

This is the practical heart of it. You cannot make them stop tonight, and you can change what you do while it happens.

Lying in the dark tracking the sound is the single most costly thing you can do, because it trains your attention onto it. The goal is not to stop hearing it. It is to stop monitoring it.

Give the sound competition rather than silence. Steady noise of your own, a fan, a recording of rain, something tedious and continuous, works better than earplugs alone, because it takes away the gaps where you listen for the next one.

Earplugs do help, and most people give up on them too early. The cheap foam ones take about a week to get used to and are better than most of what is sold as a solution.

Move where you sleep if you can, even temporarily. People resist this because it feels like losing, and a week of sleep is worth more than the principle.

And put a limit on the time you spend thinking about it when it is quiet. The daytime rehearsing is a real cost and it is entirely yours to cut.

Talking to them, which usually goes better than expected

Most people put this off for months, and most of the time the conversation is far less dramatic than the version they imagined.

Go early, before you are furious. The conversation you have at week two is a different conversation from the one at month six, and the second one tends to go badly because the anger is audible.

Lead with information, not accusation. I can hear the television through the bedroom wall after eleven, I do not think you know that, is the version that works. People genuinely do not know what carries.

Ask for something specific and small. After eleven, or on weeknights, or the speakers off the floor. A vague request to be quieter gives nobody anything to do.

Say something ordinary and friendly afterward, whenever you next see them. The relationship is the thing that keeps it fixed, and a neighbor you are on terms with is much easier to ask twice.

And go in person rather than leaving a note. Notes read colder than anyone intends, and they usually produce resentment instead of a change.

If it does not stop

Keep a plain record, with dates and times. It is dull and it is what every landlord, management company and local office will ask for first.

Find out who actually has authority before you spend energy. A landlord, a condo board, a local noise office. Complaining energetically to the wrong party is a common way to burn out.

And decide what you are willing to do, and what you are not. Some of these do not get solved, and the honest question becomes whether you can insulate, rearrange, or eventually move. That is a miserable conclusion and it is better than two more years of waiting for someone else to change.

Looking after the anger itself

Even once it is handled, the anger tends to outlast the noise, and it is worth treating separately.

Notice when you have started hearing it when it is not there. Most people in this situation get a stretch of that, and it eases once the alertness comes down.

Get out of the apartment on purpose, regularly. Being somewhere else resets things in a way that nothing you do at home can.

And be careful with the story. He knows and he does not care is usually unprovable, and it is the sentence that does the most damage to you. Keeping it to the facts of the sound keeps the anger a manageable size.

When it is worth more help

If you are not sleeping for weeks, if the anger is frightening you or spilling onto people who did not cause it, if you have stopped using rooms in your own home, or if dread of the evening has become the shape of your day, that is worth bringing to someone.

A therapist can help with the alertness and the anger, which is the part that stays after the noise is dealt with, and your doctor is the right person to tell if the sleep loss has gone on long enough to be affecting your health.
