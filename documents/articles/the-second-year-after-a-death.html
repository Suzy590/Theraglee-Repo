The Second Year After a Death

The casseroles stopped, the cards stopped, and everyone assumes you are through it. Why the second year is often harder than the first, and what helps in it.

GriefLife Transitions Visitor 973 words

The first year had markers in it. The funeral, the paperwork, the first birthday, the first Christmas, people checking on you. Everyone knew.

Now it is eighteen months and nobody mentions it. You are expected to be fine, and you are in some ways less fine than you were, and there is nowhere to put that.

Why the second year can be worse

Several things change at once, and none of them get talked about.

The adrenaline goes. The first months are often spent in a kind of emergency state: arrangements, administration, people in the house. That carries you, badly, and when it drains away you are left with the actual absence.

The support thins out. It is not that people stopped caring; it is that a death moves out of the news, and they assume someone else is checking in, or that mentioning it would upset you.

And the permanence lands. In the first year some part of you is still half expecting them back. In the second, you have lived through a whole cycle of a year without them, and you know what every season looks like now.

That is why people often say the second year is quieter and heavier, and why so many of them think something has gone wrong with them.

Nothing has gone wrong with you

Grief is not a ladder you climb. It comes in waves that get further apart and can stay just as high.

So a Tuesday in the eighteenth month can be as bad as week two, set off by a smell, a song, an anniversary you had not thought about, or by nothing. That is normal and it does not mean you are going backward.

Expect it to keep showing up in new places. The first time you need their advice. The first family event where their chair is filled by someone else. The moment you notice you have forgotten the sound of their voice, which frightens people more than almost anything.

Say it out loud, to someone

The silence around the second year is the part you can actually change.

Most people around you have simply assumed the subject is closed. Telling one or two of them plainly — this year is harder than last year, I still want to talk about him — usually produces more than you expect, because they were waiting for a signal.

Say their name in ordinary conversation. Keep telling the stories. The pressure to stop referring to someone is almost entirely imagined, and the people who loved them usually want to hear it.

If talking to family is complicated, a bereavement group is a reasonable alternative. Everyone there is in the same position and nobody needs it explained.

Rebuild the week deliberately

A large part of the second year is practical, and it is nobody's favorite subject: the structure that the person provided is gone and has to be replaced on purpose.

If they were your company in the evenings, the evenings need something in them. If they organized the social life, you now do that, badly at first. If they were the one who cooked, or drove, or handled the money, those are skills to pick up rather than failures of character.

Start with two fixed things a week that involve other people, and treat them as appointments. Volunteering, a class, a regular walk, a standing phone call. Not as a replacement, which is impossible, but as a floor to stand on.

And be careful with the house. Sitting alone in the same rooms for months is the thing most likely to turn grief into something heavier, and leaving once a day is a small, dull, effective intervention.

Let the relationship change rather than end

People often feel they must choose between holding on and moving on. In practice most people find a third thing.

Keep a place for them: the photograph, the ritual on the date, the recipe, the garden, the charity, telling their jokes. Some people write to them, or talk to them in the car, and find it steadying rather than strange.

And let your life grow around that rather than instead of it. Enjoying something is not a betrayal, and neither is a good week. Guilt about laughing is extremely common and it is not information about your loyalty.

Be ready for the second-year decisions

This is often when the big choices arrive: the house, the move, the relationship, the job, what to do with the money.

Where you can, give them time. Decisions made in the first year are often made by somebody in shock; the second year is better, and it is still worth talking each one through with somebody who is not inside it.

And expect other people to have opinions about the pace of your life, particularly about dating, selling, or clearing things out. That pace is yours.

When to get help with it

Please talk to a doctor or a therapist if you are no further on than you were a year ago, if you cannot think about anything else, if you have been unable to work or look after yourself, or if you are drinking more to get through the evenings.

Grief does not have a schedule, but it should be changing shape over time; when it stays frozen, there is specific help for that, and bereavement services will often see you without a referral, two years on or ten. Say how long it has been and what you cannot do at the moment, because that is what tells them which kind of support fits.

And if it has taken you somewhere dark, and you are not sure you want to be here, please tell a doctor or someone you trust today. In the US you can call or text 988 at any time.

If you are in crisis, call or text 988 (Suicide & Crisis Lifeline, US) or your local emergency number. Theraglee is a wellbeing tool, not a substitute for professional care.
