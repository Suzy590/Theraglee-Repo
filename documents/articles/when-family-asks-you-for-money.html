When Family Asks You for Money

A relative needs help and you have some. How to decide without being bullied or guilted, how to say no or yes clearly, and how to keep the relationship either way.

MoneyFamily Visitor 886 words

It comes by phone, or in a message, or at the end of a visit when everyone else has gone. They are behind on rent, or the car has died, or the business needs a few weeks. And they are family, so the ordinary rules about money do not seem to apply.

What makes this hard is not arithmetic. It is that the answer feels like a statement about whether you love them.

Buy yourself time before anything else

Almost nobody makes a good decision on the spot, and the pressure to answer immediately is often part of how the request works.

So have a sentence ready. I need to think about it and talk to my partner. I will call you tomorrow.

That is not a brush-off and it is reasonable in any family. If the answer must be now or it is no good, that is useful information on its own, because genuine emergencies survive a night.

Work out what you can actually give away

The honest question is not can I afford it but what happens to me if this money never comes back.

Treat anything you hand over as a gift for the purposes of deciding, even if it is called a loan, because most family loans are not repaid on schedule and a fair number are never repaid at all. If the amount would hurt you in that case, it is the wrong amount.

Then set the figure before you talk again. I can give you four hundred is a much easier conversation than an open question about how much you need.

Decide what kind of help it is, and say so

Three different things get muddled together, and naming which one you are offering prevents most of the later resentment.

A gift, which you will not ask about again, and which they do not owe you.

A loan, with a written plan: how much, when, how. Not a contract to take to court, just a message you both have, because memories about money diverge fast.

Help that is not money: a lift, a bed for a month, finding the right advice line, going with them to the bank, covering one specific bill directly rather than handing over cash.

The third kind is often more useful than the second and ruins fewer relationships.

How to say no and keep the person

A clear no, delivered warmly and once, lands better than a vague maybe that drags on.

I am not able to lend money. I am sorry. I love you and I will help in other ways.

Then do not negotiate your reasons. Reasons invite debate, and the debate is where people get worn down. Repeat the same sentence if you need to, and change the subject afterward.

You are also allowed a no with no reason at all. Family money requests often come with an implied right to inspect your finances, and that right does not exist.

Expect the pressure, and recognize it

Some requests come with leverage: reminders of what they did for you, a suggestion that you have forgotten where you came from, anger, or going quiet for a month.

That is worth seeing clearly for what it is rather than reading it as evidence you were wrong. A reasonable request can be refused and the relationship survives. If a refusal reliably produces punishment, the problem in that relationship is bigger than this one decision.

When it is a pattern rather than an emergency

One hard year is one thing. The fourth request in two years is another, and the kind thing then is usually not more money.

That is the moment for a different conversation: what is actually going on with your money, and would you let me help you get proper advice. Free debt advice exists in most countries and does far more than a few hundred ever will. Handing over money repeatedly can quietly keep a situation going that needs to change.

Be especially careful with a few situations

Co-signing or guaranteeing a loan is not lending money, it is taking on the whole debt if they do not pay, and people agree to it without understanding that.

Money that is going toward gambling, addiction or someone else's control over them deserves a different response from a check, and that is worth talking through with a professional rather than deciding alone.

And if an older relative is being asked for money by family, or is suddenly short of it, that can be financial abuse, which is common and rarely reported.

Talk to anyone else it affects

If you share money with a partner, decide together before you answer, or you will have bought a problem at home as well. Being able to say we decided together is also a useful shield.

When to get help

If a family member's money troubles are frightening you, keeping you awake or taking over your relationships, please talk to a free debt or benefits adviser about the practical side and to a counselor or therapist about the rest, since guilt, obligation and family loyalty are exactly what they work with. If you think an older or vulnerable relative is being pressured for money, ask their doctor or local social services about safeguarding, and say plainly what you have seen.

If you are in crisis, call or text 988 (Suicide & Crisis Lifeline, US) or your local emergency number. Theraglee is a wellbeing tool, not a substitute for professional care.
