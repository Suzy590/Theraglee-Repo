Drinking More Than You Meant To

It is not dramatic. It is one glass becoming three most nights, and a quiet unease about it. How to look at your drinking honestly, without a label, and what to change first.

HabitsShame Visitor 882 words

Nothing has gone badly wrong. You hold down your job, nobody has staged an intervention, and by any dramatic standard you are fine. But you have noticed that the one glass while cooking has become three by bedtime, most nights. You have noticed that you plan around it a little. You have had the thought, more than once, that you would like to cut back, and then not cut back.

That quiet unease is worth taking seriously, precisely because nothing dramatic has happened. Most people who change their drinking do it at this stage, not after a catastrophe.

Drop the labels for a minute

A lot of people avoid looking at this because the only frame on offer seems to be a binary: either you have a serious problem or there is nothing to discuss. That framing keeps people stuck, because most drinking sits on a wide range, and you do not need to accept any label to decide you would like less of it.

The only question that matters is this one: is alcohol giving me more than it is costing me, honestly?

Look at what it is doing for you

This is the most useful question, and it is not rhetorical. Alcohol is doing something, or you would not keep reaching for it. Usually one of a few things.

Ending the day. It is the switch between work and home, the signal that the shift is over. For many people this is the whole of it.

Turning the volume down. On anxiety, on a racing mind, on physical tension. It works quickly, which is why it is hard to replace.

Getting through people. Social events, family, anything where being comfortable is difficult.

Not feeling something. Grief, loneliness, boredom, a situation you have not dealt with.

Whatever yours is, that need is real, and it will need another answer, because taking away the drink without meeting the need rarely lasts.

Look at the cost, plainly

Sleep, first. Alcohol puts you to sleep and then fragments the second half of the night, which is why you wake at four and why you are tired regardless of hours in bed.

Mood, second. It reliably raises anxiety the following day, which increases the reason to drink the following evening. A great many people who think they have an anxiety problem plus a drinking habit find the anxiety drops substantially a few weeks after the drinking does.

Then the quieter things: the evenings you do not fully remember, the conversations you were not really present for, the money, the shame that follows you around the next morning and that you cannot quite name.

Get the actual numbers

Estimates are always low. For two weeks, write down every drink, with the real measure rather than the polite one, and the time. Do not change anything yet. Just count.

Almost everyone is surprised. That surprise is the most useful piece of information you will get, and it is much more persuasive than anything anyone else could tell you.

Change one thing first

Not everything. Pick one: no drinking on weeknights, or nothing before eight, or two drinks maximum with the count written down, or three alcohol-free days a week that you choose in advance.

Then notice what happens, and especially notice how hard it is. If a modest limit turns out to be very difficult to keep, that is not a failure; it is important information about how much of a grip the habit has.

Replace the ritual, not just the drink

The glass is often a ritual more than a substance: the sound, the pause, the marker of an ending. Replace the ritual and the change gets much easier. Something in a proper glass. A shower at the end of the day. A walk at six. The first ten minutes of something you look forward to.

Expect the evenings to feel strange

The first couple of weeks of drinking less are often flat and restless, and sleep can be worse before it is better. That is temporary and it is the part where most attempts stop. Knowing it is coming helps.

Tell one person

Shame keeps this private, and private habits are much harder to change. Telling one person what you are trying, and asking them to check in, roughly doubles the odds of it sticking. It also breaks the loop where drinking creates shame which makes you want a drink.

When to get real help

Please talk to a doctor if you drink daily and heavily, if you have ever felt shaky, sweaty, or sick when you stopped, or if you drink first thing. Stopping suddenly after heavy daily use can be genuinely dangerous, and it needs medical supervision rather than willpower.

And please talk to a doctor or a therapist if you have tried to cut back and could not, if drinking is affecting your work or the people you love, or if it is tangled up with low mood, anxiety, or something painful you are keeping at bay. There are effective treatments, including medications many people have never heard of, and support that looks nothing like what you may be imagining. Wanting less of something is a good enough reason to ask for help. You do not need to have lost anything first.

If you are in crisis, call or text 988 (Suicide & Crisis Lifeline, US) or your local emergency number. Theraglee is a wellbeing tool, not a substitute for professional care.
