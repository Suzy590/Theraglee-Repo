When You Look Back at Your Own Work and Cringe

You open the old file and your whole body contracts. Why the cringe is evidence of growth, what to keep and what to take down, and the version of it that stops you making things.

PerfectionismShame Visitor 834 words

You find the file. The thing you wrote three years ago, the photographs from the project you were proud of, the recording, the code, the painting. You open it expecting to feel something warm and instead your whole body contracts.

How did I think that was good. Who let me send that out.

The cringe is a sign of distance, not of failure

Here is the thing worth understanding first, because it reframes the whole experience.

You can only see what is wrong with that work because you know more now. The gap you are feeling is the distance you have traveled, measured in the only way it can be measured.

Which means the cringe is evidence of growth. Unpleasant evidence, arriving in the body, but evidence.

And the opposite would be worse. If your work from three years ago still looked perfect to you, that would mean three years of nothing.

Separate the work from the person

The cringe usually says two things at once, and only one of them is true.

The true part: that work had real weaknesses, and I can see them now.

The part that sneaks in: and therefore I am an embarrassment, and everyone who saw it thought so.

The second does not follow. Work has levels. People do not. Practice saying the first without the second, which is most of the skill here.

Remember what you could actually see at the time

You are judging past work with present eyes, which is an unfair contest nobody can win.

So ask the fairer question: was that good for what I knew and had then. Usually it was. Often it was the best you could do that year, with that much skill, in that much time, under whatever was going on in your life.

That is not a consolation prize. It is the accurate standard, and it is the one you would apply to anybody else.

Know that almost nobody noticed

The particular agony of this is imagining everybody who saw it and judged it.

They did not, mostly. They read it, used it, enjoyed it or forgot it, and moved on within a day. The flaws that are enormous to you are invisible to people who were not inside the making of it.

And the people who did notice are generally kinder than you are. They have their own old work.

Decide what to do with it, deliberately

The cringe pushes toward deleting everything, and that is usually the wrong move in the morning.

So sort it. Things that are actively wrong or harmful, and still live where people find them: fix or take down. Things that are merely worse than you would do now: leave them. Things that are private: keep them, because they are a record.

Keeping some of it is worth more than you think. A folder of old work is the clearest proof you have that you improve, and it is the thing you will want on the day you are convinced you have not.

Use it rather than just enduring it

There is real information in the cringe, if you look at it once rather than flinching away.

So name the specific thing. Too long. Overexplained. Trying to sound clever. Afraid to make a claim. Copying somebody's style. Whatever it is, it is probably a habit you still have a trace of, and seeing it clearly in old work is the easiest place to catch it.

Then stop. One pass, one lesson, close the file. Rereading it eleven times is not analysis.

Watch for the version that stops you making things

Mild cringe is healthy. There is a version that does damage, and it is worth catching.

If looking back makes you unable to publish, send or show anything new, the standard has stopped being useful and started being a brake. If you have deleted work you now wish you had, that is a sign too. If the feeling is less I have improved and more I am a fraud who got away with it, that is a different problem from craft.

And notice the long view. People who keep making things describe cringing at their work for decades, in a cycle, while the work gets steadily better. The cringe is not the thing that goes away. The paralysis is.

When to get help

Please talk to a therapist if looking back at your own work reliably tips into feeling worthless rather than simply wincing, or if the fear of making something you will later cringe at is stopping you making anything at all, because that is perfectionism rather than standards and it responds well to treatment. It is also worth a conversation if the shame attached to old work or old mistakes goes far wider than the work, which usually means it connects to something older. And see your doctor if your mood has been low for weeks, since a low mood makes every backward look harsher than it needs to be.
