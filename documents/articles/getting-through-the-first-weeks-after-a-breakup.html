Getting Through the First Weeks After a Breakup

Whether you ended it or they did, the first weeks are a strange kind of grief. What to expect, what helps and what quietly makes it worse, and how to know when you need more support.

LossRelationships Visitor 901 words

The relationship is over. Maybe you saw it coming, maybe it blindsided you, maybe you were the one who said the words. Either way, you are now in the first weeks, and they are worse than anyone warned you. You cannot eat or cannot stop. You reach for your phone to tell them something and remember. The apartment is too quiet. A song comes on and you have to leave the store.

This is grief. Not a version of it, not something like it. The person is still alive, which makes it confusing, but what you have lost is real: a daily life, a future you had pictured, the person who knew you best. Treat it as the loss it is.

What the first weeks are like

Expect your mind to do strange things. It will replay the last conversation and rewrite it. It will swing between missing them and being furious with them, sometimes in the same hour. It will make a case for calling them at two in the morning. It will insist the whole thing was your fault, then that it was entirely theirs.

Expect your body to be affected too. Sleep gets broken. Appetite goes one way or the other. You may feel physically ill, heavy in the chest, unable to concentrate. Some people describe the early days as a kind of withdrawal, and that is close to right: your system was built around another person, and it is recalibrating.

None of this means you are handling it badly. It means you are handling it.

Cut the contact, at least for now

The hardest and most useful thing in the early weeks is distance. Mute or unfollow them. Do not check their pages. Do not text to see how they are. Every contact reopens the wound, and every glance at their life feeds the replaying. This is not about punishing them. It is about giving yourself a chance to stop the bleeding.

If you share children, a lease, or a workplace, keep the contact to what is necessary and practical, and keep it short.

Let it hurt

There is a strong pull to skip the pain: stay busy, drink more, jump into someone new, decide you are fine. Some of that is survival and it is okay in doses. But the pain does not go away when you refuse to feel it. It waits. Set aside some time, even ten minutes a day, to let it in. Cry. Write it down. Say it out loud to someone. Feelings that are allowed through tend to pass sooner than feelings that are held off.

Keep the basics going

Eat something at regular times, even if it is small. Get outside every day. Go to bed at roughly the same time. Shower. These sound too basic to mention, but in the first weeks they are the scaffolding that keeps you upright, and they are the first things to slip.

Lean on people, and tell them what you need

Say the words to someone: we broke up and I am not okay. Then be specific about what helps. Some people need to talk it through endlessly. Some need to be distracted. Some need company that does not require conversation. Your friends want to help and mostly do not know how. Tell them.

Do not make big decisions yet

Do not quit the job, move cities, get the tattoo, or send the long message explaining everything. The first weeks are not a time for irreversible choices. Write the message if you must. Then leave it in your drafts for a week. Almost every message left in drafts stays there.

Resist the urge to build a case

Your mind will want to reach a verdict: who was wrong, what it all meant, whether you wasted years. You do not have to decide any of that now. Most people's understanding of a relationship changes a lot over the months after it ends. Let the story stay unfinished for a while.

When you were the one who ended it

Leaving does not spare you the grief. It adds guilt on top. You can be certain it was right and still miss them, still cry, still doubt yourself at night. Both things are allowed. Ending something is not the same as not having loved it.

If you have thoughts of not wanting to be here

Breakups can bring a pain that feels unsurvivable, especially when they land on top of other losses or a low stretch. If you have thoughts of harming yourself or of not wanting to be alive, please tell someone today. In the US you can call or text 988 at any time.

When to reach out for more help

Most people begin to feel more like themselves within a few months, with good and bad days along the way. But if weeks have passed and you cannot function, if you are not eating or sleeping at all, if you are drinking or using to get through, if the relationship was frightening or controlling and you are struggling with what happened in it, or if the sadness has gone flat and hopeless, please talk to a therapist. A breakup is a real loss, and you are allowed to get help carrying it. You will not always feel like this. That is the one thing every person who has been here can tell you.

If you are in crisis, call or text 988 (Suicide & Crisis Lifeline, US) or your local emergency number. Theraglee is a wellbeing tool, not a substitute for professional care.
