Researching a Decision Instead of Making It

Six weeks of reviews, spreadsheets and forum threads, and still no decision. How to tell useful research from avoidance, and how to set the stopping rule before you start.

PerfectionismDecision Making Visitor 877 words

You have read everything. The comparison tables, the forum threads, the long review from the person who clearly knows what they are talking about. You have a spreadsheet.

And you have not decided, and it has been six weeks, and the problem you were solving is still there.

Research is the most respectable form of putting something off, because it looks exactly like diligence. The difference is worth learning to spot in yourself.

What the research is actually doing

Up to a point, information makes a decision easier. Past that point it does something else: it postpones the moment where you commit and could turn out to be wrong.

That is the real fear in most stuck decisions. Not picking badly, but being the person who picked badly, having had every chance to get it right.

So the reading becomes a way of staying in the comfortable place where the decision is still open and nothing can be your fault yet. It even feels productive, which is why it can go on for months.

The signs you have passed the useful point

A few are reliable.

You are rereading. The same review, the same thread, the same two options going round, and you are no longer learning anything new.

The sources have started to contradict each other and you are looking for the one that agrees with what you already want.

You have moved from the thing to the meta-thing: not which bike, but how to decide which bike. Not which job, but which framework to use on which job.

And the deadline keeps drifting quietly: another week, after the weekend, once this is out of the way.

If two or three of those are true, more reading is not what is missing.

Decide what would change your mind

This is the most useful question in a stalled decision: what piece of information would actually change my answer?

Often there is one, and it is specific and findable. Whether it fits in the space. What it costs to leave. Whether the hours are negotiable. Go and get that one thing and stop reading everything else.

And often, when you ask the question honestly, there is nothing. You already know what the options are and the differences between them are small or unknowable. That is not a research problem; it is a decision that needs making, and no amount of further reading will make it feel safe.

Set the stopping rule before you start

Decide the bounds up front, because they are impossible to set once you are deep in it.

An hour of reading, three options, and then I pick. A week to look, decide on Sunday. Whatever I know by Friday is what I will decide on.

Writing it down somewhere you will see it is worth more than you would think. So is telling one person, because a stopping point somebody else knows about is much harder to slide past.

Then at the end, pick with what you have. Not the perfect answer; the best answer available at the time you agreed to stop.

Sort the reversible from the rest

A lot of this effort goes into decisions that could simply be undone, and that is where the real waste is.

Things you can return, cancel, resign from, repaint, move again: decide those fast and badly on purpose. The cost of a wrong choice is a small amount of hassle, and it is almost always less than another three weeks of your attention.

Save the careful, slow version for the genuinely hard-to-reverse ones: the mortgage, the surgery, the move across the country, the business you will sign for. Those deserve the spreadsheet. Very little else does.

And notice which kind you are treating this as. Most people apply the big method to the small decisions and then have nothing left for the big ones.

Decide how you will live with it afterward

Half of the dread is about the aftermath rather than the choice, so settle that part in advance.

Agree with yourself that you will not keep rechecking once it is done: no going back to the reviews, no pricing the other option, no reading the thread again. Those feed the idea that the decision is still open when it is not.

And agree what you will do if it turns out to be wrong. Usually the honest answer is small: I would sell it, I would change again in a year, I would live with it and be mildly annoyed. Saying that out loud shrinks the whole thing, because the catastrophe you were protecting against turns out to be an inconvenience.

When it is bigger than this one decision

If this is how most choices go, if the stuckness has cost you money, opportunities or a relationship, or if the not-deciding is keeping you awake, it is worth looking at with someone rather than trying harder on your own.

Perfectionism, a fear of being judged, and a background of being criticized for getting things wrong all produce exactly this, and all of them are workable. A therapist is the right place to take it, and it is a common enough thing to bring that you will not have to explain why it matters.
