Finding Out Someone Earns More Than You

You learn what a colleague makes and the number will not leave you alone. How to separate the unfairness question from the sting, and what to actually do with the information.

MoneyWork Visitor 849 words

Somebody left a document open, or said it outright, or you worked it out. Now you know what they earn, and it is more than you, and the number has moved into your head and will not leave.

The information is useful. The way it arrives is not, because it usually turns up without any of the context that would make sense of it, and in the meantime it is doing something to how you feel about your work.

Why it stings out of proportion

Pay is treated as a score. Nobody says so, but it operates as a summary of how much you are valued, and finding out somebody scored higher reads as a judgment rather than a line in a budget.

It also arrives retroactively. The gap was probably there for a year or two, and now every month you worked happily feels differently in hindsight.

And it is usually incomplete. You rarely learn the whole picture at once, which leaves the mind to fill in the rest, and the mind fills it in with the least flattering explanation available.

Then there is the colleague. You now know something about them that they did not choose to tell you, and that quietly changes an ordinary working relationship.

Separating two different questions

Almost everybody mixes these up, and sorting them out is most of the work.

The first is whether something unfair is happening. That is a factual question about the same role, the same level, the same work, and about whether the difference tracks something legitimate.

The second is the sting, which is about being valued, and which does not go away even when the answer to the first question turns out to be reasonable.

Both are real. They just need different responses, and treating the sting as proof of unfairness is what leads people into a conversation they have not prepared for.

What legitimately explains a gap

Worth knowing, because some of these are ordinary and some are not.

When each of you was hired. Pay offered at a particular time tends to stick, and someone hired in a hot market is often ahead of someone who has been loyal for years. This is extremely common and it is usually about the employer's habits rather than anyone's worth.

Whether they negotiated. A large share of gaps come down to one conversation at the start that one person had and the other did not.

Different responsibilities that are not visible from where you sit, or a different level on paper even though the day looks similar.

Prior experience, a competing offer they had, or a specialty that is scarce.

And none of that applies when the work really is the same and the difference tracks something it should not, which does happen and is a different conversation.

What to do with the information

Find out your market rate before anything else. Not what your colleague earns, which is one data point and may itself be odd, but what the role pays elsewhere. That is the number that gives you standing.

Write down what you have done this year, specifically, with outcomes. Most people arrive at a pay conversation able to describe their job and unable to describe their contribution, which is the thing that moves numbers.

Ask about your own pay, not theirs. I would like to talk about my salary, here is what the role pays elsewhere, here is what I have delivered. That conversation can succeed. One that opens with what someone else earns usually turns into a conversation about how you found out.

Do not reveal how you learned it if it would land on the colleague. They did not do anything to you, and their position is often more awkward than yours.

And ask what it would take. If the answer is not now, the useful follow-up is what would have to be true, and when, written down.

If nothing changes

Give it a date rather than an open-ended wait. Six months, reviewed.

Decide what you will do if the answer stays no, before you are upset. Stay and stop giving the extra, look elsewhere, ask again at a specific point. Any of those is a position. Resentment without a plan is the one option that costs you and changes nothing.

And protect the relationship with the colleague, who is not the cause. Carrying the number into every interaction with them is a real risk and it only costs you.

When it is more than a pay question

If this has become the thing you think about most, if it has you feeling worthless rather than underpaid, or if a difference you believe is discriminatory is being dismissed, those are worth taking further.

A therapist can help with the part that has attached your sense of worth to a number, which is more common than people admit. And if the gap looks like it tracks who you are rather than what you do, that is worth raising with someone qualified to advise you, rather than carried alone or settled by a professional guess.
