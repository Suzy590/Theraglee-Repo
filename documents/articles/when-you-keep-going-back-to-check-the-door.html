When You Keep Going Back to Check the Door

The lock was locked and you still are not sure. Why going back to check makes the doubt worse rather than better, and the unglamorous method therapists use to shrink it.

AnxietyCBT Visitor 839 words

You locked it. You remember locking it. You are halfway down the street and the certainty has drained out, and now there is a choice between walking back or spending the morning with a low hum of doubt.

So you walk back. The door is locked. There is about four minutes of relief, and then the whole thing starts again tomorrow.

What checking actually does to certainty

This is the part worth understanding, because it is the opposite of what common sense predicts.

Checking feels like it should build confidence. One more look, and you will know. In practice, repeated checking makes the memory worse rather than better.

The reason is that the twentieth look at a lock is not a vivid experience. It is a blur of nearly identical moments, and a blur is hard to retrieve clearly later. The more times you have checked something, the less distinct any one of those checks becomes.

So you end up with a strong feeling of doubt and a weak memory to answer it with, which is exactly the combination that sends you back to the door.

Why certainty is the wrong target

Most people are trying to reach a feeling: I know it is locked. That feeling is not available on demand, and chasing it is what turns a ten-second task into a morning.

What is available is evidence. You turned the handle, you heard it, the door did not open. That is a fact, and it stays true whether or not it feels convincing.

The useful move is to accept the gap between the two. The door is locked and I do not feel sure is a complete and accurate sentence, and learning to leave the house inside it is most of the work.

Breaking the loop in practice

The method therapists use for this is unglamorous and it works. It is not willpower and it is not reassurance.

Check once, deliberately, and pay attention while you do it. One slow check with your attention on it is worth more than six fast ones, because it leaves a memory you can actually find later.

Then say what you did out loud. I locked the door at eight ten. A spoken sentence is far easier to retrieve than a wordless action.

Then leave, and do not go back. This is the hard instruction and it is the one that changes things. The doubt will rise, peak, and come down on its own within twenty minutes or so, and every time you let it do that without going back, the next one is slightly smaller.

Expect it to feel worse before it feels better. The first few times you walk on, the anxiety is higher than usual, because the thing that used to switch it off is no longer available. That spike is the process working, not a sign that you have done something reckless.

Things that look like help and are not

Taking a photograph of the lock. This works for about a week and then becomes the new thing you check, often repeatedly, and now you are checking a picture.

Asking someone else whether they locked it. The relief comes from the other person, which means the doubt now needs them, and that is a harder loop to climb out of than the original one.

Doing it a set number of times, or in a particular order. Rules like that feel like control and they add structure to the thing you are trying to shrink.

And lying awake running back through the evening to find the moment you locked it. The memory search is checking in a different form, and it has the same effect.

What makes it worse, reliably

Tiredness, because a tired brain lays down weaker memories and you will have less to work with.

Doing it on autopilot while talking or carrying things, which is why so much of this happens at the front door with a bag in each hand.

Rushing, which guarantees an unmemorable check.

And stress in general. Checking tends to climb during a hard month and ease off afterward, which is worth knowing so you do not read a bad week as a permanent change.

When to take it to someone

A certain amount of this is ordinary and most people do some version of it.

It is worth talking to a professional when it is costing you time you cannot spare, when you are late or avoiding leaving, when the checking has spread to the stove, the windows, the car and the faucets, or when the doubt arrives with a frightening picture of what would happen attached to it.

That last one in particular responds well to treatment, and the treatment is practical and usually short: a therapist helps you cut the checking down in steps while the doubt is allowed to be there, and the certainty you were chasing turns out not to have been necessary. Your doctor can refer you, and it is a common reason to ask.
