When a Coworker Takes Up All Your Time

The chats, the questions, the work that drifts onto your desk. How to get your hours back from a colleague without a confrontation or a bad atmosphere.

BoundariesWork Visitor 973 words

They are at your desk again. It will be forty minutes, and about six of those will be about work. You like them, more or less. You also have not had an uninterrupted hour since Monday.

Or it is not chatting. It is the questions they could answer themselves, the tasks that drift onto your desk because you are easier to ask than the right person, the meeting invites with no agenda.

This is one of the most awkward problems at work, because nothing has gone wrong exactly, and the usual advice assumes you are willing to have a confrontation you would rather avoid.

The cost is not the time, it is the fragmentation

An interruption costs much more than its length. It takes the forty minutes, and it takes the time to get back into what you were doing, and it takes the quality of the hour that follows.

Which is why a day of pleasant interruptions can leave you with nothing done and a vague sense of having worked hard. Your hours were full; none of them were yours.

It also means the aim is not to reduce the chat by half. It is to get a few protected stretches, which is a different and more achievable goal.

Change the structure before the conversation

The easiest fixes do not involve telling anyone anything.

Block two hours in your calendar, named something official, and treat it like a meeting. Work somewhere else for part of the day, or put headphones on as a visible signal. Set your messaging status and let a reply wait twenty minutes rather than arriving instantly.

Instant availability is what trains an interruption habit. A twenty-minute lag trains people to batch their questions, and most of them answer themselves in the meantime.

The sentences that end an interruption kindly

You do not need a speech. You need two or three lines you can say without thinking, warmly and every time.

I am mid-something, can I come find you at four. Give me twenty minutes and I am yours. I have got ten minutes now, or half an hour tomorrow, which is better.

Note what all of these do: they offer something. A refusal with an alternative attached is almost never taken badly, and it also tells you whether the thing was urgent. Most of the time they do not come back.

Stand up when someone arrives at your desk, if you can. Conversations held standing are much shorter, and nobody reads it as rude.

For the person who asks instead of looking

Some colleagues are not taking your time socially; they are outsourcing their thinking, usually without meaning to.

The fix is to answer in a way that does not invite the next one. Point at the source rather than giving the answer: it is in the handbook under returns, have a look and come back if it is not clear. Ask what they have tried first. Answer in writing and in a shared place, so the next person reads it instead of asking.

If they come back daily, offer a slot: let us do fifteen minutes at nine each day and do your questions then. That sounds generous and is in fact the single biggest reduction available, because it moves a dozen interruptions into one.

When work itself is drifting onto you

This is the version worth taking more seriously, because it compounds and because it eventually shows up in your own results.

Be specific and factual rather than resentful: I cannot take the supplier calls as well this month, they are taking about a day a week. If you can, name what would have to drop: I can do this if the report moves to next week, which do you want.

And route it to the right place rather than just declining. That is really a question for the finance team, I would put it to them. Many of these tasks have arrived with you because the correct owner is less approachable, and the only fix is to make the correct owner the route.

If it does not improve, that is a conversation with your manager, framed as capacity rather than complaint: here is what I am carrying, here is what is not getting done, how would you like me to prioritize.

If they are lonely rather than demanding

Sometimes the chatting is about them, not about you. Someone new, someone isolated, someone having a hard time at home.

In that case cutting them off completely is both unkind and unlikely to work. Give it a shape instead: lunch on Thursdays, or a coffee at eleven, with the rest of your day protected. A reliable short slot usually meets the need better than irregular long interruptions do.

That is a genuinely good thing to do, and it is still allowed to have edges.

Do not go silent and do not go sharp

Two instincts make this worse. One is saying nothing for months and then snapping, which turns a logistics problem into a rupture. The other is going cold and formal in the hope they take the hint, which they usually read as something being wrong between you.

Warm and clear beats both. The tone stays the same; the availability changes.

When it is bigger than time management

If a colleague ignores clear limits, follows you, takes credit for your work, or makes you dread coming in, that is not an interruption problem and these techniques are the wrong tool. Keep a dated record of what happens and take it to your manager or HR.

And if work has reached the point where you are not sleeping, dreading Mondays, or losing hours to resentment you cannot put down, that is worth talking to a doctor or a therapist about, alongside whatever you change at your desk.
