Panic on the Highway

The lane goes on, there is no exit, and your body decides this is an emergency. What is happening when panic arrives in the car, and how to get your driving back.

PanicGrounding Visitor 932 words

You are in the middle lane, the traffic is heavy, and the next exit is six miles away. Your hands go cold, the road seems to tilt, and the thought arrives: I cannot get out of here.

After that it tends to shrink. First you avoid that stretch, then the highway, then anything with a bridge or a tunnel, and eventually the route to work takes forty minutes longer than it used to.

Why the car is such a common place for this

Panic attaches itself to places you cannot easily leave, and a highway is the clearest example there is.

No shoulder to stop on. No exit for miles. Speed you cannot slow down without consequence. Nobody to help. And a job you must keep doing with your hands and feet while your body behaves as though something is terribly wrong.

The monotony contributes too. Long, straight, unchanging road with little to look at can produce a drifting, slightly unreal feeling, which your alarm system then reads as a symptom rather than as boredom.

What is actually happening in your body

Adrenaline, and nothing more dangerous than that.

Fast heart, tight chest, shallow breathing, tingling hands, legs that feel distant, vision that seems narrow or too bright. Those are alarm, and they peak and come down; panic cannot keep itself going indefinitely, and it does not cause you to lose control of the car.

The frightening part is usually not the road at all. It is the sensations plus the thought about what the sensations mean, which is why the same stretch of highway can be fine on Tuesday and terrible on Thursday.

What to do in the moment

Keep it very simple, because this is not the time to learn anything new.

Loosen your grip and let your shoulders drop. Breathe out longer than you breathe in, two or three times, and do not take big gulps of air, which makes the tingling worse.

Put your eyes where they belong: far down the road, not on your own chest. Narrow attention onto your body makes everything louder, and looking at the horizon is both calming and good driving.

Say something flat and true out loud: this is adrenaline, it peaks in a couple of minutes, I can drive while it does. Then slow slightly and move to the slower lane, which is the small amount of control you actually have.

Open the window a little, or turn the fan to your face. Cool air on your skin does something real.

Plan your stop before you need it

Most of the fear is about having no exit, so give yourself exits.

Know where the next rest stop or service area is, and give yourself permission to use it. Pulling in for five minutes is a reasonable thing to do and it is not an admission of anything; standing up next to the car for two minutes resets a surprising amount.

If it happens often, drive the route at a quiet hour first so you know where the gaps are.

Do not let the avoidance set

This is the part that decides how the next six months go.

Every time you take the long way round, you get relief, and the relief teaches your alarm that the highway was genuinely dangerous and that avoiding it was the rescue. The fear grows on that, which is why routes shrink.

So go back, in small doses, and sooner than you want to. One junction with someone beside you. Then two alone. Then the same stretch again the next day, and again, until it is boring rather than until you feel confident.

Keep the drives short and frequent rather than long and brave. Repetition is what teaches the lesson; one heroic trip followed by three weeks off does not.

And drop the props slowly: the passenger who has to be there, the bottle of water you clutch, the tablet in your pocket, the route that stays within a mile of home. Each one quietly tells your alarm you are still in danger.

Make driving conditions easier while you work on it

Sleep matters more than people think; a short night raises your resting arousal and makes everything feel closer to the edge.

Go easy on caffeine before a long drive, eat something so you are not driving on empty, and get your eyes and your ears checked if you have not lately, because poor vision at speed is a real and treatable cause of feeling unsafe.

Music or a podcast you know well helps most people. Silence leaves room for the monitoring.

When to get help with it

Please talk to a doctor if the symptoms do not fit this pattern — if you have fainted, had chest pain under exertion, or felt genuinely confused — because that deserves checking before anything else.

And talk to a doctor or a therapist if panic in the car is shaping your life: if you have given up driving, turned down work, or stopped visiting people because of the roads in between. Panic responds very well to treatment, usually in a small number of sessions, and the method is largely what is above, done in order with someone keeping you honest about the avoidance. A driving instructor who works with anxious drivers is also a genuinely good use of money.

And if it has become dark enough that you are not sure you want to be here, please tell a doctor or someone you trust today. In the US you can call or text 988 at any time.

If you are in crisis, call or text 988 (Suicide & Crisis Lifeline, US) or your local emergency number. Theraglee is a wellbeing tool, not a substitute for professional care.
