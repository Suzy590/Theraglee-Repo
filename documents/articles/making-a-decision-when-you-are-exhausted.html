Making a Decision When You Are Exhausted

Half past nine at night and somebody wants an answer. What tiredness does to deciding, how to spot that you are in it, and the one rule about the end of the day worth keeping.

Decision MakingBurnout Visitor 862 words

It is half past nine at night, you have been going since six in the morning, and somebody wants an answer. Or you want one, because the thing has been hanging over you all week and tonight feels like the night to settle it.

It is almost never the night to settle it. Decisions made at the end of a long stretch have a particular flavor, and most people can recognize it in hindsight.

What tiredness does to deciding

It is not that you get stupid. It is that specific parts of deciding stop working while others get louder.

The option that ends the discomfort fastest starts looking like the best one. Quitting, agreeing, buying, sending, saying yes to make it stop.

Downsides get flat and distant. You can still list them, and they do not land.

Small differences become unbearable. Two acceptable options feel like an impossible choice, which is why you can stand in a kitchen at ten at night unable to choose between two perfectly fine things.

And the mood you are in feels like information about the decision rather than information about your day. Everything looks bleaker at the end of a hard week, including the choice in front of you.

Learn to spot that you are in it

The useful skill is not deciding better when exhausted. It is noticing that you are.

The signs are fairly consistent. A sudden certainty after days of uncertainty. A strong pull to just get it over with. Tears or irritation that seem out of proportion. The sense that everything is too much, not just this.

When two or three of those are present, treat whatever conclusion you have just reached as provisional rather than final.

Say the sentence that buys you time

Most decisions that feel urgent are not. The urgency is usually your own discomfort rather than an actual deadline.

So have a line ready. I will come back to you tomorrow morning. I am not going to decide this tonight. Let me sleep on it and tell you at nine.

Nobody reasonable refuses that, and the ones who insist on an answer right now are telling you something useful about the thing they are asking for.

If it genuinely cannot wait, choose the most reversible option available, not the best one. A deposit rather than a purchase. A week's trial rather than a commitment. Yes for now rather than yes forever.

Decide when you are at your best, on purpose

The other half of this is building a slot for decisions rather than letting them land wherever.

For most people there is a window in the day when thinking is clearer, often the first half of the morning. Put the real decisions there deliberately: the email you will regret, the conversation about money, the choice about a job.

Eat something and drink some water first. A surprising number of impossible evenings are low blood sugar in a trench coat.

And write the decision down the night before, in one sentence, with what you actually have to choose between. Looking at that sentence in the morning, rested, is often the whole job, and the answer is frequently obvious in a way it was not at eleven.

Protect the end of the day from decisions

This is the part worth making a rule, because it saves the most trouble.

No big messages after nine. No resignations, no breakups, no bank transfers, no replying to the thing that upset you. Write the message if you need to, save it, and read it in the morning.

Most people who adopt that one rule describe it as the single most useful thing they have done for their relationships and their money.

If you have to decide while depleted anyway

Sometimes there is no choice. Illness, a new baby, a crisis, a job that gives you no good hours.

Then lean on structure rather than judgment. Write the three things that matter most about the decision and score the options against those alone. Ask one person you trust to say what they would do. Take the option that keeps the most doors open.

And forgive yourself for the ones you got wrong while running on nothing. A decision made at the end of a very hard week is a decision made in bad conditions, which is different from a decision made badly.

When to get help

Please talk to your doctor if you are exhausted most of the time rather than at the end of hard days, if sleep is not fixing it, or if the tiredness arrived with a low mood, because there are physical and treatable reasons for that and it is worth finding out which one you are dealing with. A therapist can help if you are facing a decision you have been circling for months, or if the exhaustion comes from a situation you cannot see a way out of, which is a common reason for both. And if you are being pressed for immediate answers by someone in a way that leaves you frightened or trapped, that is worth saying out loud to a professional rather than carrying alone.

If you are in crisis, call or text 988 (Suicide & Crisis Lifeline, US) or your local emergency number. Theraglee is a wellbeing tool, not a substitute for professional care.
