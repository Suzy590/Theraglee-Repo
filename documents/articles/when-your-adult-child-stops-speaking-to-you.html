When Your Adult Child Stops Speaking to You

The calls stopped. You do not fully understand why, and every attempt to fix it seems to make it worse. What tends to help, what makes it harder, and how to survive the waiting.

FamilyLoss Visitor 970 words

They stopped answering. Maybe there was a message explaining it, maybe a conversation that went badly, maybe nothing at all. You have written, apologized, sent things on their birthday. Some of it went unanswered and some of it made things worse. You do not know whether this is a season or the rest of your life.

Estrangement from an adult child is one of the loneliest things a person can go through, partly because of the loss itself and partly because there is nowhere to talk about it. Other people's children call them.

Start with the hardest part

Whatever you believe about the fairness of it, the practical situation is this: they have the say. An adult gets to decide who is in their life, and no amount of being right will change that. Any path back runs through respecting that, even when you think they have it wrong.

That is not the same as accepting a version of events you do not agree with. It is accepting who controls the door.

Try to hear what they actually said

If they gave you a reason, read it again, slowly, as information rather than as an accusation. Most estranged children say afterward that they explained clearly and were not heard: the parent argued with the facts, or apologized for the wrong thing, or made the conversation about their own pain.

Try to separate what you did from what you meant. Most parents in this situation did not intend harm. Intention and effect are different things, and they are asking about the effect.

If you genuinely do not know the reason, you can ask once, plainly, and then wait.

Apologize for the thing, not for the estrangement

The apology that helps names specifics and takes responsibility without conditions. I am sorry I dismissed you when you told me about that. I have thought about it and I can see now that it hurt you.

The apology that makes things worse is the one that is really a complaint: I am sorry for whatever I did, but I do not think I deserve this. I am sorry you feel that way. I did my best and you have no idea what I sacrificed.

Send one good one and then stop. Repeated apologizing becomes pressure.

Do not flood them

The instinct is to keep reaching: messages, gifts, showing up, calling other relatives, sending long letters. Every one of those is love and most of them push a person further away, because they signal that their decision is not being respected.

A better pattern is small, low-pressure, and infrequent. Something once in a while that requires no reply: thinking of you on your birthday, I love you, no need to write back. Then leave it.

Do not go through other people

Enlisting siblings, your ex, or their partner as messengers or advocates almost always backfires and tends to damage those relationships too. If there are grandchildren involved, that becomes agonizing, and pressure still does not work. Keep the line simple and direct.

Take the blame you actually own, and only that

Some estrangements follow something serious. Some follow years of small things: contempt, control, taking sides, not protecting them, an inability to hear criticism. Some follow nothing that a reasonable person would call harm, and involve a partner, a new belief, or an illness.

Work out honestly which parts are yours, and let go of defending the rest. A parent who can say I got that wrong without also needing to win is the parent an adult child can eventually come back to.

Get help for yourself, separately

This is the practical advice most often skipped. Estranged parents frequently carry this alone for years, because it feels shameful and because people assume the worst about them.

A therapist of your own gives you somewhere to put the grief, and helps with the harder question of what to look at in yourself, which is much easier to do with support than alone. There are also support groups specifically for estranged parents; being with people who do not need the situation explained is a considerable relief.

Build a life that is not waiting

The hardest and most necessary thing: do not put your life on hold. People can wait years. Keep your friendships, your work, your interests, your health. Not as a way of moving on, which you may never do, but because a parent whose entire existence is suspended around one absence is both very unhappy and harder to reconnect with.

Grieve it, even though they are alive

This is a real loss and it deserves to be grieved rather than only fought. Let yourself be sad about it, mark the birthdays, cry when it comes. Grief for a living person is disorienting because there is no ending, and it still works better felt than held down.

Leave the door visibly open

If there is one thing to keep doing, it is making sure they know the door is not locked. A message once a year that says it, without pressure, without a list of grievances, without conditions. Many reconciliations happen years later, and almost all of them happen because the child knew they could come back without having to fight their way in.

When you need more support

Please talk to a therapist if this is affecting your sleep, your health, or your ability to function, if you have withdrawn from the people still in your life, or if the grief has become despair. And if you ever find yourself feeling hopeless or having thoughts of harming yourself, please reach out that day. In the US you can call or text 988 at any time. This is one of the heaviest things a parent can carry, and you should not be carrying it without help.

If you are in crisis, call or text 988 (Suicide & Crisis Lifeline, US) or your local emergency number. Theraglee is a wellbeing tool, not a substitute for professional care.
