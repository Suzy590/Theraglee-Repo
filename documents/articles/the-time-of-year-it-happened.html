The Time of Year It Happened

Some dates carry weight. Why the weeks around an anniversary can knock you sideways before you have even noticed the calendar, and how to plan for one rather than be ambushed by it.

TraumaGrief Visitor 945 words

You are irritable, sleeping badly, and oddly tired, and you cannot think why. Then you see the date on something and it lands: it is nearly a year, or five, or twenty, since the accident, the diagnosis, the day they died, the night it happened.

This is one of the most common experiences there is and one of the least talked about, so people tend to assume they are going backward. You are not. Your body is keeping a kind of calendar, and it often knows before you do.

Why the calendar matters

Memory is not only words. It is light, temperature, smells, the sound of the heating coming on, what is on the trees.

So when the season comes round, a great deal of what was present at the time is present again, and your nervous system responds to all of it at once. That is why an anniversary reaction can feel physical and arrive without a thought attached: a heaviness in the chest, broken sleep, jumpiness, a short fuse, a flatness you cannot argue with.

Holidays and family dates do the same work. The first birthday, the first Christmas, the day you always went somewhere together.

It often starts before the date

The hard part is frequently the run-up rather than the day itself, which catches people out.

Two or three weeks beforehand, the dreading starts, usually unrecognized. People find themselves snapping, withdrawing, drinking a bit more, working later, unable to settle to anything. Then the actual day arrives and is sometimes quieter than expected, and the week after can be the flattest of all.

Knowing that shape in advance takes a surprising amount of the frightening out of it. Nothing has gone wrong; this is what the body does with a date.

Decide what the day is for

The worst version of an anniversary is the one nobody has planned, where you pretend it is an ordinary Tuesday and then fall apart at four in the afternoon.

So make a decision, even a small one, about what the day holds. Some people want ritual: the grave, the beach, the song, cooking what they cooked. Some want distraction and company. Some want to be alone with the photographs, and some want to be at work with their hands busy.

All of those are legitimate. What helps is that you chose it, rather than being carried by the day.

Build in the exits too. If you are going somewhere with people, decide in advance how you will leave if you need to, and tell one person so you do not have to explain in the moment.

Plan the whole week, not just the date

Treat the surrounding days as part of it.

Protect sleep where you can, because an anniversary week with three bad nights in it is a different proposition. Keep the week's demands low: this is not the week to host, interview, move, or have the difficult conversation you have been putting off.

Put two or three ordinary, steadying things in it on purpose. A walk at the same time each day, a swim, a meal with someone easy. Not a project, just structure, because structure is what holds a week like this up.

And expect to be worse at everything for a few days. Slower, more forgetful, less patient. Plan around it rather than being disappointed by it.

Tell one or two people

The silence around anniversaries is the part that does the extra damage, because the people closest to you often have no idea what week it is.

So say it plainly: next Thursday is the anniversary, I am not great in the run-up, I may be quiet. That is usually all it takes, and it turns an unexplained bad mood into something the people around you can be kind about.

If you are the friend rather than the person, the message to send is the simple one. Thinking of you this week. No question in it, nothing required back.

What helps in the moments it comes at you

When it surges, the aim is not to argue it away but to be where you are: in a room, in October, years later.

Get your feet flat on the floor, breathe out longer than you breathe in, and name five things you can see. Cold water on your wrists or face is oddly effective. So is moving: a walk around the block will do more than an hour of trying to think your way out.

And if the memory itself is coming at you in pictures, remind yourself in plain words that it is a memory, it is in the past, and you are here now. Say it out loud if you can.

When to get help with it

Anniversary reactions usually ease off within a few weeks. If yours does not, if each year is getting heavier rather than lighter, or if the memories are intruding during the day, the sleep is not coming back, or you are keeping yourself going on alcohol, please take it to a doctor or a therapist.

Grief and trauma both respond well to treatment, including years afterward, and there is nothing unusual about asking for help for something that happened a long time ago. Say to whoever you see that this time of year is when it is worst; it is information they will know exactly what to do with.

And if the week has taken you somewhere dark, and you are not sure you want to be here, please tell a doctor or someone you trust today rather than waiting. In the US you can call or text 988 at any time.
