When You Keep Getting Talked Over

Being interrupted repeatedly wears down more than one conversation. What is usually happening, phrases that hold the floor without a confrontation, and when it is worth naming the pattern.

AssertivenessAnxiety Visitor 822 words

You start a sentence. Somebody else finishes theirs over the top of it. You stop, because stopping is polite, and the conversation moves on without the thing you were going to say.

Then it happens again. And after enough rounds you stop starting, which everybody reads as having nothing to add.

What is usually going on

Most interrupting is not aimed at you, which matters mainly because it tells you what will and will not fix it.

Some of it is conversational style. In some families and some regions, talking over each other is how enthusiasm is expressed, and nobody involved experiences it as rude. Put a person raised that way next to someone raised to wait and the second person will lose every exchange without either of them intending it.

Some of it is pace. Fast groups leave very short gaps, and people who think before speaking never find an opening. That is a timing mismatch rather than a lack of respect.

Some of it is status, and it is worth being honest that it is not evenly distributed. Plenty of people are talked over more than others for reasons that have nothing to do with how they speak.

And some of it is one particular person who does it to everyone and has never been told.

Why it costs more than the sentence

The immediate loss is small. Nobody heard one point, and the point was often not that important.

The accumulation is the real cost. You start pre-editing, deciding things are not worth the effort of getting in, and the quiet becomes a habit. Over a year that reshapes how a room sees you and, worse, how you see yourself in rooms.

It also produces a particular kind of tension, because part of you is on alert the whole time, looking for the gap. That is tiring and it makes you worse at the conversation.

Holding the floor without a confrontation

Most of this is handled with small mechanics rather than a difficult talk.

Do not stop when they start. Lowering your voice and trailing off is a learned reflex and it teaches the room that you yield. Carrying on for your remaining few words, at the same volume, resolves a surprising amount.

Use a short flag. Let me finish this bit. One second, I am nearly there. Hold on. These sound blunter written down than they land in a real conversation.

Come back to it rather than letting it go. Going back to what I was saying is almost always accepted, because nobody wants to look like they cut someone off.

Signal a length at the start. Three quick things. Can I take thirty seconds on this. A stated edge makes people wait, because they know when it ends.

Drop the preamble. Long run-ups invite interruption. Getting to the point in the first sentence means there is less to interrupt.

And in meetings, say the thing early. The gaps close as a discussion warms up.

Naming the pattern, when it is worth it

If one person does it consistently and it matters, say so, privately and lightly rather than in the moment.

Keep it specific and short. I notice I get cut off a fair bit when we talk and I end up not saying things. Can you give me a second when I start.

Expect them to be surprised. Most habitual interrupters genuinely do not know, and many take it well because nobody has ever told them.

If it is a pattern in a group rather than one person, raising it with whoever runs the room usually works better than raising it with the group.

What to let go

Not every instance is worth handling, and treating each one as a test is its own kind of exhausting.

In a fast, friendly, overlapping conversation where everybody interrupts everybody, the answer is usually to join in rather than to wait, and that is a skill you can practice.

Where it is not worth it at all, keep what you had to say and send it afterward. A short message with the point in it often lands better than the interrupted version would have.

When this is bigger than the interrupting

If you have stopped speaking up in most settings, if the thought of taking the floor brings real dread, or if being cut off leaves you shaken for hours rather than mildly irritated, then the interrupting is sitting on top of something larger.

That is common ground for a therapist, and the work on it is practical rather than open-ended: how to hold a room, what the fear is actually predicting, and where the habit of yielding came from. And if the pattern is specific to one place, such as a workplace where only certain people get talked over, that is worth naming as what it is rather than treating it as a problem with how you speak.
