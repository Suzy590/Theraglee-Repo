The Message You Are About to Send

It is written, it is accurate, and some part of you knows it will cost you. How to put a gap between the feeling and the send button, and what to do with the message instead.

DBTEmotion Regulation Visitor 993 words

It is typed. It says the true thing, finally, in the right order. Your thumb is over the button and there is a strange pressure behind your eyes, because you know and do not want to know that this will make things worse.

Maybe it is the reply to the email that insulted you. Maybe it is the four-paragraph text at one in the morning. Maybe it is the one line designed to land exactly where it will hurt most.

Why it feels so urgent

A strong feeling arrives with a sense that something must be done right now. That urgency is part of the feeling, not information about the situation, which is why people send things at midnight that they would not send at ten in the morning.

The relief of sending is real, and it is short. It lasts a few minutes, and then it is replaced by watching the screen, waiting to see what comes back, which is a far worse state than the one you were in before.

So the question is not whether what you wrote is true. Plenty of these messages are entirely true. The question is what you will have left after you send it.

Write it, then do not send it

Writing is useful. Sending is the problem. So keep the writing and lose the send.

Put it somewhere it cannot go anywhere: a note on your phone, a blank document, a piece of paper. Write the unedited version, with all the parts you would never say. Getting it out of your head takes most of the pressure off, because much of what drives the urge is the need to say it somewhere.

Make sending take an extra step

Willpower at midnight is not a plan. Friction is.

Delete the draft from the message window and keep it in notes, so sending requires copying it back. Put the phone in another room. Turn it off. Log out of the app. If you have ever sent something you regretted, ask a friend to hold your phone when you are drinking.

None of this is childish. It is how people manage a predictable moment of poor judgment.

Wait out the peak

Strong feelings have a shape. They rise, hold, and come down, usually within twenty minutes if you are not feeding them. Rereading the offending email every two minutes is feeding it.

So set a deadline rather than a decision. Not I will not send this, which your mind will immediately argue with, but I will not send this before nine tomorrow morning. That is far easier to keep, and by nine it is usually a different message or no message.

While you wait, do something physical. Walk, shower, stand outside, wash up, move something heavy. Thinking it over keeps the urgency alive. Moving uses it up.

Check what you actually want

In the morning, with the draft in front of you, it helps to ask one plain question: what do I want to happen after this is read.

If the honest answer is I want them to feel as bad as I do, then the message will not get you anything you want, because people who feel attacked defend themselves rather than understand you. That answer is not shameful. It is just worth knowing before you act on it.

If the answer is I want them to stop doing this, or I want an apology, or I want to be taken seriously, then write a message that could plausibly produce that: shorter, specific, about one thing, without the lines designed to wound.

Send the smaller version

The sendable version is almost always three sentences. What happened, how it landed, what you want.

When you changed the plan without telling me, I ended up looking foolish in front of the client. That mattered to me. Please tell me directly next time.

Most of what gets cut was for you rather than for them: the history, the proof, the sarcasm, the list of previous offenses. Cutting it is what makes the message work.

Some things belong in a conversation instead

If what you need to say is complicated, or about the relationship rather than one event, a written message is the worst available format. Tone does not survive text, and the other person will read it in the worst voice available.

Those go in person or on the phone, which also slows you down naturally. Can we talk later is a complete message, and it is often the right one.

Forgive yourself for the ones you already sent

Nearly everyone has sent something they wish they had not. If that is where you are, the useful move is a short, clean repair without relitigating the argument: I sent that when I was furious and I went too far. I am sorry. The part I do still want to talk about is this one thing.

No long explanation, no taking it all back if some of it was true. People accept that kind of repair far more often than you would expect.

Notice if the pattern is bigger than the messages

If this happens regularly, the texts are a symptom rather than the problem. Look at what tends to be true beforehand: drinking, exhaustion, a particular person, a particular time of night, an old wound getting pressed. That pattern is what to work on, and it is much more tractable than it feels.

When to get help

If sending things you regret is costing you relationships or your job, if the urges feel impossible to sit with, or if the anger is bigger than the situations setting it off, a therapist can help with the skills for exactly this, and they are learnable rather than innate. If alcohol is involved most times it happens, say that to a doctor or counselor too. Wanting to be heard is not the problem here. The timing is, and timing can be changed.
