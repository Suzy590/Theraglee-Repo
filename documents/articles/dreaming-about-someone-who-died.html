Dreaming About Someone Who Died

They were alive in the dream, and then you woke up. Why these dreams come, why waking from them hurts so much, and what to do with the hour afterward.

GriefLoss Visitor 917 words

In the dream they were just there. Making tea, sitting in the passenger seat, saying something ordinary. Nothing about it was strange while it was happening. Then you woke up, and for a second or two you did not know, and then you did.

That drop is one of the cruelest parts of grief, and almost nobody warns you about it. Some mornings you lose them again before you have even got out of bed.

Why these dreams come

Sleep is partly where your mind sorts through what it has been carrying. When somebody central to your life is gone, that is the largest thing you are carrying, so it shows up at night whether or not you have been thinking about them during the day.

The dreams tend to come in waves rather than steadily. Often more in the first months, then in bursts around anniversaries, birthdays, holidays, a house move, or any time life changes in a way you would have told them about.

What the dreams are like varies enormously and none of it means anything is wrong with you. Some people dream the person alive and well and perfectly ordinary. Some dream the illness or the accident over and over. Some dream they are searching for them through rooms and never find them. Some dream nothing at all for a year and then dream vividly. Some people never dream of them and feel cheated by that, which is its own kind of ache.

Why waking up hurts so much

For a moment after waking, the part of you that keeps track of what is true has not caught up. So the loss arrives fresh, with the full force it had at the beginning. People describe it as going back to day one.

That does not mean you are back at day one. Grief does not undo its own progress overnight. What has happened is that you had a few seconds without the knowledge, and then the knowledge returned, and returning is painful in a way that simply knowing is not.

Give the first hour some room

The hour after one of these dreams deserves gentler handling than you are probably giving it.

Do not jump straight to the phone and the day. Sit up. Put your feet on the floor. Have something warm. Let the tears come if they are there, and let them not come if they are not, since plenty of people wake from these dreams dry-eyed and shaken.

Say out loud what is true: it is Thursday, they died in March, I dreamed about them. Naming the facts does not take the sadness away. It stops the confusion from making everything worse.

If you can, tell someone. A text to a sibling or a friend saying I dreamed about Dad last night does more than you might think, mainly because it stops you carrying the morning alone.

What to do with what the dream contained

It is tempting to read these dreams as messages, and whether you take them that way is yours to decide. There is nothing foolish about finding comfort in one. There is also nothing missing in you if you find them simply painful.

What is usually more useful than interpreting the dream is noticing what it stirred up. A dream where you argued may point at something unresolved. A dream of searching often arrives when you are feeling the absence most practically. A dream of them well and happy can leave behind a longing that is worth letting yourself feel rather than shaking off.

Writing a few lines about it helps some people. Not an analysis, just what happened and how it left you. Over months, those notes often show the dreams softening, which is hard to see from inside one morning.

The dreams that keep you from sleeping

If a dream of the death itself keeps repeating, or you are frightened to fall asleep, that is a different thing and it is treatable. Repeating distressing dreams respond well to specific approaches a therapist can teach, and you do not have to put up with them for years.

The same is true if you are waking at the same time every night, drinking to get to sleep, or lying awake dreading the dream. Those patterns tend to build on themselves, and they are worth mentioning to a doctor sooner rather than later.

When the dreams change

Many people find that over a long time the quality shifts: less searching, less of the hospital, more of the person as they were. That shift is not something you can hurry, and it is not a sign of forgetting. People worry about that a great deal, as though sleeping peacefully were a kind of disloyalty. Carrying someone well and sleeping well are not opposites.

When to get help

Please talk to a doctor or a therapist if the dreams are keeping you from sleeping, if you dread the night, if you are using alcohol to get through it, or if months on the grief is as raw as the first week and you cannot function in your days. Grief that stays stuck has good treatments, and so do nightmares. If you ever feel you cannot go on without them, please tell someone today. In the US you can call or text 988 at any time. Dreaming about someone you loved is not a problem to be fixed. The exhaustion around it sometimes is, and that part can be helped.

If you are in crisis, call or text 988 (Suicide & Crisis Lifeline, US) or your local emergency number. Theraglee is a wellbeing tool, not a substitute for professional care.
