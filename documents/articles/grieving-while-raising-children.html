Grieving While Raising Children

Someone died and the school run did not stop. How to grieve in a house that still needs lunches made, what children can handle seeing, and where to find room for your own loss.

GriefParenting Visitor 872 words

Someone central to you died, and a child still needed breakfast the next morning. There was a funeral and then a Tuesday, and then packed lunches, and homework, and a birthday party you had to pretend to care about.

Everyone asks how the children are doing. Almost nobody asks how you are, and if they do, you have four minutes to answer before someone needs something.

Grief postponed does not go away

With children in the house, grieving gets pushed into the edges of the day: the car, the shower, after everyone is asleep. That is survival and it works for a while.

What it cannot do is cancel the grief. It waits. People often find it arrives in force months later, when the practical emergency is over, and feel ashamed of falling apart at a point when everyone assumes they are through it. That is not a relapse. It is the first time there was room.

So where you can, make a little room on purpose rather than waiting for it to force its way in. Twenty minutes with the door shut. A walk alone. One afternoon a month that is yours. A session with someone whose job it is to listen.

Children can handle a sad parent

The fear that drives most of the hiding is that your grief will damage them. In fact, seeing a parent sad and still functioning is how children learn that feelings are survivable.

What children find frightening is not tears. It is tears with no explanation, because they fill the silence with something worse, usually that it is their fault or that you are about to disappear too.

So explain simply, at the size of the child. I am crying because I miss Grandma. It is not because of anything you did. I will be all right, and I am sad today.

Then let them see the recovery as well as the crying, since that is the half that teaches them the lesson.

Say the truth, in small pieces

Children do better with plain words than with soft ones. Died rather than lost, gone to sleep or passed over, which small children take literally and then become frightened of bedtime.

Expect questions at terrible moments, in the car, at the supermarket, out of nowhere, repeated for months. Answer briefly and go back to what you were doing, which tells them the subject is allowed and not an emergency.

And expect grief in a child to look like behavior rather than sadness: clinginess, tantrums, going quiet, falling behind, a sudden return to younger habits, headaches and stomach aches.

Let the standards drop for a while

The house does not have to run as it did. Lower everything that can be lowered: more simple meals, more screen time, fewer activities, a looser bedtime, a plainer version of the holidays.

Keep two or three anchors steady, because children run on routine: roughly the same bedtime, school, and one small daily ritual that belongs to you both. Everything else can wait a few months.

Take the help, and be specific about it

People want to help and cannot guess what you need, and grief makes asking almost impossible. So keep a short list of things you can hand over when someone offers: a school pickup, a casserole, an hour of childcare, a lift, someone to sit in the house while you sleep.

Specific requests get said yes to. Let me know if you need anything does not.

Tell the school

One short message to the teacher means someone is watching for the bad afternoons, and it means your child is not in trouble for something that is grief in disguise. Ask what they have seen, too. Children often behave very differently there.

Watch for the bits that are harder than they should be

Some grief with children carries extra weight: being the only parent now, grieving the person you used to parent alongside, a child who looks like them, or a death you are also angry about.

Bedtime and mornings are the usual pressure points. If you know that, you can plan for them rather than being ambushed every day.

You are allowed your own loss

This is the part that gets lost. The person who died was your mother, your partner, your brother. Being the grown-up in the house does not make you the support staff for everyone else's grief.

Saying I am grieving too, out loud, to one adult who will take it seriously, is worth more than it sounds.

When to get help

Please talk to your doctor if you cannot function, if you are not sleeping for weeks, if you are drinking more to get through the evenings, or if months on nothing has softened at all. Ask about grief counseling for you and about support for the children, since many places have services specifically for bereaved families and they are often free. Tell someone today if you have thoughts of harming yourself or of not wanting to be here. In the US you can call or text 988 at any time. You do not have to do this well. You only have to get through it, and you are allowed help with that.

If you are in crisis, call or text 988 (Suicide & Crisis Lifeline, US) or your local emergency number. Theraglee is a wellbeing tool, not a substitute for professional care.
