How to Find Out What You Actually Value

Everyone says to live by your values, but most of us could not name ours if asked. Four honest questions that reveal what you care about, and what to do once you know.

Values Visitor 881 words

Live according to your values. It sounds obvious and wise, and then someone asks, so what are yours, and the answer comes out as a list of nice words. Honesty. Family. Growth. Kindness. The words are true enough, but they are so general that they do not help you decide anything, which is the whole point of knowing them.

Values are not a poster on the wall. They are the things you would keep choosing if nobody were watching and nothing were at stake. Finding them takes a little digging, and the digging is worth it, because a life that fits its values feels very different from one that does not, even when the two look the same from outside.

Values are directions, not destinations

A goal is something you can finish: get the promotion, run the race, pay off the card. A value is a direction you can keep moving in forever: being someone people can rely on, learning, being close to the people you love. You never complete a value. You just keep facing it or turning away from it, in small choices, all day long.

This matters because goals can be reached and then leave you empty, while values give the goals their meaning. Two people can chase the same promotion, one because they value providing for their family and one because they value being respected, and the promotion will mean different things to each of them.

Four questions that reveal them

Rather than picking words off a list, try these. Write the answers down; they are more honest on paper.

When were you last proud of yourself, quietly, not for an audience? Not a big achievement, necessarily. Maybe you told the truth when lying would have been easier, or you stayed with someone who was struggling, or you finished something nobody asked for. What was underneath the pride? That is a value.

What makes you angry in a way that lasts? Anger points at a value being violated. If unfairness makes your blood boil, fairness is yours. If being talked over infuriates you, being heard, and hearing others, matters to you. If waste bothers you, care and stewardship are in there.

What do you envy? Envy is embarrassing and very informative. If you feel a pang when a friend takes a year to travel, adventure or freedom may be a value you have been ignoring. If you envy someone's calm home life, that is a signal too. Envy shows what you want that you have not been giving yourself.

What would you regret not having done? Imagine looking back from very old age. Not the achievements. The way you spent your days and who you spent them with. What would sting to have missed? That is the clearest signal of all.

Look for the pattern

Read your answers together. Most people find three to five themes underneath them: things like closeness, making things, being of use, honesty, freedom, steadiness, learning. Write those themes in your own words, not the poster words. Being someone my kids can count on is better than family. Building things that work is better than achievement.

Check them against your calendar

Now the uncomfortable part. Look at where your time and energy actually go in an ordinary week. Not where you wish they went. Where they go. Then hold that next to your list.

There will be gaps. Everyone has them. Maybe closeness is at the top of your values and you have not had an unhurried conversation with your partner in a month. Maybe making things matters most to you and you spend your days in meetings about things other people make. The gaps are not a verdict. They are a map of where a small change would make the biggest difference.

Use them to decide

The real payoff of knowing your values is that decisions get easier. When you are torn, ask which option moves you toward what you care about. Not which is safer or more impressive, but which is more like the person you want to be. Values do not tell you what will happen. They tell you what you would be proud of regardless of what happens, and that turns out to be a much steadier way to choose.

They also help you say no. Once you know that steadiness is something you value, it becomes easier to decline the exciting opportunity that would blow up your life, without feeling like a coward for it.

Let them change

Values shift with life. What mattered at twenty-five may not at forty-five, and that is not inconsistency. Revisit the questions every year or so, and expect some answers to move.

When the gap is too wide to close alone

Sometimes the exercise reveals that your life and your values have drifted very far apart, and the thought of closing the gap brings up fear, grief, or a kind of paralysis. That is worth talking through with a therapist, who can help you sort what is possible to change from what you are grieving, and take the first steps without blowing everything up. Knowing what you value is the beginning. Living it sometimes needs a professional's help, and asking for that is itself an act of taking your own life seriously.
