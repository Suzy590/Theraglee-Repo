Going to the Doctor When You Have Been Putting It Off

The longer you leave it, the harder the appointment gets to make. Why delay feeds on itself, what the embarrassment is usually about, and how to get the first call made.

BodyShame Visitor 921 words

There is something you have been meaning to get looked at. It has been a few months. Possibly a few years. You think about it in bed and then not again until the next time you think about it in bed.

And somewhere along the way the appointment stopped being about the symptom and started being about how long you have left it, which makes picking up the phone harder, which makes it longer still.

Why waiting gets harder, not easier

Delay feeds on itself in a particular way. At first it is a small thing you will get around to. Then a certain amount of time passes and the delay itself becomes a thing to explain, and now the appointment carries two embarrassments instead of one.

There is also the question you are avoiding. As long as you do not go, the thing stays undefined, and undefined is more comfortable than a specific answer might be. People put off checks for exactly this reason, and it is the opposite of reassuring, because most of what gets found early is more fixable than the same thing found late.

And avoidance builds its own momentum. Every week you do not make the call, you have practiced not making the call, and the practice makes it feel more natural than doing it.

What the embarrassment is usually about

It is rarely the symptom. It is usually one of a few other things, and naming which one helps.

Being judged for leaving it. Almost everybody who works in medicine sees this every week. The thing that is remarkable to you is routine to them, including the delay.

Being judged about your body, your weight, how you live, whether you have been looking after yourself. That fear is not irrational, since plenty of people have had an appointment go that way. It is also something you can prepare for rather than avoid, which the next section covers.

Being thought of as making a fuss over nothing. Most people worry about this and it is worth saying plainly that working out whether something is nothing is the job you are asking them to do. It is not your responsibility to pre-screen yourself.

Being seen or examined. A real reason for delay and one people rarely say out loud, which means it rarely gets accommodated when it easily could be.

Making the first call easier

Lower the bar from fix this to have it looked at. The goal of the first appointment is not a resolution. It is to stop carrying it alone.

Write down what you want to say before you call, in one or two sentences. People talk themselves out of appointments mid-sentence because they start apologizing. I have had a lump on my shoulder for about a year and I would like someone to look at it is a complete reason.

Do not rehearse the explanation for the delay. If it comes up, I know, I put it off is enough, and nothing further is required of you.

Book the earliest slot you can bear, because time between booking and going is time for the avoidance to rebuild. If the first available is weeks out, take it and put it somewhere you will see it.

If calling is the hard part, use whatever other route exists. Online booking, a message through a portal, asking somebody to sit with you while you call. The route does not have to be dignified.

Making the appointment itself easier

Bring your list on paper or your phone, and lead with the thing you are most worried about. People often save the real reason for the end and run out of time.

Say what you need, plainly, at the start. I get anxious about being examined. I would rather not be weighed today unless it matters for this. I want to understand the options before deciding anything. These are ordinary requests and most clinicians will simply accommodate them.

Take someone with you if that helps, or ask whether you can. A second person remembers what was said, which matters more than people expect.

Ask for the next step in writing, or write it down yourself before you leave. A lot of follow-up gets lost in the gap between what was said and what you remember an hour later.

If a past appointment went badly

Some of this delay is not avoidance at all. It is a reasonable response to being dismissed, spoken to badly, or not believed.

If that is your history, say so at the start. Last time I raised this I felt brushed off, so I want to be sure we get to the bottom of it. It changes most conversations.

And you are allowed to change clinician. People stay with a practice out of a sense of obligation that does not actually exist. Asking to see somebody different is a normal request.

When the delay is bigger than one appointment

If you avoid medical care generally, if the thought of an examination brings real fear rather than mild dread, or if there are several things you have been not dealing with for years, that pattern is worth treating as its own thing rather than tackling it one symptom at a time.

A therapist can work on health-related avoidance directly, and it responds well. Tell whichever doctor you do see that appointments are difficult for you, too. They can often make the practical parts easier, and what they cannot help with is what they do not know.
