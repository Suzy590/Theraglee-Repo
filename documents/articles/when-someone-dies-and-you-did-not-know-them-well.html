When Someone Dies and You Did Not Know Them Well

A colleague, a neighbor, someone from a group. Why grief does not check your credentials, how it tends to arrive sideways, and how to mark it in a way that fits the relationship.

GriefLoss Visitor 881 words

A colleague you liked. Your neighbor. Somebody from a class, or a team, or a group you are in. Not a friend exactly, and not family, and now they have died and you feel much worse than you expected to.

Then comes the second, stranger feeling: that you are not entitled to it. You were not close. Other people are properly grieving. You have no standing here, so you should probably keep it to yourself.

Grief does not check your credentials

The size of a loss is not set by how close you were on paper. It is set by what the person occupied in your actual days.

Somebody you saw five mornings a week for six years can be a bigger absence than a cousin you saw at weddings. Somebody who said one kind thing at the right moment can leave a hole out of all proportion to the relationship.

And some of what you feel is not only about them. A death at the edge of your life tends to open other doors: your own losses, your own mortality, how fragile the ordinary arrangement of your week turns out to be.

None of that needs justifying. It is what happened.

Expect it to come sideways

Grief for someone you were not close to tends to arrive in odd forms, which is part of why people doubt it.

Crying at something unrelated. A flat, tired week. Preoccupation with the details of how it happened. Looking for their name in the group chat. Finding their chair, their shift, their place in the room strange for months.

You may also feel things that seem wrong. Guilt that you did not know they were unwell. Relief that it was not somebody closer. A kind of hunger for information that feels unseemly. All of that is ordinary and none of it makes you a ghoul.

Find a place to put it that fits

You do not need to insert yourself into the front row of a grief that belongs to other people. You also do not have to swallow it.

So choose a proportionate version. Go to the funeral if it is open, and sit at the back. Write a short note to the family about one specific thing: he always asked about my daughter, she taught me how to do this job properly. Families treasure those, and they are often the only ones that tell them something they did not know.

Mark it in your own way. Walk the route you used to walk. Make the thing they recommended. Keep the mug on the shelf.

And say it out loud to one person. Honestly, I am more upset about this than I expected to be. The relief of saying it is considerable, and almost everybody else at the edges feels the same and assumes they are alone in it.

Let the group talk about it

With a colleague or someone from a group, the usual mistake is a quick acknowledgment and then nothing, which leaves everybody separately pretending.

So be the person who mentions them. I keep thinking about her. It is strange being here without him. That gives everyone else permission, and shared remembering is most of what helps a group through it.

If it was your workplace, ask whether something small can be done. A card, a plant, a minute at the start of a meeting, a note on the board. Not a ceremony, just an acknowledgment that somebody was here.

Be careful with the gaps

When you are not close, you often do not get the facts, and the mind fills them in.

Try to resist the reconstruction, especially if the death was sudden or the circumstances are unclear. People lose weeks to imagining a final scene they will never have confirmed, and it is not a kindness to anybody.

What you can do is hold the part you actually have: the version of them you knew, which is real, and which the people who loved them may be glad to hear about one day.

Give it longer than you think

Because the relationship was small, people expect their grief to be brief, and then feel foolish when it is not.

It can sit around for months, surfacing in the gaps. That is not a sign of something wrong with you or of a secret attachment you are not admitting to. It is a person who was part of your life being gone from it.

When to get help

Please talk to a therapist or a grief counselor if this is not easing after a couple of months, if you are not sleeping, or if it has pulled up a loss of your own that you have never really dealt with, which is one of the most common reasons an edge-of-life death hits so hard. See your doctor too if your mood has been low for weeks, since bereavement at any distance can tip that. If your workplace has an employee assistance line, this is exactly what it is for, and you do not need to have been close to use it. And if you are having thoughts of not wanting to be here, please tell someone today. In the US you can call or text 988 at any time.

If you are in crisis, call or text 988 (Suicide & Crisis Lifeline, US) or your local emergency number. Theraglee is a wellbeing tool, not a substitute for professional care.
