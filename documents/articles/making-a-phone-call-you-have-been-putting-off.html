Making a Phone Call You Have Been Putting Off

The call takes four minutes and you have carried it for three weeks. Why phone calls are harder than they look, and a short script for getting one over with.

Social AnxietyWorry Visitor 994 words

The call has been on your list for eleven days. You know the number. You know roughly what you need to say. It would take four minutes.

And every time the list reaches it, your eyes slide past, and somehow it is six in the evening and the office is closed again.

Then there is the extra layer: you feel ridiculous. It is a phone call. You are an adult. That feeling does not help, and it is worth putting down before anything else.

Why a phone call is harder than an email

There are real reasons this particular task is heavier than its size suggests.

A call happens in real time, so you cannot edit yourself. You do not know what they will say, so you cannot plan the whole thing. You might be put on hold, transferred, or asked something you do not know. And if it goes badly, it goes badly while you are in it, with no way to close the tab.

On top of that, many calls on the avoided list are about something unpleasant to begin with: a bill, a mistake, a result, a cancellation, a conversation you have been dreading with a person rather than an institution.

So when you cannot make it, you are not being feeble. You are avoiding something genuinely more demanding than typing.

What the waiting costs

Avoidance is a loan with interest. The call stays the same size; the dread grows.

You pay in the small daily hit of seeing it on the list, in the mental rehearsal, in the sleep you lose the night before you finally plan to do it, and sometimes in real consequences: a fee, a missed window, a situation that got worse while nobody asked about it.

And each day you do not call, the call gets harder, because you have now had eleven days of practice believing it is a big deal.

The two-minute setup

Most of the difficulty is in the first three seconds of the call. So prepare only enough to get through those.

Write one sentence: who you are and what you want. I am calling about the invoice dated the third, and I think there has been a mistake. That is the whole script you need for the opening.

Write down any numbers you will be asked for: reference, account, date of birth, the date of the thing. Fumbling for a number mid-call is a large share of the stress and it is completely preventable.

Write the one question you must have answered before you hang up, so that even if the call goes sideways you know what success looks like.

Then stop preparing. Rehearsing the whole conversation is not preparation; it is worry wearing a useful hat, and you cannot script the other person anyway.

Make it harder to postpone

A few things help more than resolve.

Pick a time today and treat it like an appointment, ideally early. Calls get easier the fewer hours you have spent dreading them.

Stand up when you dial. It sounds like nothing; it changes your voice and your nerve.

Dial before you feel ready. The readiness does not arrive on its own, and the first ring usually settles you faster than another ten minutes of waiting will.

If it is a long hold, put it on speaker and do something with your hands. Hold time is where most people give up, and it is the easiest part to survive if you are busy.

What to say when you get stuck

You do not have to be smooth. A few plain sentences cover almost everything.

Sorry, can you repeat that. Can you spell that for me. I do not know, can you tell me what you need. Can you hold on while I write that down. Let me check and call you back.

And the most useful one: I am not sure I have understood, can you say it another way. Nobody on the other end will think less of you for it, and getting it right matters more than sounding assured.

Afterward, close it out properly

Write down who you spoke to, the date, and what they said they would do. Thirty seconds of that saves an enormous amount later if the thing is not done.

Then notice, deliberately, what actually happened. Nearly every avoided call turns out to be shorter and more ordinary than the version you had been carrying. That is the evidence that makes the next one easier, and it only counts if you register it rather than moving straight on to the next thing you are avoiding.

If it did go badly, that is survivable too. Write the next step and put it in tomorrow rather than back on the indefinite list.

The pile, and how to work through it

If there are several, do not try to clear them in one afternoon.

One call a day, the easiest first, and the hardest third or fourth, once you have remembered that you can do this. Give yourself a fixed slot: Tuesdays at ten, two calls, done.

And consider which ones genuinely have to be a call. Plenty of things can now be done by message, form or app, and choosing the easier route is not cheating. Save the phone for the ones that really need it.

When it is bigger than a few calls

For some people this is not about phone calls at all. If making calls, answering the door, or speaking to strangers brings real dread, if unopened mail is piling up, or if avoidance has started to cost you money, health appointments or work, that pattern is worth treating rather than managing.

It responds well to practical therapy, often in a small number of sessions, and a doctor or a therapist is the right place to start. Bring the list of what you have been avoiding; it says more than any description you could give from memory.
