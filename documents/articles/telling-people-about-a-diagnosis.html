Telling People About a Diagnosis

The second job nobody prepares you for: deciding who gets told and how much. Why nobody is owed this, how to sort people into circles, and what to do with the advice that follows.

Life TransitionsBoundaries Visitor 924 words

The diagnosis has arrived. Now there is a second, separate job nobody prepares you for: deciding who gets told, how much, and when.

And people will have opinions about that too, which is part of what makes it hard. The information is yours. How it travels is a set of choices you are allowed to make deliberately.

Nobody is owed this

Start here, because it is the thing most people need permission for.

Your health information belongs to you. Not to your parents, not to your team, not to the group chat. There is no obligation to tell anybody, and no deadline for it.

The only exceptions are practical rather than moral: where somebody needs to know to keep you safe, where a condition is genuinely passed down and a relative could act on knowing, or where your employer needs a fact to arrange something you want.

Everything else is your call, and it can be made slowly.

Sort people into circles

The useful move is to stop treating this as one decision and make it several.

The inner circle gets the whole thing: one or two people, the full detail, the frightening parts.

The next circle gets the headline and the practical: I have a condition, this is what it means for the next few months, here is what would help.

The wide circle gets almost nothing, or a vaguer version: I am dealing with a health thing and I am well looked after.

Write the names down if your head is full. People find that enormously settling, because the question stops being whether to tell and becomes which circle somebody is in.

Decide your sentences in advance

You will be asked at bad moments, by people you had not planned for, and whatever you say the first time tends to become your script.

So write two versions. The short one: I am dealing with a health thing, thanks for asking, I would rather not go into it. The longer one for the people who get detail.

Then practice both out loud once. It feels silly and it means you will not be improvising at a party.

And decide what you will say to the question people cannot resist: what is the prognosis. A good answer is: I am not discussing numbers, and I will tell you if there is news.

Expect the reactions to be about them

This catches people out. You deliver news and somebody panics, cries, offers a diet, tells you about their aunt, or goes strangely quiet.

Almost none of that is a comment on you. It is what happens when people are frightened and have no script, and the worst reactions often come from the people who care most.

You do not have to manage it. You are allowed to say I cannot talk about this today, and leave the room.

The one reaction worth planning for is the advice. Somebody will send you something. A flat sentence helps: I am following my doctors on treatment. That is said once, and repeated unchanged.

Handle work as a separate question

Work needs its own thinking, because the rules there are different.

You generally do not have to say what you have. You do have to say what you need, if you want adjustments: time off, a changed schedule, a lighter load, somewhere to sit.

So separate the two. The diagnosis is private. The accommodation is a request, and it can be made without naming anything: for medical reasons I need this for the next few months.

Find out what your workplace actually offers before the conversation, and put the request in writing afterward. Occupational health, HR or an employee assistance line can tell you what exists, and they are usually more helpful than people expect.

Control the spread

Information moves, and it moves faster than anybody intends.

So say what you want out loud when you tell somebody. I am telling you and I would rather you did not pass it on. People generally respect that when it is said plainly, and generally assume the opposite when it is not.

Decide about the internet separately. A post ends the exhausting business of telling people one at a time, and it cannot be taken back. Plenty of people are glad they did it. Plenty wish they had waited a month.

If you have children, decide together with the other adults what they are told, and keep it simple and true rather than cheerful.

Let it change over time

The first version of your telling is not binding. People widen the circle as they get used to it, and sometimes narrow it again when they find the conversations tiring.

Both are allowed. You can tell somebody more later, and you can stop giving updates to anybody who has turned your health into their hobby.

When to get help

Please ask your medical team the questions you have not asked yet, however small, and ask whether a nurse specialist or a patient advocate is available to you, since they often have more time than the doctor and know what help exists locally. Talk to a therapist if the weeks since the diagnosis have been sleepless or frightening, which is extremely common and is treated well alongside your other care, and ask your team whether there is a counselor attached to the service. A support group with the same condition is worth a try, and so is one for your family, who are carrying something too and often have nowhere to put it.

If you are in crisis, call or text 988 (Suicide & Crisis Lifeline, US) or your local emergency number. Theraglee is a wellbeing tool, not a substitute for professional care.
