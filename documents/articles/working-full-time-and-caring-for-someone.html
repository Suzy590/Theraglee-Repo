Working Full Time and Caring for Someone

Appointments in your lunch break, phone calls in meetings, and no slack anywhere. How to make two full-time commitments fit, and what to tell work about it.

CaregivingWork Visitor 945 words

You take the call in the stairwell because your mother has fallen again. You have a meeting in nine minutes and a form to fill in that closes today.

Nobody at work knows the half of it, and the people at home think you have more flexibility than you do. Both jobs assume they are your only one.

Why this particular squeeze is so tiring

Care does not fit in the gaps between things. It arrives at nine fifteen on a Tuesday, in office hours, from people who only work office hours.

Doctors, pharmacies, social services, banks, the benefits office: all open exactly when you are supposed to be somewhere else. So the care comes out of your lunch breaks, your annual leave and your evenings, and the evenings were where your rest used to be.

And the mental load runs all day underneath the work. Even on a quiet week, part of your attention stays on your phone, which is why this is exhausting in a way that neither job alone would be.

Get the invisible work written down

Most people carrying this have never seen the whole of it in one place, and seeing it changes what you do next.

Spend twenty minutes listing everything: medication, appointments, transport, shopping, meals, laundry, money and paperwork, phone calls, the visits themselves, the being-on-call. Put a rough time next to each, weekly.

Two useful things come out of it. A total, which is usually far bigger than you expected and explains why you are tired. And a column of items that do not actually need you specifically, which is the start of the next step.

Hand over what does not need to be you

The tasks that only you can do are fewer than it feels like. The rest are candidates.

Repeat prescriptions can often be delivered. Shopping can be ordered. A cleaner for two hours is cheaper than the weekend you lose doing it. Pharmacies will blister-pack medication. Day centers, lunch clubs and volunteer driving services exist in most places and are badly underused.

Paid help feels extravagant until you compare it to the alternative, which is you doing it at eleven at night. And where money is the obstacle, a benefits check is worth an hour of somebody's time: carer's allowance, attendance allowance and local authority support are frequently unclaimed.

Ask for a needs assessment from your local social services and a carer's assessment for yourself. Both exist, both are free to ask for, and plenty of people carry this for years without knowing that the second one is about them.

Tell work something, even if you do not tell them everything

The instinct is to say nothing, in case it counts against you. That usually costs more than it protects.

A manager who does not know thinks you are unreliable. A manager who knows can often offer things you did not realize were available: a later start, a fixed day at home, a block of unpaid leave, flexible hours, or simply not being first in line for the early meeting.

You do not owe anyone the details. I am one of the people looking after a parent, and there are some appointments I cannot move is enough.

And find out what you are entitled to, which is more than most people assume: unpaid time off for emergencies involving a dependant, flexible working requests, and, in many workplaces, a carer's policy nobody has ever read. Human resources will know even if your manager does not.

Protect the boundary in both directions

The hard part is that both jobs expand to fill whatever you give them.

So set a couple of plain rules and keep them: no care calls in the two hours when you need to concentrate, except emergencies. No work email after you get back from a visit. One evening a week that belongs to neither.

Tell the family what the rules are, rather than hoping they will guess. People generally cooperate with a specific request and ignore a general sense that you are stretched.

And share the load where there is anyone to share it with. Siblings who live further away can take the phone calls, the paperwork, the ordering and the research, which is real work and can be done from anywhere.

Keep one thing that is yours

This is the first casualty, and the one that matters most over a long stretch.

Pick something small and fixed: a swim, a class, a walk with a friend, one coffee on a Saturday morning. Put it in the calendar as an appointment rather than a nice idea, because an unscheduled plan is the one that gets eaten.

Carer groups are worth more than they sound, too. Not for advice, mostly, but because everybody there already understands the thing you are too tired to explain.

When to get help for yourself

Please talk to a doctor if you are not sleeping, if you are getting ill repeatedly, if you are drinking more, or if you have stopped being able to feel much about any of it. Those are the standard signs of being past capacity rather than evidence that you are failing at this.

A therapist is worth considering if resentment, guilt or dread have become the loudest part of your day, or if you are grieving someone who is still here. And if the person you care for is becoming unsafe, or you are reaching the end of what you can do, say so plainly to their doctor or to social services rather than waiting until something breaks. Asking for more help is part of the job, not an admission about you.

If you are in crisis, call or text 988 (Suicide & Crisis Lifeline, US) or your local emergency number. Theraglee is a wellbeing tool, not a substitute for professional care.
