When You Only Feel Calm When You Are Busy

Rest makes you uneasy and work settles you, so you keep taking on more. Why busy can work as a sedative, what it costs, and how to build a tolerance for having nothing to do.

AnxietyBurnout Visitor 796 words

Sitting down is when it starts. The to-do list is clear, the evening is yours, and within twenty minutes there is a restlessness that only goes away when you find something to do.

So you find something. And people tell you that you work too hard, and you agree, and what you do not say is that working is the comfortable part.

Why being busy can work as a sedative

This is a real mechanism rather than a personality quirk, and understanding it takes some of the moralizing out of it.

A full mind has no room. Worry needs capacity, and a task that occupies you completely genuinely crowds it out. That is why the relief is real and not imagined.

It supplies a steady answer to how you are doing. Tasks completed is a clear measure, available hourly, and it quiets a question that is otherwise hard to answer.

And it is extremely well rewarded. Almost no other way of managing anxiety gets you praised at work, thanked at home and admired by people who have no idea what it is doing.

Then there is what shows up in the gap. For a lot of people the first quiet hour brings whatever has been deferred, and the obvious response to that is to stop having quiet hours.

What it costs

The bill arrives slowly, which is part of why this runs for years.

The tolerance goes down. The amount of stillness you can handle shrinks over time, so the thing that used to take an evening of activity now takes the whole weekend.

The rest you do get stops working. Holidays, days off and evenings are spent either braced for the restlessness or filling them, which is why people come back from a week away as tired as they left.

Relationships thin out. Not through neglect, but because being with somebody unstructured is one of the situations this makes hardest.

And the body keeps a tally regardless of how well it is going. Sleep, stomach, recurring minor illness. These tend to be the first honest reports.

Building a tolerance for nothing

The approach that works is the opposite of a big rest, which usually backfires.

Start with ten minutes, deliberately, with nothing in it. Not a task that counts as rest. Nothing. The aim is not to enjoy it, which you will not at first.

Expect the discomfort to climb and then come down. It is at its worst in the first few minutes and settles on its own if nothing is done about it, and finding that out firsthand is most of the work.

Do it regularly rather than for long. Ten minutes most days does more for the tolerance than a whole empty Sunday, which is usually unbearable and gets abandoned.

And add something low-grade rather than nothing, if nothing is too much to begin with. Sitting with tea, a slow walk with no destination, something with your hands. The requirement is only that it does not produce anything.

Taking the measurement away gradually

The other half is loosening what busy is doing for you.

Notice the sentence underneath the day. For most people it is some version of I am all right as long as I am useful, and getting it into words takes some of its force.

Collect evidence from somewhere else. Something you did badly and were still welcome, time somebody wanted you around when you were producing nothing. These exist and they do not get counted.

And watch for the swap, which is the most common failure. People give up the overwork and immediately take up intensive exercise, a renovation, a course. The activity changed and nothing else did.

What this is not

It is not laziness waiting to happen. People in this position worry that stopping would reveal that they would never start again, and that is almost never what occurs.

And it is not an argument against being busy. Plenty of people are busy because they like the work. The question is only whether you can stop, and what happens when you do.

When it is worth getting help with

If you cannot be still without real distress, if the restlessness has started arriving during the activity rather than only in the gaps, if your sleep has gone, or if you have noticed you are drinking to get the off switch to work, those are worth taking seriously.

Anxiety that has been managed by activity for years responds well to treatment, and a therapist will work on it from the practical end: what the gap brings up, and how to be in it without needing a task. Your doctor is the right person to tell if the exhaustion or the sleep has started to affect your health.
