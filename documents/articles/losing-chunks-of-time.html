Losing Chunks of Time

An hour is gone and you cannot say what you did with it. Why dissociation drops stretches of time, how to tell the ordinary kind from the kind worth checking, and practical ways to stay with yourself.

TraumaGrounding Visitor 995 words

It is twenty past three. The last thing you remember clearly is sitting down at half past one. You are in the same chair. Nothing bad has happened. But the two hours in between are not there, and no amount of searching for them brings anything back.

This is frightening in a particular way, because time is the thing we use to feel like one continuous person. When a piece of it goes missing, it can feel as though you went missing with it. You probably have not told anyone, in case it makes you sound unwell. It is worth knowing that this happens to a great many people, that it has understandable causes, and that it responds to help.

Why the mind drops time

Your sense of a continuous day is built, not recorded. Your brain lays down memory as you go, and it does that best when something has your attention and matters to you. When attention goes elsewhere for long enough, nothing gets written down, and later there is nothing to find.

Several ordinary things make this more likely. Exhaustion. Long stretches of repetitive work. Weeks of unrelenting stress. Grief. Very high anxiety, which uses up so much attention that little is left for the rest of the day.

There is also the protective kind, the one usually called dissociation. When something is too much to be fully present for, the mind steps back, and the hours around it do not get recorded. For people who lived through frightening or unsafe situations, especially when young, this was once a genuinely useful ability: a way of not being wholly there for something unbearable. The skill does not switch itself off when the danger ends. It stays available, and it can activate at times you would not choose, often when something in the present quietly resembles the past.

Some physical causes look exactly like this too. Sleep problems, certain medications, alcohol, low blood sugar, migraine, and some kinds of seizure can all take pieces out of a day. That is why the first step is a doctor rather than a search engine.

Telling the two kinds apart

The ordinary kind usually has a shape: a dull task, a long drive, a tired afternoon, and you were plainly in one place doing one thing the whole time.

Pay more attention if you find objects moved or work completed that you do not remember doing. If you come back somewhere other than where you started. If people describe conversations you have no memory of. If you find yourself dressed differently, or with injuries you cannot account for. If it is happening several times a week, or it is getting worse. Please take those to a doctor, not because they mean something terrible, but because they deserve a proper look rather than private worry.

Build an outside record

When your own memory of a stretch of time is unreliable, borrow a record from the outside. Keep a small notebook or a note on your phone and write one line every hour or two: the time, where you are, what you are doing. It takes five seconds and it does two useful things. It gives you something to read afterward instead of a blank. And the act of writing it pulls your attention back to the present for a moment, which makes the next gap less likely.

Put landmarks through the day

Gaps grow in long, featureless stretches. Breaking the day into pieces gives your memory something to hang on. An alarm every ninety minutes. Standing up and walking to the window between tasks. Drinking a glass of water. Stepping outside at the same two times each day. None of this is dramatic. All of it makes the day less like one undifferentiated block.

Come back through the senses

Thinking your way back does not work well, because thinking is the part that drifted. Sensation is more reliable and much faster. Cold water on your face or wrists. An ice cube in your hand. Feet pressed hard into the floor. Something sour or strongly minty. A rough stone or your keys in your pocket to rub between your fingers.

Then say where and when you are, out loud if you can: I am in my kitchen, it is Tuesday evening, it is October. It feels odd to do. It works, because it reinstates the exact thing the state has lost.

Track what comes before

Afterward, when thinking is available again, write down what was happening in the hour before. Over a few weeks, most people find a pattern: a certain person, a certain kind of pressure, a particular place, being very short of sleep, a specific time of year. A pattern turns something random into something you can see coming, which lets you use the tools early.

Be careful about the frightened story

The explanation people land on alone is usually the worst one available: that this means they are losing their mind, or that they are not a reliable person. Neither follows. A mind that steps back from too much is doing something protective, clumsily and at the wrong moment. It is not evidence of anything broken in your character.

If it gets dark

Losing pieces of yourself is genuinely distressing, and for some people it comes with a flat sense that nothing is real or that it would not matter what happened to them. If thoughts like that are around, please tell someone today. In the US you can call or text 988 at any time.

When to get help

Please talk to your doctor if you are losing time, so the physical causes can be ruled out, and ask about a therapist who works with trauma if the cause turns out to be something you lived through. There are people who understand this well, who will not be alarmed by it, and who can help it fade instead of becoming something you manage alone forever.

If you are in crisis, call or text 988 (Suicide & Crisis Lifeline, US) or your local emergency number. Theraglee is a wellbeing tool, not a substitute for professional care.
