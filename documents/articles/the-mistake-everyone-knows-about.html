The Mistake Everyone Knows About

You got something badly wrong and other people saw. Why a public error feels so much worse than a private one, and how to get through the weeks that follow it.

ShamePerfectionism Visitor 928 words

You sent the figures with the wrong number in them. You said the thing in the meeting. You missed the deadline that other people were waiting on, and now it has been noticed, discussed, and possibly fixed by somebody else.

It is nine in the evening and you are still going over it. Tomorrow you have to walk back in there.

Why public gets under your skin

A private mistake is a problem to solve. A public one adds an audience, and the audience is what turns it into shame.

Guilt is about the thing you did: I got that wrong, I need to fix it. Shame is about what the thing says about you: they have all seen what I am actually like. The first one can be acted on. The second just burns.

That is why the size of the error often has little to do with how bad it feels. A small mistake in front of people you want to respect you can hurt more than a large one nobody saw.

And if you have built your sense of yourself on being competent — the reliable one, the one who does not drop things — a visible error does not feel like an event. It feels like exposure.

The first day is about handling it, not about feeling better

Do the practical part early, because unfinished business feeds the rumination.

Name it plainly to whoever needs to know: I got this wrong, here is the effect, here is what I am doing about it, here is when it will be done. No long preamble, no case for the defense, no theatrical self-flagellation, which puts others in the position of comforting you.

Fix what can be fixed, and say what cannot. Then write one line about how it will not happen again, if there is a real change to make.

That conversation is almost always shorter and less terrible than the version you rehearsed, and it moves you from the thing being about your character to it being about a task.

What the replaying is doing

For a few days your mind will offer you the scene on a loop, usually at night, with commentary.

That is not processing. It is punishment, and it does not produce insight; by the fourth round you are not learning anything, you are only feeling worse. The lesson, if there is one, is usually available in ten minutes with a pen.

So take the ten minutes. What exactly went wrong, what was in my control, what will I do differently, who did I need to tell. Write it down, decide it is finished, and when the loop starts again say: that is written down, I am not doing it now.

Expect the evenings to be worse than the mornings. It gets louder when you are tired.

Get the scale right

Shame makes a terrible estimator, so check the numbers.

Other people are thinking about this far less than you imagine, not because they are kind, but because they are busy with their own week. By Friday most of them have moved on; you are the only one with a full transcript.

And look at the actual record rather than today. How many things have you got right in the last year? Would you write someone off for this if they did it? Has anyone you respect ever made a mistake like it?

Nearly everyone has a version of this, which is why people who have been around a while are usually much kinder about errors than people who have not.

Do not overcorrect

Two reactions tend to make the following months worse.

Hiding: going quiet, stopping volunteering, no longer putting your hand up. That reads as disengagement rather than conscientiousness, and it also removes the ordinary successes that would otherwise put this in proportion.

Overcompensating: double-checking everything to the point of being slow, sending apologetic emails for weeks, taking on extra to prove something. That broadcasts the mistake long after everyone else has filed it away, and it is exhausting.

The steadier move is to be visible and ordinary. Turn up, do the next piece of work well, do not refer to it again after the repair conversation.

Be careful what you build from it

A mistake teaches you something small and specific: check that file before sending, confirm the date, do not do that task at four in the afternoon.

What it does not teach you is that you are careless, unfit for the job, or found out. Those are conclusions shame draws, and they tend to stick around much longer than any actual consequence.

So keep the lesson narrow and in writing. A narrow lesson improves your work; a broad one about your character just makes you frightened of doing the work at all.

When it is more than a bad week

Please talk to a therapist or a counselor if the replaying has gone on for weeks, if you are dreading work every morning, if you have started avoiding people, or if one mistake has turned into a settled belief that you are not good enough.

Shame responds well to being said out loud to someone who does not flinch, which is a large part of why therapy helps with it. And if the low mood has brought your sleep or your appetite down with it, mention that to a doctor too; it is a common and treatable thing to come in with, and it is much easier to think clearly about one mistake once it lifts.
