When Your Job Quietly Doubled

Someone left, nobody replaced them, and their work landed on you. How to tell what your job actually is now, and how to hand some of it back without a confrontation.

BurnoutAssertiveness Visitor 945 words

Two people left last spring. Their work did not leave with them; it arrived on your desk in pieces, one reasonable request at a time, and you were competent and willing, so it stayed.

Now you work through lunch, you take things home, and you cannot point to any one thing that is unreasonable. That is what makes this so hard to raise.

Why it happens so easily

Nobody decides to double your job. It accumulates, which is why there is never a moment where saying no would have been obvious.

Each piece arrives as a favor, a temporary cover, or a small thing. Each one, on its own, is genuinely small. And a competent person who says yes becomes the obvious person to ask next time, so capability quietly becomes the reason for the load.

Managers also lose track. From above, your work looks like one line on a plan; nobody has the list of forty things you actually do, and most of them assume the old job plus the new bits is roughly the old job.

Write down what you actually do

You cannot negotiate about a feeling, and right now you have a feeling. Get a list.

Spend half an hour writing every task you own, including the invisible ones: the reporting nobody asked for formally, the system you are the only person who understands, the training of new people, the covering of the phone, the chasing.

Put a rough weekly hour estimate on each, mark which were yours a year ago and which arrived since, and total it. Most people find a number well over their contracted hours, and seeing it written down changes the conversation from complaining to arithmetic.

This document is the single most useful thing you can make here, and almost nobody makes it.

Have the conversation about priorities, not effort

The instinct is to say I am overloaded, which invites reassurance and changes nothing.

A better opening is to bring the list and ask for decisions: here is everything I am carrying, it comes to about fifty hours, here are the three things I think matter most, which of the rest should stop, move, or wait?

That does three things. It makes the load visible, it puts the choice where the authority is, and it makes you look organized rather than reluctant.

Go in with a proposal rather than only a problem. One extra pair of hands two days a week. This piece moving to another team. That report going quarterly instead of monthly. The weekly meeting that could be an email.

And ask for the title and the money if the job has genuinely changed into a bigger one. If the answer is no, that is information worth having.

Say no to the next piece, in a way that is easy to accept

Most of the growth happens in small moments, so that is where to hold the line.

I can take that on if something else comes off, which would you rather I drop? Then wait. That question is not aggressive and it is extremely hard to answer with everything.

Or: I can do it by Thursday rather than tomorrow. Or: I can do the first part, not the whole thing. Or simply: not this week.

Say it once, warmly, and do not explain for three sentences. Over-explaining invites negotiation.

And stop the silent absorbing. Doing things nobody knows about at ten at night is kind, invisible, and the reason the arrangement looks like it works.

Make the cost visible if nothing changes

If the real answer from above is to carry on, make the situation legible rather than quietly impossible.

Put things in a queue and say so: that is in the list, I expect to get to it in two weeks. Send a short monthly note of what got done and what did not. Keep a record of what you flagged and when, which matters if this ever becomes a dispute.

Stop rescuing every deadline. Deadlines that are only met by your unpaid evenings tell everyone the staffing is fine.

This feels uncomfortable and it is far better than the alternative, which is that the whole arrangement depends on you never getting ill.

Decide what you will do if it does not improve

Give it a date. If nothing has changed by the end of the quarter, then what?

Options are usually: formally reduce what you own, apply internally for the bigger role with the bigger pay, go to your union or human resources, or start looking elsewhere. Writing down which one you would pick takes some of the trapped feeling out, even before you act.

And be honest about the part that is yours. If you find it almost impossible to let a standard slip, or to be the person who did not get it done, that is worth knowing, because a workplace will happily accept an unlimited amount of that.

When to get help

Please talk to a doctor if you are not sleeping, waking at three, getting ill repeatedly, dreading Mondays from Saturday onward, or drinking more to switch off. Those are the standard warning lights, and arriving with them early is far better than arriving once you cannot work at all.

A therapist is worth it if the inability to put work down is the real engine, or if your sense of being worth something has become tied to how much you absorb. And if the load is not actually survivable, say so to someone plainly — a doctor, a union, a professional you trust — rather than waiting for the point where the decision gets made for you.
