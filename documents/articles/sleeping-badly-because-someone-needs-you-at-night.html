Sleeping Badly Because Someone Needs You at Night

When a child, a parent or a partner needs you in the small hours, the usual sleep advice does not fit. How to protect the sleep you can actually get, and how to stop running on empty.

SleepCaregiving Visitor 933 words

You are not awake because you are worrying. You are awake because someone called out, or needed turning, or wandered into the hallway, or had to be helped to the bathroom. By the time you lie down again, the night has been cut into pieces, and tomorrow starts anyway.

Most advice about sleep assumes the only thing standing between you and eight hours is your own habits. Put down the phone, keep the room cool, go to bed at the same time. None of it addresses a night that belongs partly to someone else, and being handed that advice can leave you feeling like you are failing at something you are not even being allowed to attempt.

What broken sleep actually costs

It helps to know that this is not a question of willpower. Sleep does its repair work in stretches, and a night cut into three or four pieces does less for you than the same total hours in one run. That is why you can be in bed for seven hours and still feel wrung out.

The effects are predictable and they are not character flaws. A shorter fuse. Tears arriving over small things. Forgetting words. Finding decisions harder than they should be. Feeling bleak in the early morning. If you have been blaming yourself for getting snappy or weepy, this is the reason, and it changes when the sleep changes.

Protect the first stretch

If only part of the night is yours, make that part the deepest one. The first long stretch after you fall asleep carries a lot of the restoration, so going to bed earlier is worth more than lying in, which is often not available anyway.

That usually means giving up something in the evening that feels like the only time you get to yourself. It is a real loss, and worth weighing honestly. An extra hour of sleep before midnight generally does more for tomorrow than an extra hour awake tonight.

Make the night wakings cheaper

You cannot always prevent being woken. You can often shorten how long you are up and how awake you get.

Keep the light low. A dim lamp or a nightlight in the hallway rather than the overhead light. Bright light tells your body the night is over.

Set things out beforehand. Water, medication, pads, chargers, whatever the usual call involves, within reach and in the same place every night, so you are not searching while half asleep.

Stay off your phone. Checking it once during a waking can cost you forty minutes of sleep you would otherwise have had.

Do not start calculating. Working out how much sleep you have left wakes you up further. If your mind reaches for the arithmetic, give it something duller instead: the feel of the sheet, your breath going out slowly.

Share the nights if there is anyone to share them with

If another adult is in the house, alternate. Not every other waking, which no one can keep track of in the dark, but whole nights. One of you is on duty and sleeps lightly. The other takes earplugs and a closed door and is genuinely off. Two people sleeping badly every night is worse for a household than each person having a real night in turn.

If you are doing this alone, look hard for one night of cover a week, even once a month. A relative, a friend, a paid carer for a night, a respite service. One protected night does more than you would expect, and asking is not a confession of weakness.

Nap without guilt, and do it right

A nap is not a failure to sleep properly at night. When nights are broken, daytime sleep is a legitimate repair. Twenty minutes helps alertness and mood. If you can take ninety minutes, you get a fuller cycle and wake less groggy. Avoid the forty to sixty minute range when you can, since that often ends mid-cycle and leaves you feeling worse.

Say the quiet thing out loud

Many people in this position carry a resentment they feel terrible about: a flash of anger at the person who needs them, in the middle of the night, through no fault of their own. That flash is a sign of exhaustion, not of a cold heart, and it almost always goes quiet once the sleep improves. Telling one person who will not be shocked by it takes a surprising weight off.

Ask what the nights are really for

It is worth checking whether every waking is necessary, because households build routines in a crisis and then keep them long after. Could a bed rail, a commode, a baby monitor, a motion-sensor light, a different medication schedule or a different bedtime remove one of the wakings? The person's own doctor or nurse can often change something, and nobody will suggest it unless you describe the nights in detail.

When to get help

Please tell your doctor what your nights look like, in plain numbers: woken this many times, awake this long. Say it even if you went in about something else, because broken sleep over months affects your blood pressure, your mood and your immune system, and because a doctor may be able to change the reason you are being woken. Ask about carer assessments and respite where you live. If you have been low, hopeless, or unable to see an end to it, that is worth telling a doctor or a therapist too. Caring for someone at night is real work, and you cannot keep doing it on no sleep without help.

If you are in crisis, call or text 988 (Suicide & Crisis Lifeline, US) or your local emergency number. Theraglee is a wellbeing tool, not a substitute for professional care.
