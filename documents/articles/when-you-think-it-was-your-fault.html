When You Think It Was Your Fault

I should have left sooner. I should have known. Why self-blame is so common after something frightening happens, and how to look at it with the facts you actually had.

TraumaThinking Traps Visitor 882 words

You have run it back a hundred times. The moment you could have said something. The lift you accepted. The warning you did not act on. The thing you did not notice about someone you trusted.

And every time, the verdict comes back the same: you should have known, and you did not, and therefore it was partly your fault.

Why blame lands on yourself

It is nearly universal after something frightening, and there are reasons that have nothing to do with what actually happened.

Blame feels better than helplessness. If it was something you did, then you have some control, and next time you could prevent it. The alternative — that bad things happen to people who did everything reasonably — is harder to live with.

You also know your own thoughts and nobody else's. You remember your hesitation, your doubt, the moment you nearly said something, in detail. You cannot see the other person's intent with anything like the same clarity, so your own part looks larger than it was.

And if you were ever blamed as a child for things outside your control, self-blame is a well-worn route. It arrives automatically rather than as a conclusion.

Hindsight is not evidence

The crucial distinction, and the one that does most of the work: what you know now is not what you had then.

Once you know the ending, every earlier moment looks like a signpost. The slightly odd message, the friend's comment, the feeling in the car park — all obvious, afterward. Before, they were ordinary details among hundreds of others, most of which meant nothing.

So the useful question is not what could I have done. It is: with only what I knew at the time, and feeling what I was feeling, what was a reasonable thing to do?

Write that down properly. Not the film version with all the information, but the actual moment, with the actual fog in it.

What a frightened body does

People often blame themselves hardest for how they reacted rather than for what happened.

I froze. I did not fight. I went along with it. I was polite. I did not run.

Those are not choices. Under real threat the body does whatever it calculates will get you through: freeze, appease, go quiet, comply, go somewhere else in your head. That happens faster than thought and it is not available for reconsideration at the time.

Surviving something in the way your body chose is not consent and it is not cooperation. It is what the alarm system does.

Separate regret from responsibility

Both can be true at once, and conflating them is what keeps people stuck.

Regret: I wish I had not got in the car. I wish I had told someone earlier. That is real and it is allowed, and it does not assign fault.

Responsibility: who actually did the thing. If another person chose to hurt, deceive or endanger you, the responsibility is theirs, entirely, regardless of what you were wearing, drinking, trusting or hoping.

And where an accident is nobody's fault at all, the honest answer is that it happened, and no amount of reassigning it will make it make sense.

Check the standard you are using

Ask whether you would hold anyone else to it.

If a friend told you exactly this story, with the same information available at the time, what would you say about their part in it? People in this position are almost always applying a standard to themselves that they would call absurd if it were applied to anybody else.

And notice the particular shapes self-blame takes. If only, which rewrites history. I should have known, which assumes information you did not have. I always, which turns one event into a character. Those are patterns rather than facts, and naming them takes some of their authority away.

Say it out loud to one person

Self-blame survives best in silence. It grows in the retelling you only do in your own head.

So tell one person who will not flinch: a friend, a counselor, a helpline, a group of people who have been through something similar. What most people find is not that they are talked out of it, but that saying it to another human loosens its grip.

Expect it to come back. This is rarely settled once; it is settled repeatedly, a bit more each time, usually over months.

When to get help with it

Please talk to a doctor or a therapist if the blame will not shift, if the memories are intruding during the day, if your sleep has broken down, or if you are avoiding places, people or ordinary things to keep the thoughts away.

Trauma treatment is specific and it works, including years afterward, and working on self-blame is one of the standard parts of it rather than something you have to get past first. Say plainly to whoever you see that you hold yourself responsible; it is a common thing to bring and it tells them exactly where to start.

And if it has taken you somewhere dark, and you are not sure you want to be here, please tell a doctor or someone you trust today. In the US you can call or text 988 at any time.

If you are in crisis, call or text 988 (Suicide & Crisis Lifeline, US) or your local emergency number. Theraglee is a wellbeing tool, not a substitute for professional care.
