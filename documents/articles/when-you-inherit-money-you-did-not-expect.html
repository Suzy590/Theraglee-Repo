When You Inherit Money You Did Not Expect

Money arrives because somebody died, and nothing about having it feels straightforward. Why it sits so oddly, what to do with it in the first year, and how to handle the people around it.

MoneyGrief Visitor 883 words

A letter arrives, or a lawyer calls, and there is a sum of money that is now yours. More than you have had before, or enough to change something, or enough that you have had to look at it twice.

And instead of relief there is a kind of static. You cannot be straightforwardly pleased, because of where it came from, and you cannot be straightforwardly sad, because there is money on the table. Most people find they are not able to feel either thing cleanly.

Why it sits so strangely

It is worth knowing that almost nobody experiences this simply, including people who badly needed the money.

The two things arrive together. The loss and the gain are the same event, so every good feeling about one is interrupted by the other, and there is no order in which to take them.

It can feel like payment. Something in the arithmetic reads as an exchange, and that is uncomfortable even when you know it is nonsense.

It arrives at the worst possible time for decisions. Grief in the first months does specific things to judgment, attention and memory, and that is exactly when the paperwork demands answers.

And it is often entangled with the person. Money from somebody who was careful with it, or chaotic with it, or who you fought with about it, carries all of that into your account along with the balance.

The first year

Almost every piece of good advice about inherited money is some version of slow down.

Put it somewhere boring and leave it. A savings account you cannot easily reach. The decision not to decide is itself a decision, and it is usually the right one for the first six to twelve months.

Deal only with what has a deadline. Tax, probate paperwork, anything the estate actually requires. The rest can wait, and most of it will look different later.

Expect to want to do something dramatic. Quit, move, give it all away, spend it fast so it stops sitting there. Those impulses are common and they are usually about the grief rather than about the money.

And get one piece of professional advice before anything large. An accountant or a financial planner, once, paid for by the hour. The tax position on inherited money is not intuitive and the cost of getting it wrong is larger than the fee.

What to do about the guilt

This is the part people do not say out loud, and it is almost universal.

Notice whether you are refusing to use it as a form of loyalty. Plenty of people leave an inheritance untouched for years because spending it feels like agreeing that the person is gone.

Ask what they would have wanted, and be honest about the answer. For most people, the answer is that they wanted you to have it and use it. That is usually the whole point of leaving it.

Consider spending a small part of it deliberately and visibly on something good. Many people find this is what breaks the deadlock, and it does not have to be large.

And separate the guilt from any actual wrong. If nothing unfair happened, the discomfort is grief wearing a different coat, not a verdict on you.

The people around it

Money reshapes families, and this is where most of the lasting damage happens.

Keep the amount private, including from people who ask directly. You are not obliged to say, and once it is known it cannot be unknown.

Expect requests, and decide your position before one arrives rather than in the moment. Having a settled answer makes it much easier to say.

Be careful with early gifts to family. Generosity in the first months is often grief looking for somewhere to go, and it sets a precedent you may not want.

And where there is a sibling who got a different amount, that is a conversation worth having directly and early. Silence on this reliably turns into a decade of resentment.

If the money is tangled with the relationship

Sometimes the inheritance is from somebody you were not close to, or who hurt you, or who you had not spoken to in years.

That version is harder, not easier, because the money arrives with an unfinished relationship attached and no one left to settle it with.

You are allowed to take it. Accepting money is not forgiveness, it is not a statement about them, and it does not revise the history.

And you are allowed to use it for something entirely your own, without any gesture toward their memory, if that is what you want.

When to talk to someone

If the money has become the main thing you think about, if you cannot make any decision at all, if you are spending in a way that frightens you, or if the grief has not moved at all since it arrived, that is worth taking to somebody.

A therapist is useful here because the money is rarely the real subject, and grief that is stuck around an object or a sum is a recognizable thing rather than an unusual one. Your doctor can point you toward local grief services, many of them free. In the US you can call or text 988 at any time.

If you are in crisis, call or text 988 (Suicide & Crisis Lifeline, US) or your local emergency number. Theraglee is a wellbeing tool, not a substitute for professional care.
