Flying When You Are Frightened of It

The dread starts weeks before and peaks at the gate. What the fear is actually made of, what the noises mean, and how to get through a flight you have already booked.

PanicThinking Traps Visitor 938 words

The holiday is booked and you are already dreading it. Not the trip, the three hours in the air. You check the weather for the route. You read about the aircraft type. You lie awake the night before, and at the gate you look at the plane through the glass and think about turning around.

Then every bump in the air becomes evidence, and you spend the flight gripping an armrest and watching the cabin crew's faces for a sign.

What the fear is usually made of

Fear of flying is rarely one fear. It helps to work out which pieces are yours, because they need different handling.

Fear of a crash, which is a fear about the aircraft.

Fear of panicking, of being trapped, of being unable to get off or get help, which is really a fear of your own body and of confinement rather than of flying.

Fear of heights, of turbulence, of not being in control, of being judged for being frightened.

Most people who think they are afraid of crashing are substantially afraid of the second one. That matters, because fear of panicking responds to panic treatment, which is highly effective, and not to statistics about aviation.

Get the facts about the noises

A great deal of in-flight fear is misread information. Knowing what the ordinary sounds are removes a lot of it, because you stop reading routine events as warnings.

Turbulence is uncomfortable air, not damage. Aircraft are built to flex and the wings are designed to move. Cabin crew sit down because carrying hot drinks is unpleasant in it, not because something is wrong.

The thumps and whines after takeoff are the wheels retracting and the flaps coming in. The engine note dropping a few minutes after takeoff is a normal power reduction. The bump on landing is intentional firmness.

If the specifics help you, airline-run fear of flying courses go through all of it with a pilot in the room and they are among the most useful things available.

Stop the pre-flight rituals that feed it

The checking is not preparation, it is reassurance-seeking, and reassurance wears off faster each time.

So: no reading about accidents, no checking the weather for the route, no tracking the aircraft's history, no watching takeoff videos. Each of those gives twenty minutes of calm and strengthens the idea that you need information to be safe.

Alcohol is also worth skipping. It makes sleep worse, dehydrates you, and makes the panic response more likely later in the flight.

Have a plan for your body, not for the airplane

You cannot control the aircraft. You can control what your body does with the adrenaline, and that is where the work pays off.

Breathe out longer than you breathe in, which lowers your heart rate when nothing else will. Press your feet into the floor and your back into the seat. Hold something cold. Keep your hands busy with something that needs a little attention: a puzzle, a game, a book you can actually follow.

Air vents on your face help a lot of people, as does a cold drink and taking a layer off, because heat makes panic worse.

Let the wave crest rather than fighting it

Panic peaks and falls, on a plane exactly as on the ground. The thing that makes it last is fighting it and watching for it.

So when it starts, name it: this is panic, it is extremely unpleasant, it is not dangerous, it will peak and pass. Then keep doing whatever you were doing, badly, rather than stopping everything to manage the feeling.

Tell the cabin crew

This one surprises people. Telling a flight attendant quietly that you are a nervous flyer gets you noticed in the right way: a check-in, a word about the turbulence, somewhere to look. They deal with this on most flights and nobody thinks anything of it.

Practical choices that genuinely help

A seat over the wing, which moves least. An aisle seat if being hemmed in is the problem, a window if looking out helps you. A morning flight, since afternoon air is bumpier in warm weather. A direct flight rather than two takeoffs and two landings. Someone with you who knows and will not tease you.

Be careful with medication as the whole plan. Something prescribed for one flight can help you get on the plane, and relying on it every time tends to leave the fear intact, because you never find out you could do it. Worth a conversation with your doctor rather than a borrowed pill.

Go, if you can

Fear of flying shrinks lives quietly: jobs not taken, weddings missed, family not visited. The thing that reliably reduces it is flying, several times, while using the tools, and letting your nervous system find out what actually happens.

If avoidance has gone on for years, do not expect to resolve that alone the week before a wedding. Get help in advance.

When to get help

Please talk to your doctor or a therapist if fear of flying is shaping what you do with your life, or if the fear is really about panic, confinement or losing control, since those have specific and effective treatments. Cognitive behavioral therapy for panic and phobias works well and does not take years. Airline courses help many people, and a pilot explaining turbulence does more than any amount of private research. If the dread has left you hopeless or you have had thoughts of not wanting to be here, please tell someone today. In the US you can call or text 988 at any time.

If you are in crisis, call or text 988 (Suicide & Crisis Lifeline, US) or your local emergency number. Theraglee is a wellbeing tool, not a substitute for professional care.
