What Actually Happens in a First Therapy Session

If you have been putting off therapy because you do not know what to expect, this is a plain walk through the first session: what gets asked, what you have to say, and what you do not.

AnxietySelf-Care Visitor 819 words

A lot of people who would benefit from therapy never go, and it is often not because they do not want help. It is because they do not know what they are walking into. Will I have to talk about my childhood? Will I cry? What if I do not know what to say? What if the therapist thinks my problems are too small?

Here is what a first session is usually like, so that the unknown is a little smaller.

Before you arrive

Most therapists send a few forms ahead of time: contact details, a short questionnaire about how you have been feeling, and a document explaining confidentiality and fees. Fill them out honestly but do not agonize. They are a starting point, not a test.

You do not need to prepare a speech. If it helps, jot down one or two sentences about why you are going. I have been anxious for months. I am not handling my dad's illness well. I keep having the same fight with my partner. That is enough.

The first few minutes

The therapist will usually start by explaining how they work and what confidentiality means, including the few situations where they would have to break it, such as an immediate risk to your safety. Then they will ask some version of, so what brings you here?

You can answer as simply as you like. Many people start with, I am not really sure where to begin. That is a completely fine beginning, and a good therapist will help you from there with questions.

What they will ask

The first session is mostly the therapist getting to know you. Expect questions about what is going on now, how long it has been going on, what you have tried, how you are sleeping and eating, what your days look like, who is in your life, and whether anything like this has happened before. They may ask a little about your family and history, but you get to decide how much you share, and it is fine to say, I would rather not get into that yet.

They will also probably ask what you are hoping to get out of therapy. If you do not know, say so. Figuring that out together is part of the work.

What you do not have to do

You do not have to cry, and you do not have to hold it in if you do. You do not have to tell them everything. You do not have to have a diagnosis or a tidy explanation for how you feel. You do not have to be in crisis; wanting to feel better, or understand yourself, or handle something specific, is more than enough reason to be there.

And you do not have to be sure this therapist is the right one. The first session is an audition in both directions.

Notice how it feels

Pay attention to whether you felt heard. Did they let you finish? Did they seem interested in you, not just your symptoms? Did you leave feeling a little lighter, or at least a little less alone with it? It is normal to feel awkward, drained, or exposed after a first session. It is not normal to feel judged or dismissed. Trust that difference.

If it did not feel right, that does not mean therapy is not for you. It means that therapist may not be. Finding the right fit sometimes takes two or three tries, and the search is worth it. Most therapists will not be offended if you say you are going to try someone else.

The end of the session

Near the end, the therapist will usually summarize what they heard, suggest how they might work with you, and ask whether you would like to book another session. You can say yes, say you want to think about it, or ask questions about their approach. Good questions include: how do you usually work with something like this, how often would we meet, and how will we know if it is helping?

What the second session is like

The second session is usually easier. The introductions are done, and the real conversation begins. Many people find that the dread was almost entirely about the first one, and that once they have been through it, going back feels ordinary.

If you have been putting it off

The gap between deciding to look for a therapist and actually sitting in the room is where most people get stuck. If you have been circling it, pick one small step today: search for one name, send one email, ask your doctor for one referral. You do not have to be ready. You just have to make the first appointment, and the rest follows from there. A therapist is exactly the professional to help with whatever has been weighing on you, and the first session is the door.
