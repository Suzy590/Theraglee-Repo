Asking for More Money at Work

You know you are underpaid and the thought of raising it makes you feel sick. How to prepare, what to actually say, and how to handle a no without it becoming a verdict on you.

AssertivenessSelf-Esteem Visitor 910 words

You have done the work. You have probably been doing more of it than your title says for a while. And you have rehearsed the conversation in the shower eleven times and never had it, because every version of it in your head ends in humiliation.

So the year goes by, and the small raise arrives automatically, and the feeling of being quietly taken for granted settles in deeper.

Why this is harder than other requests

Money at work sits on top of something tender: the belief that you are worth something, and a fear that asking will reveal that you are not.

People also confuse two separate things. What you are worth as a person, which is not a number, and what the market pays for this work in this place, which is a number and has nothing to do with your character. Keeping those apart makes the conversation enormously easier.

Find out the actual number first

Walking in without data turns the conversation into a feeling, and feelings lose.

Look at job listings for your role in your area, right now. Ask people in your field, including a recruiter, who will tell you plainly what they place people at. Look at the salary band for your level if your organization publishes them. Check what the job would be advertised at if you left tomorrow.

Then pick a specific figure rather than a vague more, and have a slightly higher ask with a number you would accept behind it.

Build the case on what you did, not on what you need

Your rent going up is real and it is not an argument your manager can take to anyone. What they need is a case they can repeat upward.

So write down four or five things, with specifics. The thing you now run that you did not run a year ago. The work you absorbed when someone left. A number if you have one: money brought in, time saved, customers kept, errors prevented. The things people come to you for.

Then one sentence tying it together: I am doing a bigger job than I was hired for, and I am paid below the market for it.

Ask for the meeting properly

Do not raise it in a corridor or at the end of an unrelated call. Book time and say what it is for.

I would like twenty minutes to talk about my pay. Giving notice is not a threat, it is a courtesy, and it also means they arrive prepared instead of defensive.

Timing helps. Budgets get set at particular points, and your manager usually knows when. Asking a month before the cycle beats asking a week after it closes.

Say the ask plainly and then be quiet

Open with it rather than building up for five minutes.

Based on what I am doing now and what the market pays, I am asking for X. Here is the case.

Then stop. Let the silence be theirs. People talk themselves down in the pause, adding or maybe a bit less, or it is fine if not. Do not negotiate against yourself before they have said a word. Nothing you add in that silence will help you.

A no is usually about the system

Most noes are about budget cycles, bands, a freeze, or someone above your manager, and almost none are a judgment about your worth.

So treat a no as the start of the next question: what would need to be true for this to be a yes, and when can we look at it again. Ask for specifics, write them down, and send a short note afterward confirming what you both said. That note is what makes the second conversation much easier.

If money is genuinely impossible, other things have value: a title change, a training budget, a day at home, time off, a defined path to the next level. A title change in particular is often cheap to give and worth real money the next time you move.

Do not let it curdle quietly

If the answer is no with nothing behind it, and this has happened repeatedly, that is information. The strongest position in any pay conversation is knowing what you would do if the answer is no, which usually means knowing what else is out there.

You do not have to threaten anything. You only have to stop feeling trapped, and quietly looking is what does that.

Separate the outcome from how you did

People leave these conversations deciding they were humiliating when in fact they went fine. Rate the asking, not the answer. You booked it, you said a number, you stayed in the room. That is the part you controlled and it gets markedly easier the second time.

When to get help

If dread of this conversation is keeping you in a job that underpays you, or if the thought of asking for anything brings on a level of fear that is out of proportion, that is worth taking to a therapist, since it is usually part of a wider pattern with asking and it responds well to practice with someone. Please talk to your doctor if money worry is keeping you awake or has pulled your mood down for weeks, and if the underpayment is tied to your sex, race, age or disability, get proper advice from a union or an employment adviser rather than treating it as a confidence problem.

If you are in crisis, call or text 988 (Suicide & Crisis Lifeline, US) or your local emergency number. Theraglee is a wellbeing tool, not a substitute for professional care.
