When You Are Caring for Two Generations at Once

A parent who needs more and children who still need you now, at the same hours. Why this is different from caring for one person, how to sort the demands, and the decisions worth making in advance.

CaregivingDecision Making Visitor 947 words

There is a parent who needs more than they did last year, and there are children who need you now, and there is a job, and all three are asking at the same hours of the same days.

Nobody plans for this and a great many people end up in it. What makes it specific is not the amount of work. It is that every hour you give to one is visibly taken from another, so there is no version of the day that feels like you did enough.

Why it is different from caring for one person

Worth naming, because people in this position often measure themselves against someone caring for a parent alone and conclude they are coping badly.

The demands are not stackable. Two needs at the same hour cannot be done one after the other, and a lot of days come down to choosing, repeatedly, in front of everyone.

The guilt runs in two directions. Whatever you choose, somebody is getting the lesser version, and that is a different feeling from being merely tired.

The timescales are opposite. Children are moving toward needing you less. A parent is usually moving the other way. So the pressure does not resolve on its own the way the first kind does.

And you are the connection point. Your parent and your children are usually not organizing anything between them. Every arrangement runs through you, which is where the real load sits.

Sorting the demands rather than meeting them

The instinct is to work harder. The thing that actually helps is deciding, in advance, what is not going to be done.

Write down everything you are currently doing for each of them, including the invisible parts, and mark each one must, should, or would like to. Most people find a third of the list is would like to, and that list is the first place to find hours.

Decide what good enough looks like for each, out loud. Not the best version. Something you could sustain for two years, since that is the sort of stretch this usually runs.

And protect the few things that matter most to each person rather than spreading evenly. Children usually need a reliable small thing more than a large irregular one. A parent usually needs a predictable visit more than a longer one.

Getting other people into it

This is where most of the available relief is, and it is the part people skip, usually because asking feels like admitting something.

Ask for specific, repeating help rather than general offers. Could you take Tuesday evenings. Could you handle the pharmacy. An open-ended could you help never turns into anything.

Give siblings real jobs even at a distance. Paperwork, insurance, appointments by phone, ordering things. Distance is a weak excuse and a lot of this does not need anybody present.

Find out what is actually available before you decide you cannot afford it. Adult day programs, respite care, visiting services and local agencies on aging exist, and a surprising number of people in this position have never checked.

And tell your employer something. Many people carry this entirely in secret and discover far too late that flexible hours or leave were available.

What to tell the children

Children in this situation usually know more than they are told and understand less than they need to.

Say something plain and true, pitched to their age. Grandma is not well, I have to help her, that is why I am out some evenings. Vagueness is more frightening than facts.

Give them a job if they want one, and let them refuse. Being useful helps children a great deal, and being conscripted does not.

Protect something small and regular that is theirs. Ten minutes at bedtime that nothing interrupts is worth more than an occasional big day out.

And let them see you accepting help. What they take from this stretch is mostly about whether asking is allowed.

The decisions that keep arriving

This kind of caregiving is a series of decisions rather than one, and they get easier with a method.

Decide what you will not do before you are asked, because the answer is hardest to find in the moment. Knowing you will not have anyone move in, or that you will not give up your job, is not cold. It is what makes the rest sustainable.

Ask what a decision is actually for, and who it is serving. A lot of choices in this territory get made to manage somebody's guilt rather than anybody's wellbeing, and naming that changes the answer.

And revisit on a schedule rather than in a crisis. A set date every few months to look at what is working is far better than the version where everything changes after a fall.

When you are running on nothing

This is the part people in the middle almost never say out loud, so it is worth saying for them.

If you cannot remember the last time you did something that was not for somebody else, if you are getting ill repeatedly, if you are snapping at people you love, or if you have stopped seeing anyone outside the house, that is not a character problem. It is the predictable result of this arrangement, and it is the point at which it stops being sustainable.

A therapist is useful here, and so are caregiver support groups, which many people dismiss and then find to be the most useful hour of their month. Your doctor is the right person to tell if the exhaustion has started to affect your health, and they can usually point you to the local services as well.

If you are in crisis, call or text 988 (Suicide & Crisis Lifeline, US) or your local emergency number. Theraglee is a wellbeing tool, not a substitute for professional care.
