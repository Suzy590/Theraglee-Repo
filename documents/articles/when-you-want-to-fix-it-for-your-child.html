When You Want to Fix It for Your Child

Watching your child struggle with something they could manage is genuinely hard. How to tell the difference between a struggle worth leaving and one worth ending, and what to do with your own discomfort.

ParentingResilience Visitor 854 words

Your child is struggling with something, and you can see exactly how to fix it. One phone call. One intervention. Five minutes of doing it yourself instead of watching them do it badly for forty.

Not fixing it feels like neglect. Fixing it feels like relief. And a quiet voice somewhere says you are not supposed to, which is not very helpful at six in the evening when they are crying.

This is one of the genuinely hard parts, and it is not solved by a rule.

What struggling builds, and what it does not

The useful thing is not the difficulty itself. Difficulty on its own just teaches that things are difficult.

What builds something is getting through a hard thing with enough support that the outcome is reachable. That is where a child learns the thing worth learning, which is not I am tough but hard things can be got through and I have some say in it.

So the question is never whether to let them struggle. It is whether this particular struggle is one they can get through, with the support available, at the size they currently are.

A rough test before you step in

Three questions sort most situations quickly.

Can they do it, slowly and badly, with help. If yes, help rather than take over. Badly and theirs beats well and yours, almost every time, on anything that will come around again.

Is the cost of failing recoverable. A forgotten assignment, a lost game, an awkward conversation, a project that turns out worse than it could have. Those are the costs that teach. Safety, health, a situation with an adult who has power over them, anything that would mark them for years: those are not lessons, and stepping in is simply your job.

Are they asking. A child who has asked for help and a child who is refusing it are in different situations, and the second one usually needs a smaller offer rather than a bigger rescue.

If the answers say leave it, the rest of this is about the hard part, which is you.

What to do with your own discomfort

Watching it is often worse than the thing itself. That is worth naming honestly, because a great many rescues happen to relieve the parent rather than the child.

Before you step in, ask whose discomfort is driving this. If the honest answer is mine, that is not a reason to abandon them. It is a reason to wait ten minutes and see whether the urge is still there.

Notice, too, when the fear is about how it looks. Whether another parent will see the uncut hair, the wrong outfit, the missed deadline. That is a real pressure and it is not a reason to take a problem away from a child who was managing it.

And watch for the worry about what it means. A child struggling with something ordinary is not evidence about your parenting or about how their life will go. It is a Tuesday.

Helping without taking over

There is a lot of room between fixing it and standing back.

Narrow the problem. Not what are you going to do about the whole project, but what is the next single thing.

Lend the part they do not have yet. Sit beside them while they make the phone call. Write the first sentence and let them write the rest. Drive them there and let them go in alone.

Ask before advising. What do you think you will do first is a better opening than here is what I would do, and it tells you whether they are stuck or just upset.

Let them hear that you think they can. Not as cheerleading, which lands as pressure, but as a plain observation: this is hard and I think you will figure it out, and I am here.

And be a reliable place to come back to. The strongest version of this is a child who tries something, gets it wrong, and comes home knowing they will not be met with I told you so.

When stepping in is right

Do it without agonizing when it is needed: when they are overwhelmed rather than challenged, when they have asked plainly, when the stakes have moved past recoverable, when they are unwell, or when something has gone on long enough that they are losing rather than learning.

Taking over for a while is not a failure of nerve. It is what support looks like sometimes, and children who are caught when it matters take more risks, not fewer.

When to get another view

If your child's struggle has become constant, if they have stopped trying at things they used to attempt, or if the anxiety of watching is taking over your own week, it is worth talking to someone. Their teacher often sees patterns you cannot from home. A doctor or a child therapist is the right call if the difficulty has lasted months, and a therapist of your own is a perfectly reasonable thing to want when the watching is the part you cannot carry.
