Talking to a Teenager Who Will Not Talk

How was your day? Fine. What is wrong? Nothing. If every conversation with your teenager ends in one word, here is what is actually going on, and how to get more than a shrug.

ParentingCommunication Visitor 871 words

A few years ago they told you everything. Now you get fine, nothing, I do not know, and a bedroom door. You ask about school and get a shrug. You ask if they are okay and get an eye roll. You lie awake wondering whether the silence is normal or whether something is wrong and they are not telling you.

Both of those can be true at once, which is what makes this so hard. Here is what is going on behind the door, and some ways in that work better than asking how their day was.

The silence is doing a job

A teenager pulling back from a parent is not a sign the relationship is broken. It is part of what teenagers are built to do. They are working out who they are apart from you, and the first tool they reach for is privacy. Every fine and nothing is a small act of building a self that is theirs.

That does not make it pleasant. But it helps to know that the quiet is a stage of development, not a verdict on you. The parents who take it personally and push harder usually get more silence. The ones who understand it and stay steadily available usually get let back in, in time.

Stop asking questions

This is the counterintuitive one. Questions, even loving ones, can feel like interrogation to a teenager, and interrogation produces one-word answers. How was school? What did you do? Who were you with? Each one closes a door.

Try replacing questions with observations and offers. You seem tired. That test sounded rough. I am making tea if you want some. I am around tonight if you want to watch something. These give them a way to come toward you without having to answer anything.

Be around without an agenda

Teenagers rarely talk on demand. They talk sideways, at odd moments, usually when you are not looking at them. In the car. While you are cooking and they are wandering through. Late at night when they should be in bed. The trick is to be present and unhurried in those moments, and to let them start.

That means not filling every silence. Sit in the room. Drive without the radio. Say nothing for a while. It is uncomfortable, and it works far better than any question.

Do not fix it

When they do open up, the fastest way to close them again is to solve it. Well, you should just tell her. Why did you not study earlier? That is easy, here is what you do. Even good advice, delivered too fast, tells a teenager that you were not really listening, only waiting to fix.

Instead, reflect back what you heard. That sounds really frustrating. So she said that in front of everyone? Then wait. If they want advice, they will ask, or you can offer it later: do you want ideas, or do you just want me to listen? Ask that out loud. They will tell you.

Keep your reaction small

If you flinch, they stop. A teenager who tells you something worrying and sees panic on your face learns that telling you is expensive. You do not have to hide your feelings, but hold them steady in the moment. Thanks for telling me. I am glad you did. Then breathe, and deal with your own reaction later, away from them.

Say the important things anyway

Even when you get nothing back, keep saying the things that matter. I love you. I am proud of how you handled that. You can always tell me anything, and I will not lose it. These land even when they are met with a grunt. Teenagers hear far more than they show, and the words go into a reserve they draw on later.

Respect the door, mostly

Privacy is part of what they are building, and respecting it, within reason, is how you show that you trust them. Knock. Do not read the journal. Do not demand the phone at every turn. The exceptions are real safety concerns, and those are worth crossing a line for, but they are the exception, and you will know them when they come.

Watch for what is not normal

Ordinary teenage withdrawal is moody, private, and mixed with moments of ordinary life: laughing with friends, eating, caring about something. What is worth more attention is a change: a bright kid going flat for weeks, pulling away from friends and not just from you, sleeping all the time or not at all, losing interest in everything, getting into trouble that is unlike them, or saying things that sound hopeless.

If you see that, do not wait for them to talk. Say what you have noticed, gently and specifically, and say that you want to get them some help. A therapist who works with teenagers can reach a kid who cannot talk to a parent right now, and that is not a failure on your part. It is often the best gift you can give them. And if they say anything that frightens you about their safety, act on it that day. In the US you can call or text 988 at any time.
