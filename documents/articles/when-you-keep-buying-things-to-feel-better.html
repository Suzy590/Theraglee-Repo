When You Keep Buying Things to Feel Better

The lift lasts four minutes and the feeling is still there. What the buying is actually doing, where to put friction so willpower is not the plan, and why shame is fuel rather than brakes.

ShameEmotion Regulation Visitor 864 words

The parcel arrives and there is a small lift for about four minutes. Then it is a thing in a bag in the hall, and the feeling it was supposed to fix is exactly where it was.

You know the pattern. You have promised yourself you will stop. And yet the half hour before bed, or the bad afternoon, or the argument that went nowhere, tends to end with a basket and a confirmation email.

What the buying is actually doing

This is not greed and it is not stupidity, and treating it as either is why the promising-to-stop approach keeps failing.

Buying something does three things fast. It gives you a decision you control at a moment when very little feels controllable. It gives you a small hit of anticipation, which is a genuine lift. And it gives you a few minutes of being someone who is sorting their life out.

Look at the sequence and it is almost always the same: a feeling arrives, the feeling is uncomfortable, the buying interrupts it. That is the mechanism, and it works, briefly, which is exactly why it repeats.

Find out which feeling it answers

The useful question is not why am I like this. It is what was happening ten minutes before.

For most people it is one of a small number of things. Tired. Bored on a long empty evening. Lonely. Flattened after a bad day at work. Angry with somebody and with nowhere to put it. Or ashamed of how your home or your body or your life looks, in which case the thing you buy is a promise that it will be different.

Notice it for a week without changing anything. Just the time, the feeling, and what you bought. That record on its own usually tells you more than any advice.

Put friction where the decision happens

Willpower at the moment of buying is the weakest point in the chain, so do not fight there. Make the moment slower instead.

Delete the apps from your phone and use a browser, which adds steps. Take the saved cards off, so each purchase needs the physical card. Turn off the emails and the notifications that create a reason to look. Unfollow the accounts that make you want things.

Then the rule that does the most work: put it in the basket and leave it for a day. Not never, just not now. A surprising amount of it does not survive the night, and what does survive you can buy without the shame.

Give the feeling somewhere else to go

Taking the behavior away and leaving the feeling in place rarely holds, because the feeling was the reason.

So have two or three things ready for the usual hour. A shower. A walk around the block. A phone call to a particular person. Ten minutes of something with your hands. Getting into bed with a book at nine because you are tired and tired was the whole problem.

None of these is as quick as buying something, which is the honest difficulty. They last longer, which is the honest benefit.

Deal with the money plainly

Shame keeps this in the dark, and in the dark it grows.

So look at the number. Three months of statements, totalled. It will be uncomfortable and it is better than not knowing, because a vague sense of overspending produces anxiety, and a figure produces decisions.

If there is debt, a free debt adviser is the right call, and it is a practical conversation rather than a telling-off. If there is not, set a monthly amount that is yours to spend on wanting things, with no justification required, and keep it separate. Permitted spending tends to shrink. Forbidden spending tends to burst.

Deal with the unopened bags too. Many people have a cupboard of things still in packaging, and opening it or returning what can be returned does a surprising amount for how you feel.

Go gently with yourself about it

Almost everybody has some version of this, and the ones who get on top of it are not the ones who are hardest on themselves.

Shame is actually part of the loop. You feel bad about last week's spending, feeling bad is uncomfortable, and the thing that reliably interrupts discomfort is buying something. Self-criticism is fuel here, not brakes.

So treat a slip as information about what the hard hour is, and keep the record going. Progress looks like fewer of them, not none.

When to get help

Please talk to a therapist if the buying is something you hide, if it is causing debt or arguments at home, or if you have tried to stop more than once and could not, because compulsive spending responds well to treatment and it is treated as a real thing rather than a weakness. Mention it to your doctor too if it arrived alongside a low or unusually high mood, a new medication, or a stretch of not sleeping, since that pattern is worth looking at properly. For the money itself, a free debt adviser is the most useful professional you can call, and sooner is considerably easier than later.

If you are in crisis, call or text 988 (Suicide & Crisis Lifeline, US) or your local emergency number. Theraglee is a wellbeing tool, not a substitute for professional care.
