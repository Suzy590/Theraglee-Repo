The Thing You Have Never Told Anyone

Most people carry something they have never said out loud. Why the keeping is often heavier than the thing itself, and how to begin setting it down without telling the whole world.

ShameSelf-Compassion Visitor 887 words

There is probably something. A thing you did, or something that was done to you, or a thought you have had. You have carried it for years, maybe most of your life. Nobody knows. And some part of you is certain that if anyone found out, they would see you differently forever.

Almost everyone has one of these. That is worth sitting with for a moment, because the thing shame insists on most strongly is that you are the only one.

What secrecy does

The secret itself may be small or serious. What makes it heavy is the keeping.

Every relationship you have is slightly filtered through it: there is a part of you nobody is allowed near, so closeness always stops at a certain depth. You are watchful, in case a conversation gets close. And you cannot get any perspective on it, because it has never been outside your own head, where it has been growing unchallenged for years.

Guilt says I did something bad. Shame says I am something bad. Secrets almost always feed the second one, and the second one is what wears a person down.

The story has been unchecked for a long time

Whatever this is, you have been the only one interpreting it. And you have been interpreting it with the least generous reading available, probably from the perspective of whoever you were when it happened, often a child or a much younger person.

If it happened when you were young, consider that a child cannot be responsible for an adult's behavior, whatever they were told and however they behaved afterward. If it was something you did, consider whether the judgment you pass on yourself is one you would pass on anyone else who did the same thing at the same age in the same situation. You are almost certainly applying a standard you apply to no one else.

Say it to yourself first

Before anyone else hears it, let it exist outside your head. Write it down in plain words, exactly what happened, with no softening and no self-attack. Read it back. Then, if you can, write what you would say to a friend who handed you that same page.

This is not a substitute for telling someone. But it is often the first time the thing has had edges, rather than being a fog of dread, and edges make it smaller.

Then tell one person

One. Not everyone, not publicly, not on the internet. One person, chosen carefully: someone who has shown over time that they can be trusted with something, who is not entangled in the situation, and whose reaction you can survive.

For many people the safest first person is a therapist, precisely because they have no stake in your life, have heard a great deal, and are bound to keep it. If that feels impossible, a helpline, a support group, or a religious counselor can serve the same purpose. What matters is that it leaves your head and lands somewhere without the world ending.

What usually happens when you tell

People who finally say the thing out loud tend to report a few things. The other person was far less shocked than expected. The actual telling took about ninety seconds after years of dread. And the relief afterward was physical, as if something had been put down.

That is not a guarantee. Sometimes people react badly, which is why the choosing matters. But the near-universal experience is that the anticipated catastrophe does not come.

You do not owe it to everyone

Setting something down is not the same as broadcasting it. You get to decide who knows, and it is fine for the answer to be one or two people forever. Telling is for your sake, not as a penance or a debt to the world.

If it involves harm you did

If what you are carrying is something you did that hurt someone, the relief is real but so is the repair. Once it is out of the dark and you can think about it clearly, you can consider whether there is an amends to make, and what an actual amends would look like. That is worth thinking through with someone rather than acting on impulse, because a confession made to relieve your own guilt can sometimes land as another injury on the person you hurt.

If it involves harm done to you

Then the shame you have been carrying was never yours. It belonged to whoever did it and got transferred to you, which is what abuse tends to do. This is among the most common and most painful versions of a kept secret, and it is the one most worth taking to someone trained to help. If any of this brings up thoughts of harming yourself, please tell someone today. In the US you can call or text 988 at any time.

Where to take it

A therapist's room is built for exactly this. You do not have to lead with it; you can take weeks to get there. But it is the one place where saying the thing has no social cost, and where someone can help you look at it without flinching and without judgment. You have carried this alone for long enough. It was never proof of what you are.
