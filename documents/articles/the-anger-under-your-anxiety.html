The Anger Under Your Anxiety

You worry constantly and rarely get angry. For some people those two facts are connected. How anger that has nowhere to go can come back as worry, and what to do when you find it.

AngerAnxiety Visitor 831 words

You are an anxious person. You worry, you overthink, you feel wound tight most of the time. What you almost never feel is angry. If asked, you would say you are not really an angry person. Other people in your life get angry; you get worried.

For some people, those two things turn out to be the same thing wearing different clothes. Not for everyone, and anxiety has many roots. But if the description above fits you closely, it is worth looking at.

Why anger can turn into worry

Anger is a signal. It tends to arrive when something is unfair, when a line has been crossed, when you need something you are not getting. It is meant to move you: to speak up, to push back, to change something.

But a lot of people learned early that anger was not safe. Maybe someone in your house had a temper that frightened everyone, so you decided never to be like that. Maybe anger in you was punished, or met with withdrawal, or treated as unacceptable in a way it was not for others. Maybe your job in the family was to keep things calm.

When anger has nowhere to go, it does not vanish. It gets converted into something more permitted. For many people that something is anxiety: a restless, vigilant, uncomfortable state that feels safer than the feeling underneath, because it points at the future rather than at a person.

How to tell whether this is you

A few signs. You worry a great deal but almost never feel angry. You find yourself anxious specifically after interactions where you were treated badly and said nothing. You have physical tension, jaw or shoulders or stomach, without a clear worry attached. You get irritated at small, safe things, the traffic, the dishes, while remaining unbothered by large unfair ones. You feel guilty at even the first flicker of anger, and it disappears quickly into worry about whether you are a bad person.

None of these is proof. Together, they are a pattern worth exploring.

Look at what happens before the spike

For the next while, when anxiety jumps, ask a different question than usual. Instead of what am I worried about, ask what just happened, and was there anything in it that was not okay?

Often you will find something: a comment that stung, a request you did not want to agree to, being talked over, taking on someone else's job again. The anxiety arrived right after, dressed as worry about something unrelated.

Let yourself say the angry sentence, privately

You do not have to express anger at anyone to feel it. Try writing it down, in the bluntest words you have. That was not fair. I did not want to do that. I am furious that this keeps landing on me. Nobody sees it. You are not doing anything to anyone. You are just letting the signal exist long enough to be read.

Many people find that the anxiety drops noticeably once the real feeling has been named, even with no action taken.

Move it through the body

Anger is a physical state, and one that has been held down for years tends to be stored physically. A hard walk, pushing against a wall, squeezing and releasing your fists, a loud song in the car. Not to work yourself up, but to let the charge move rather than sit. Many people feel calmer afterward in a way that talking has not achieved.

Then ask what it was pointing at

Anger is information. Once you can feel it, ask what it wants. Usually it names something concrete: I need to say no to this. I need to tell them how that landed. I need to stop being the one who always handles this. That is the useful end of anger, and it is the part that avoidance costs you.

Acting on it does not mean shouting. It usually means one calm sentence you have been swallowing for years.

Expect it to feel wrong

If you grew up believing anger was dangerous, the first times you let yourself feel it will be uncomfortable. You may feel guilty, frightened, or convinced you are turning into the person you did not want to be. Notice that feeling angry and behaving badly are two different things, and that you have spent a lifetime proving you can hold the second. You are allowed to have the feeling.

When to get help with it

If anxiety is running your days, if you cannot locate anger at all even when something is clearly unfair, or if looking in this direction brings up old memories that feel too big to handle alone, please talk to a therapist. This pattern usually starts young and in a specific place, and it untangles much more easily with someone alongside you. Anxiety that has been carrying anger for decades can ease a great deal once the anger is allowed to be what it is.
