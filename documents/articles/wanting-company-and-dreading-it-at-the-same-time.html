Wanting Company and Dreading It at the Same Time

You are lonely and you also cancel plans. Why those two things live together so often, how the cycle feeds itself, and what kinds of contact are easiest when both are true at once.

Social AnxietyLoneliness Visitor 844 words

You are lonely. You also turned down two invitations this month and felt relieved both times. Someone suggests coffee and you feel a lift, then a sinking, then you start looking for a way out of it.

If that sounds contradictory, it is one of the most ordinary combinations there is. Wanting people and dreading them are not opposites, and having both at once does not mean you do not really want company.

Why they travel together

Loneliness is about not having enough connection. Dread before contact is about what the contact might cost: being judged, running out of things to say, being tiring company, being seen on a bad week.

Nothing about wanting the first makes you immune to the second. In fact they feed each other. The less contact you have, the more unfamiliar it feels, and the more unfamiliar it feels, the bigger the dread before each attempt.

So the two grow together, and after a while you can end up genuinely starved of company and genuinely afraid of it, at the same hour of the same day.

How the cycle keeps itself going

The dread is loudest before. That is the key thing to know.

Anticipation is almost always the worst part of social contact for people in this position. During, you are usually busier than you expected and it goes better than predicted. Afterward there is often relief and sometimes real warmth.

But the canceling happens during the worst part, when the prediction is at its most pessimistic. So you make the decision at the one moment you have the least accurate information, and then the cancellation feels good, which teaches you to do it again.

Every cancellation also removes a chance to find out that it would have been fine. So the prediction never gets corrected, and next time it is a little louder.

Picking contact that is actually easier

Not all company costs the same. If both things are true for you, choose on the dimensions that make dread smaller, not on what sounds most sociable.

Side by side beats face to face. Walking, driving, cooking, a task, a movie. Less eye contact and fewer silences to fill, because the activity carries part of the conversation.

Short and fixed beats open-ended. Forty-five minutes with a stated end is far easier to say yes to than an evening with no edges. You are allowed to say I have about an hour.

A known person beats a new one, and one person beats a group, when you are starting from a long gap.

Regular beats special. The same walk every Thursday takes less out of you each time, because the deciding stops and the novelty wears off. One large event takes much more and teaches you less.

Working with the pre-contact slump

Assume the hour before will be the worst and plan for it rather than treating it as new information.

Decide once, in advance, and treat the decision as made. When the dread shows up later, it is not a fresh assessment. It is the thing you already knew was coming.

Do not get ready too early. A long stretch of waiting while dressed and anxious gives the dread somewhere to build. Keep yourself occupied until closer to the time.

Give yourself an honest exit. I might have to leave early is a legitimate sentence, and knowing you can use it often means you do not.

Afterward, write one line: what you predicted, what happened. Not a journal entry. Two sentences. Over a few weeks this becomes the only argument that actually works on the dread, because it is in your own handwriting about your own life.

Lowering the stakes on the small stuff

Contact does not all have to be plans. Loneliness also lifts a little from brief, low-cost human contact that asks nothing of you.

Being somewhere with other people present. A library, a counter, a class where you do not have to talk. Saying hello to the same person twice a week. Sending one message that does not need a reply.

None of this substitutes for close company, and all of it makes close company easier to approach, because it keeps you in practice.

Being honest with the people who ask

If someone keeps inviting you and you keep saying no, they will eventually read it as a verdict on them. One sentence prevents years of that: I want to see you and I am bad at plans right now, so keep asking.

Most people can work with that. What they cannot work with is silence.

When to get help with it

If the dread has narrowed your life, if you have stopped answering people you care about, or if the loneliness has become a flat kind of hopelessness rather than an ache, this is well-trodden ground for a therapist. The approach to social fear is specific and practical, and it works on exactly the prediction problem described here. You do not have to be at the end of your rope to make the call.
