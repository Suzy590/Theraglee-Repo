Worrying About Someone Who Will Not Worry About Themselves

When the person you are frightened for refuses to be frightened, the worry has nowhere to go. How to tell your part from theirs, say the hard thing once, and keep the caring survivable.

WorryFamily Visitor 873 words

Someone you love is not taking care of themselves. They are not going to the doctor. They are drinking more than they should. They are ignoring a symptom, or a debt, or a relationship that is plainly hurting them. You can see where it goes. They either cannot see it or will not look.

So you carry it for them. And carrying worry on behalf of somebody who refuses to carry it themselves is a particular kind of exhausting, because there is no action at the end of it.

Why it eats so much of you

Ordinary worry has an exit. You are worried about a bill, you pay the bill. You are worried about a test result, the result comes. The worry rises, something happens, it falls.

This kind has no exit, because the thing that would resolve it is a decision inside another person. You can bring information. You can offer to drive them. You can say the hard sentence out loud. None of that is the decision. The decision is theirs and it stays theirs.

So the worry has nowhere to discharge. It circles. And because it circles, you keep going back for more: more reading, more scenarios, more conversations in your head where you finally find the words that work.

The conversation you keep rehearsing

Most people in this position have a speech. You have refined it for months. It covers everything you have noticed, why it matters, what you are afraid of, and what you want them to do.

The hard news is that the speech is usually not the missing piece. When someone is not acting, it is rarely because nobody has explained it well enough. It is more often because acting would mean facing something they are not ready to face, or because the thing they are doing is solving a problem for them that you cannot see from outside.

That does not mean say nothing. It means say it once, plainly, and stop auditioning better versions. One clear sentence about what you see and what you are afraid of, with no pile of evidence attached, lands better than the full case. It also leaves the door open, which the full case usually does not.

Separating your part from theirs

Write two lists. What is actually mine to do. What is actually theirs.

Yours might include telling them the truth once. Finding the phone number and leaving it where they will see it. Offering a ride. Not covering for them with other people. Staying in contact.

Theirs includes going. Stopping. Asking for help. Changing.

This is not a trick for caring less. It is a way of making the caring survivable. When the two lists are mixed together, every day they do not act feels like something you failed at, and that is the part that burns people out.

What to do with the worry itself

It is not going to vanish, so it needs somewhere to live that is not all of your waking hours.

Give it a window. Fifteen minutes, around the same time most days, where you let yourself think about it properly and write down anything that is genuinely a next step. When it shows up outside the window, note it and tell yourself you will get to it at the usual time. This sounds too simple to work and it works more often than people expect.

Keep your own life running on purpose. Not as a reward, and not as self-care in the slogan sense. Specifically, keep the parts of your week that have nothing to do with this person, because worry expands to fill whatever space it is given.

Tell one person outside the situation. Not for advice. So that you stop being the only one who knows how bad it looks from here.

If something changes, be easy to come back to

People in this position often brace for the moment they can finally say they were right. Understandable, and it costs you the thing you actually want.

If they do decide to act, the deciding factor is often how hard it is to come to you. So keep the cost of coming back low. No I told you so. No ledger of everything you did while they would not listen. Just help, offered plainly.

That is a lot to ask of yourself while you are tired, which is exactly why it is worth deciding in advance rather than in the moment.

When to get support for your own sake

If this worry is running your sleep, your mood, or your other relationships, it has stopped being only about them and started being about what it is doing to you. That is a legitimate thing to take to a therapist on your own, without the other person involved and without waiting for them to change. Many people find it strange to seek help about somebody else's choices. It is one of the more common reasons people come in, and one of the more workable.

And if you are seriously frightened for their safety rather than their choices, that is not a worry to sit on quietly. Take it to a doctor or a counselor today, even without their agreement.
