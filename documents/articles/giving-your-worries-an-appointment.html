Giving Your Worries an Appointment

Trying not to worry does not work. Setting aside fifteen minutes a day to worry on purpose does, surprisingly well. How to run a worry window and what to do inside it.

CBTWorry Visitor 887 words

Worry does not respond to being told to stop. You have probably tested this thoroughly: the harder you push a worry away, the more often it comes back, and now you have two problems, the worry and the effort of suppressing it.

There is an approach that works better, and it sounds odd the first time you hear it. Instead of trying to worry less, you give worry a fixed appointment and keep the rest of the day out of its reach.

The idea in one paragraph

Pick a time and a length: fifteen minutes, mid-evening, same slot each day. During that window you worry deliberately and thoroughly. Outside it, when a worry arrives, you note it down and tell yourself it will be dealt with at the appointment.

That is the whole method. It comes from cognitive behavioral therapy, and it is one of the more reliable things you can do about a mind that runs all day.

Why it works when suppression does not

Pushing a thought away has a cost: some part of your attention has to keep watch for the thought in order to block it, which keeps it live.

Postponing is different. You are not arguing with the worry or denying it. You are agreeing to take it seriously, later. That turns out to be enough for most worries to loosen their hold in the moment, because the thing they are demanding is attention, and you have promised it rather than refused it.

There is a second effect that people notice within a week or two. By the time the appointment arrives, a good number of the worries have expired. They looked urgent at eleven in the morning and are uninteresting at eight at night, which is useful information about what worry actually is.

How to set it up

Choose a time that is not just before bed, because you do not want to hand your worries the hour when you are trying to sleep. Early evening works well for most people.

Choose a place that is not your bed or the sofa where you relax. A kitchen table is ideal. You want the worrying attached to a spot you can get up and leave.

Set a timer for fifteen minutes. When it goes, you stop, even mid-thought. Stopping on time is part of what makes the method credible to yourself.

Keep a short list during the day, on paper or your phone: three or four words per worry, just enough to recognize it later. Writing it down is what makes postponing possible; trying to remember it keeps it in the room.

What to do inside the window

Do not use the fifteen minutes to spin. Use them to sort.

Take each worry on your list and ask one question: is this a problem or a what-if? A problem has a next action, even a small one. A what-if does not; it is about a future you cannot influence today.

For a problem, write the next action and when you will do it. That is all. You are not solving the whole thing, just naming the next step and giving it a slot.

For a what-if, the job is different and less satisfying. You let it be there without solving it. Say the feared thing plainly to yourself, including the part you usually avoid, and notice that you are still sitting at a table. Repeated deliberately, a feared thought loses some of its charge; chased and pushed away, it keeps it.

When the timer goes, stand up, leave the table, and do something with your hands for a few minutes. The transition matters.

What usually goes wrong

Three things, all fixable.

You skip the window when you feel fine, and then the postponing stops working, because your promise has been broken. Keep the appointment even on quiet days, and finish early if there is nothing in the list.

You let the worry negotiate. It says this one is different, this one is urgent. Unless there is an action you can take in the next hour, it is not urgent; it is loud. Write it down and let it wait.

You use the window to reassure yourself, checking and rechecking until you feel better. That is the rechecking loop wearing a different hat, and it will keep you exactly where you are. The aim is sorting and sitting with, not settling.

Give it two weeks before you judge it

The first few days feel clumsy and you will forget often. That is normal, and the method survives being done imperfectly.

What tends to change first is not the amount of worry but its timing. The day gets quieter before the total goes down, and a quieter day is worth a great deal on its own.

When it is not enough

Worry that has taken over most of your day, that comes with a knot you never lose, that has you avoiding things, or that keeps you awake most nights is worth treating properly rather than managing alone. This kind of worry responds well to therapy, often in a fairly small number of sessions, and a doctor or a therapist is the right place to start. Bring the list you have been keeping; it is more useful than trying to describe it from memory.
