Arriving Home With No Memory of the Drive

You park the car and cannot account for the last twenty minutes. The everyday end of dissociation: what that blank stretch of road is, when it is ordinary, when to mention it to a doctor, and how to stay present.

GroundingMindfulness Visitor 925 words

You park, turn off the engine, and realize you cannot account for the last twenty minutes. You know you left work. You know you are home. The road in between is simply missing. You did not run a light. You did not drift out of your lane. You arrived safely, and still there is a blank where the drive should be.

Almost everyone has had some version of this, and almost nobody mentions it, because saying it out loud sounds worse than it felt. It has a name and a reason, and knowing both tends to take the alarm out of it.

What is happening in that blank stretch

Attention and memory are not the same system. A road you know well asks very little of the part of you that makes decisions, so your attention quietly hands the job to the practiced, automatic part of your mind. That part is a competent driver. It holds the lane, reads the lights, and brakes when the car ahead brakes. What it does not do is lay down the kind of detailed memory you could describe later. Nothing was recorded, so there is nothing to recall.

Meanwhile the thinking part of you went elsewhere. Over the conversation you had at four o'clock. Over what you will say tomorrow. Over nothing in particular. Twenty minutes of being somewhere else do not get filed.

This is the mild, everyday end of what gets called dissociation: a gap between where your body is and where your attention is. It sits on a wide range, and the missing drive home is the end of it that nearly everyone visits.

When it is ordinary, and when it is worth noticing

On a familiar route, after a long day, with your head full, a missing drive is unremarkable. It happens more when you are tired, more when you are under sustained stress, and more when the route never changes.

It is worth closer attention if the gaps are getting longer, or happening on roads you do not know. If you arrive somewhere you did not intend to go. If you come back to yourself unsure what time of day it is, or feeling that the world looks slightly unreal. If it happens while a child is in the car. Or if the blanks started after something frightening happened to you.

None of those are reasons to be frightened of yourself. They are reasons to mention it to a doctor, because several of the possible causes are physical, common, and quick to check: poor sleep, low blood sugar, a medication, a thyroid problem, migraine, or a seizure type that looks like nothing more than a few lost seconds.

Anchor the first minute

You cannot pay attention for a whole drive by deciding to. What you can do is set the first minute on purpose, and that changes more than it sounds like it should.

Before you turn the key, do one small physical thing deliberately. Put both hands on the wheel and feel the texture of it. Say where you are going, out loud: I am driving to my sister's house. Notice whether the air is warm or cold. Ten seconds is enough. You are telling your mind that this trip is something you are doing rather than something happening around you.

Give your attention places to come back to

Pick two or three landmarks on your usual route and use them as check-in points. At the bridge, name three things you can see. At the gas station, feel your back against the seat and your hands on the wheel. This gives the drive a shape your memory can hold, and it brings you back before a gap gets long.

Cool air helps more than people expect. Crack the window, turn the temperature down a little, or aim a vent at your face. Cold is the fastest route back into the body, in the car and everywhere else.

Silence sometimes makes it worse, because your mind fills it. Something that asks a little of you, people talking rather than music you know by heart, keeps you nearer the surface. If you are so tired that the road is sliding away from you, that is not a focus problem. Pull over and stop.

End the trip on purpose too

When you arrive, give yourself thirty seconds in the parked car before you go in. Feel your feet on the floor. Say the day and the time. Take one slow breath out. Most people drive from one demanding place to another and never mark the change, so the day stays in one long blur. A short, deliberate ending makes the next hour feel more like yours.

Look after the conditions that make the gaps longer

Tiredness is the biggest one by a wide margin. Regular sleep, food at reasonable hours, a real break somewhere in the day, and something that lets the day's tension out of your body will not make you a perfectly attentive driver, but they noticeably shrink how often you lose the road.

When to get help

If the blanks are frequent, lengthening, or happening when you are not driving, if you lose longer stretches of time, or if they began after a frightening experience, please bring it up with your doctor, who can rule out the physical causes, and with a therapist if the answer turns out to be stress or something you went through. This is a known, well understood thing, and it is far more treatable than it is mysterious.
