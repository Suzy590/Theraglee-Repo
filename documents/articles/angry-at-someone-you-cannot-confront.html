Angry at Someone You Cannot Confront

The person has died, moved away, holds all the power, or would only deny it. What to do with real anger when the conversation that would settle it is not available.

AngerDBT Visitor 943 words

You are furious with someone, and there is nowhere to put it. They died before you could say it. They would deny every word. They are your manager, or your landlord, or the hospital, and saying it plainly would cost you more than it is worth.

So you carry it, and it comes out sideways: in the car, at the dog, at the person who did nothing, at three in the morning.

Being stuck with it is its own problem

Anger is designed to move you toward something: a boundary, a correction, a conversation. When that route is closed, the energy does not simply switch off.

It usually turns into one of three things. Rumination, where you run the argument you will never have. Irritability with everyone nearby. Or a low, flat resentment that drains the color out of things and lasts for years.

None of those are a character flaw. They are what happens when a reasonable reaction has nowhere to go.

Decide honestly whether it is really closed

Before working around the conversation, check that it is actually unavailable.

Sometimes the barrier is real: they have died, they are dangerous, it would cost you your job or your housing, or they have shown repeatedly that they will twist anything you say.

Sometimes the barrier is fear, which is different. If the only thing stopping you is that it would be uncomfortable, that is worth looking at, possibly with help, because an unsaid sentence that could be said is a different problem from one that cannot.

And there is often a middle option. Not the full confrontation, but one line, once: when you say that about my parenting, I am not going to discuss it. Small, survivable, and sometimes enough.

Say it somewhere other than to them

The anger usually needs to be expressed, not resolved, and the person is not the only possible audience.

Write the letter you will never send. All of it, unedited, with the parts that are unfair to them left in; it is not a legal document. Many people find this does more in forty minutes than months of thinking.

Say it out loud to someone who is on your side, or to the empty room, or to a chair. Say it to your voice recorder and delete it. The point is to get the sentences out of your head and into the air, because unspoken anger stays at full volume indefinitely.

Then notice what the sentences are really about. Underneath most of it there is a plain statement — you were supposed to look after me, you took something I cannot get back, I did everything right and it did not matter — and finding that line is often the turn.

Let your body finish the thing

Anger is physical, and when the action it prepared you for never happens, the charge stays in your muscles.

So use it on purpose. Walk fast, lift something heavy, swim, dig, scrub, hit a pillow if that is your sort of thing. Push hard against a wall for ten seconds and let go.

That is not a metaphor. It is the simplest way to discharge what the conversation would have discharged, and it tends to drop the volume enough for the rest to be possible.

Accepting the situation is not forgiving the person

This is the part people resist most, and it is worth being precise about.

Accepting means: this happened, they are not going to admit it, there is no apology coming, and I am the one carrying the cost. That is a bitter set of facts and it is where you actually are.

It does not mean they were right, or that you forgive them, or that you must stay in touch, or that you have to feel calm about it. Those are separate questions and some of them have the answer no.

Fighting the facts — refusing to accept that no apology is coming — keeps you tied to the person far longer than accepting it does. The anger usually loosens not when they change but when you stop waiting for them to.

Choose where the energy goes

Anger with nowhere to point can be aimed, which is not the same as swallowing it.

Some people put it into a complaint that goes on a record, a letter to an organization, a safeguarding report, a review, a union. Even when nothing comes back, there is a real difference between silence and having said it to someone official.

Others put it into the thing the anger is about: the parent who looks after their own children differently, the person who makes sure it does not happen to the next patient. That is not a tidy ending, and it is better than carrying it unspent.

And some of it is grief rather than anger, and grief needs time and company rather than a target.

When to get help with it

Please talk to a doctor or a therapist if the anger is taking up most of your days, if it is coming out at people who do not deserve it, if you are drinking to manage it, or if it has settled into a resentment you cannot put down.

This is one of the things therapy handles well, partly because a therapist can be the audience the conversation never had, and partly because the skills for sitting with an unresolvable situation are teachable rather than a matter of character. If the anger is about something that was done to you, say so plainly to whoever you see; it points them to the right kind of help straight away.

If you are in crisis, call or text 988 (Suicide & Crisis Lifeline, US) or your local emergency number. Theraglee is a wellbeing tool, not a substitute for professional care.
