When Everything Feels Too Loud

The strip lights, the radio, somebody chewing, three people needing you at once. What sensory overload is, how to get out of it quickly, and how to have fewer of these afternoons.

GroundingEmotion Regulation Visitor 918 words

The extractor fan is going, the radio is on, someone is asking you a question while a child talks over them, and the kitchen light is the kind that hums.

Something in you reaches a limit. You snap, or go silent and rigid, or walk out of the room, and afterward you cannot explain what the problem was, because nothing happened.

What is actually going on

Your nervous system is handling every sound, light, smell and touch in the room, and all of it costs something.

Most of the time that cost is invisible. When the input is high and your capacity is low — tired, hungry, ill, stressed, in pain, already holding a lot — the total goes over what you can process, and the body treats that as a threat.

So you get the alarm response: irritability, a strong urge to escape, a tight chest, sometimes tears, sometimes a kind of shutdown where you cannot speak or decide anything.

It is not rudeness, drama or weakness. It is capacity, and it varies enormously between people and from day to day.

Get out first, understand later

In the moment, explaining is not the job. Reducing input is.

Leave the room. The bathroom, the car, the garden, the stairwell, outside the shop. Thirty seconds of less helps more than a minute of trying to cope with more.

Then cut the biggest channel. Noise is usually it: earplugs, headphones, the fan off, the radio off, the extractor off. Light next: turn the overhead light off, use a lamp, sunglasses in a supermarket, which looks odd and works.

Make yourself physically simpler. Cold water on your wrists or face. Something heavy over your lap or shoulders. Press your feet into the floor. Breathe out longer than you breathe in.

And do not make any decisions for a few minutes. Decisions are the first thing to go and the last thing to come back.

Say the short version out loud

Other people cannot see what is happening, so they read a snapped sentence as being about them.

Have one line ready and use it before it is urgent: too much noise, I need five minutes. Or: I am not cross with you, there is too much going on, I will be back.

Tell the people you live with what it is, once, in a calm hour. Most are relieved to have an explanation, and some will recognize it in themselves.

With children, keep it simple and unfrightening: my ears are full, I am going to stand outside for a minute.

Reduce the baseline rather than coping harder

Most of this is won in advance by lowering the ordinary level of input in your day.

Deal with the permanent noise: a noisy extractor, the radio nobody is listening to, notification sounds, a ticking clock, the television on for company. Turn off what nobody is using.

Change the light where you can. Overhead fluorescent strips are the worst offenders; a lamp, a warmer bulb, or a desk away from flicker makes a real difference.

Keep earplugs and headphones where you will actually have them: by the door, in the car, in a bag. The ones that take the edge off rather than block everything are usually the most useful.

And watch the stacking. Supermarket, then a phone call, then cooking while the children are hungry is three hard things in a row; shifting one of them is easier than enduring all three.

Protect the predictable overload points

Everyone has particular situations. Learn yours and plan for them rather than being surprised.

Supermarkets at busy hours. Open-plan offices. Parties. Soft play. Trains. Family gatherings in one room. Cooking with company. Christmas.

For each one: go at a quieter time, arrive early, sit at the edge, step outside every half hour, bring headphones, agree an exit with someone, and keep the event shorter than feels polite.

Food, sleep and recovery time are the three things that most change your threshold, and they are not luxuries if this happens to you regularly.

Afterward, do not add a verdict

The hour after is often full of shame: I ruined the afternoon, there is something wrong with me, everyone else manages.

Keep it factual instead: it was loud, I was tired, I got overloaded, I went outside and it passed. Then repair anything that needs repairing in one sentence and let it go.

And look at the conditions rather than your character, because the conditions are the part you can change.

When to get help with it

Please talk to a doctor if this is new, getting worse, or coming with headaches, dizziness, ringing in your ears or changes in your hearing or vision; sensitivity to light and sound has physical causes worth checking, including migraine, concussion, ear problems and some medications.

It is also worth a conversation if sensory overload is shaping your life: avoiding places, leaving jobs, dreading family events. Being highly sensitive to input is a known and ordinary way for a nervous system to be — it is a common part of how autistic and attention-different people experience the world, and it also follows burnout, illness and long stretches of stress — and support for it is practical rather than vague.

A therapist can help with the overwhelm and the shame around it, and an occupational therapist is often the most useful professional of all here, because their work is exactly the business of adjusting an environment so a person can function in it.

If you are in crisis, call or text 988 (Suicide & Crisis Lifeline, US) or your local emergency number. Theraglee is a wellbeing tool, not a substitute for professional care.
