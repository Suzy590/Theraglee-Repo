Running Into Someone You Used to Know

You see them across the store and have about four seconds to decide. Why these encounters are so uncomfortable, what to actually say, and how to stop replaying it afterward.

Social AnxietyCommunication Visitor 802 words

You spot them before they spot you. A former colleague, someone from school, a friend you let drift, an ex's sibling. Four seconds to decide whether to wave, say something, or develop a sudden interest in the shelf beside you.

Then whatever happens, you spend part of the afternoon going back over it.

Why four seconds is so hard

There is no script. Most social situations come with one, and this one does not: you do not know what you are to each other now, and you have to decide in the time it takes to walk past.

The information is also missing. You do not know whether they remember the last conversation the way you do, whether they know what happened to you since, whether they even recognize you.

And the gap has to be accounted for somehow. Any exchange carries an implicit question about why you have not spoken in three years, and both of you can feel it.

Then there is the mismatch. They have a vague warm memory and you have a specific one, or the reverse, and neither of you knows which.

What to actually say

Short, warm, and without an explanation for the silence. That combination handles almost all of these.

Lead with their name and something plain. It is good to see you. How long has it been. You look well.

Do not account for the gap. Nobody needs it, and launching into an explanation signals that the silence was loaded, which it usually was not for them.

Ask one specific question rather than how are you, which produces nothing. Are you still at the same place. Is your mother doing all right. How did the move go.

Then give it an ending yourself, which is the part people forget. I am glad I ran into you, I have to get this stuff home. Walking away cleanly is what stops these dragging into something neither of you wanted.

Three or four exchanges is a complete encounter. It does not have to go anywhere.

If you do not want to talk

That is allowed, and a small amount of acknowledgment is easier than hiding.

A nod and a smile while continuing to walk is a complete interaction and nobody reads it as rude.

If you genuinely cannot face it, going the other way is not a crime. It is worth knowing, though, that the guilt afterward is usually larger than the conversation would have been.

And if it is somebody you have reason to avoid, you are not obliged to be available because you are both in the same store. Leaving is a reasonable thing to do.

The replaying afterward

This is often the worst part, and it runs on a predictable script.

You will remember what you said and not what they said, which produces a very lopsided account of how it went.

You will grade the performance, usually on things nobody else registered: that you said good twice, that you did not ask about the job, that you walked away oddly.

And you will decide how it landed with no information at all. The certainty is the tell, as with most of this: with nothing to go on, confidence is coming from your mood rather than from evidence.

What helps is a time limit and a plain sentence. I saw someone, it was slightly awkward, it is finished. Then do something with your hands.

And it is worth knowing that the other person is almost certainly not doing this. Most people walk away from these with no more than a passing thought.

Deciding what to do next

If it went well and you want to see them, say so immediately rather than leaving it to a vague let us catch up. A specific suggestion within a day or two is the only version that happens.

If you do not want to, that is fine and requires nothing. A drifted friendship is not an unpaid debt.

And if it stirred something up, a loss, a job that ended badly, a version of yourself you do not want back, the encounter is not really the subject. Worth noticing, rather than analyzing the four seconds.

When it is more than awkward

If you are structuring your days around not running into people, going to different stores, avoiding a neighborhood, not answering the door, that has grown past discomfort into something that is shrinking your life.

That is well-trodden ground for a therapist, and the work on it is practical and usually short: what the fear is predicting, what actually happens, and how to get the two closer together. If a particular person is the reason you are avoiding public places, bring that to a professional too, because it is a different problem and needs a different answer.
