When a Friend Owes You Money

You lent it months ago and they have not mentioned it since. Why money between friends goes wrong so easily, how to actually ask, and how to decide whether to let it go.

MoneyBoundaries Visitor 978 words

You lent them the money because they needed it and you had it. No paperwork, no date, just a transfer and a thank you. That was four months ago. They have been out to dinner twice since, they have not mentioned it, and you think about it every time you see them.

And you have not said anything, because the thought of raising it makes you feel small and grasping, which is a strange thing to feel when you are the one who is owed.

Why this goes wrong so predictably

Money and friendship run on different rules, and a loan puts them in the same room. Friendship works on rough reciprocity that nobody counts. A loan is a specific debt with an amount. Once one exists, the friendship has a ledger in it, and ledgers change how people behave.

It also creates an imbalance that neither of you chose. They are now in the position of owing, which is uncomfortable enough that many people cope by avoiding the subject entirely. Their silence is much more often avoidance than bad faith, though from your side the two look identical.

And the amount is rarely the real problem. What bothers most people is the not-mentioning: the sense that something owed to them has been quietly dropped, and that raising it would make them the difficult one.

Get clear on what you actually want

Before any conversation, decide which of these you want, because they need different approaches.

The money back, in full. Then you need a plan and a date.

The money back over time. Then you need a schedule that suits their situation.

Not the money, but an acknowledgment. Many people find this is the real thing. They want to hear that it is remembered.

To let it go entirely, and be free of thinking about it. That is a legitimate choice, and it takes a deliberate decision rather than drifting.

Ask plainly and early

The conversation is easier than the dread suggests, and much easier at four months than at two years. The keys are to be direct, to be warm, and not to apologize for asking.

Hey, I wanted to check in about the money I lent you in May. What would work for paying it back?

That is all. Not, I hate to bring this up, sorry, I know things are hard. Softening it invites more delay, and it signals that you feel you are doing something wrong, which you are not.

Then be quiet and let them answer.

Make it easy to say the true thing

Most of the time the honest answer is that they cannot pay it right now. Leave room for that: if it is not possible at the moment, tell me and we will figure out a plan.

Then make an actual plan, with numbers and dates, even small ones. Fifty a month starting in October, said out loud, resolves this better than a vague promise, and it lets them stop avoiding you.

If they say yes and then nothing happens

Follow up once, in writing, without heat: just checking, are you still able to start that in October? A written record is not aggressive; it is how adults handle money, and it removes the ambiguity that lets it drift again.

If it keeps not happening after that, you have learned something real. At that point the question changes from how do I get this back to what do I want this friendship to be.

Decide whether to let it go

Sometimes the most self-respecting choice is to write it off. Not out of resentment, and not silently while keeping score forever. An actual decision: this money is gone, I am not going to think about it again, and I am not going to lend to this person again.

That last clause is the important one. Letting go of the amount without changing the pattern means you will be here again.

If you do decide to release it, say so if it helps: let us call it a gift, I would rather have you than the money. Then mean it, and stop mentioning it.

Watch what it is doing to the friendship

Unspoken money sits in the middle of a relationship and slowly changes it. You start noticing what they spend on. You feel a flash of something when they order the expensive thing. They feel watched, and they start seeing you less.

Whichever route you pick, the point of picking one is to get the ledger out of the friendship. Almost any resolution beats another year of quiet accounting.

Next time, decide in advance which it is

The most useful habit is to decide before you hand anything over whether this is a loan or a gift, and to say which out loud. If it is a loan, name an amount and a rough date. If you would not be willing to chase it, make it a gift and expect nothing.

And a rule many people keep, having learned it the hard way: only lend an amount you could lose without it changing how you feel about the person.

When it is bigger than this

If the amount is large enough to be affecting your own finances, it is worth taking seriously rather than politely. If a family member keeps borrowing and not repaying, or if there is pressure, guilt, or anger when you raise it, that is a pattern rather than an incident, and a therapist can help you work out what to do about it. Money in relationships is one of the most common things people bring to therapy, precisely because it is never only about money. And if you are the one who owes, please know that saying I cannot pay this yet, here is what I can do is almost always received better than silence.
