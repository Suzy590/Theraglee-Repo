Feeling Like a Fraud at Work

You got the job, the promotion, the praise, and you are sure it was a mistake that will be discovered any day. Why capable people feel like impostors, and what actually loosens the feeling.

WorkSelf-Esteem Visitor 906 words

From the outside, you are doing well. Good reviews, a job people respect, maybe a promotion or a degree. From the inside, it feels like a trick you are getting away with. You are certain that any day now, someone will look closely, realize you do not know what you are doing, and the whole thing will fall apart.

This feeling is so common it has a name: impostor feelings. It shows up in new graduates and in people who have run their field for decades. Knowing that it is common does not make it go away, but it is a start, because the feeling insists that you are the only fraud in a room full of real people.

How the feeling keeps itself going

Impostor feelings run on a loop. You get a task. You feel anxious, sure you cannot do it. You either overprepare, working far harder than needed, or you put it off until the last minute. You get it done and it goes fine. And then, instead of thinking, I did that, you think, I got lucky, or, I only pulled it off because I worked twice as hard as anyone else should have to.

The success gets explained away. The doubt survives. Next task, same loop. Years of evidence pile up and none of it counts, because the feeling has a rule: anything good was luck, and anything bad was the truth.

Who tends to feel it

Almost everyone, at some point. But it hits hardest in certain situations. Being new or the youngest. Being the only person like you in the room, whether by gender, race, background, or anything else, so that you feel you are representing more than yourself. Growing up in a family where praise was rare or where being smart was your role. Working in a field where everyone seems confident and nobody admits to struggling. High achievers, especially, because the higher you go, the more it looks like you have something to lose.

The feeling is not information about your ability. It is information about your situation and your history.

Look at what you are calling luck

Take a recent success and write down what actually happened. Not luck, the specifics. You prepared. You asked a good question. You noticed something others missed. You kept going when it got hard. Luck does not do those things. You did. The exercise feels silly and it works, because the feeling depends on never looking too closely.

Do the same with the mistakes. You made one. Did the world end? Did anyone conclude you were a fraud, or did they just see a person who made a mistake and fixed it?

Notice that competence does not feel like anything

Here is something that trips up a lot of people. You expect that once you are good at something, you will feel confident. Then you get good at it and you still feel unsure, so you conclude you must not really be good. But competence mostly does not feel like confidence. It feels like seeing more clearly how much there is to know. The people who feel certain are often the ones who have not looked hard enough. Doubt can be a sign that you understand the job, not that you are failing at it.

Say it out loud to someone

Impostor feelings live in secrecy. The whole terror is that people will find out. So find out first, on your terms. Tell a colleague you trust, or a mentor: I feel like I am faking this half the time. Watch their face. Most of the time they will say, me too, and you will both be surprised. That does not make the feeling vanish. It does make it a shared human thing instead of your private shame.

Stop overworking to earn your place

Working twice as hard as everyone else to justify being there is not a solution. It burns you out and it feeds the belief that you only belong because of the extra effort. Try, in a small way, doing the ordinary amount. Notice that the work is still fine. That is evidence too.

Separate being new from being unqualified

If you are new to a role, not knowing things is not fraud. It is the job description of being new. Nobody expects you to know it all; they expect you to learn it. Ask the questions. Asking is what competent people do. Silence to hide gaps is what makes gaps bigger.

Let praise land

When someone tells you that you did well, the reflex is to deflect: it was nothing, anyone could have done it, I had a lot of help. Try just saying thank you and letting it sit for a moment. It will be uncomfortable. That discomfort is the feeling being challenged, and challenging it is the point.

When it gets in the way

Some impostor feeling is nearly universal and can even keep you humble and careful. But if it is stopping you from applying for things, speaking up, or accepting what you have earned, if it drives constant anxiety or has you working yourself into the ground, it deserves real attention. A therapist can help you trace where the feeling came from and loosen its grip, and the difference in how it feels to go to work can be enormous. You got where you are. It is worth learning to believe it.
