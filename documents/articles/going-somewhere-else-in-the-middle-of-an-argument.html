Going Somewhere Else in the Middle of an Argument

Halfway through a disagreement you go flat and far away, and afterward you cannot remember what either of you said. Why conflict does this, what it looks like from the other side, and how to stay in the room.

RelationshipsEmotion Regulation Visitor 936 words

The conversation starts badly and then something switches off. Their voice keeps going but the words stop landing. You can feel your face going still. You hear yourself say fine, or whatever you want, in a voice that sounds like it belongs to someone two rooms away. Later you could not repeat a single sentence of what either of you said.

Afterward you feel ashamed of it, usually. You wanted to explain yourself and instead you went blank. The other person may have accused you of not caring, or of shutting them out on purpose. From inside, it does not feel like a choice at all.

What the going-away is doing

Under enough threat, a body has a small number of moves. It can get bigger and push back. It can get out. And when neither of those is available or safe, it can go still and quiet and far away. That last one is sometimes called shutdown, and it belongs to the same family as the spacing-out that gets called dissociation.

Conflict with someone who matters to you is a threat your nervous system takes seriously, because being at odds with the people you depend on is one of the oldest dangers there is. If you grew up somewhere raised voices meant something was about to happen, or where arguing never once ended well, your system learned early that the safest thing in a fight is to not quite be there. That lesson does not expire on its own, and it does not check first whether the person in front of you now is actually dangerous.

This is why it fires fastest with partners, parents, and anyone whose opinion of you you cannot shrug off.

What it looks like from the other side

This matters, because the gap between the two experiences causes most of the damage. Inside, you have gone numb and distant and are barely holding on. Outside, you look cold. Flat voice, still face, short answers, eyes somewhere else. To someone who is upset and wants contact, that reads as contempt or indifference, and they push harder to get a reaction, which pushes you further away. Both people end up feeling abandoned by the other.

Say the true thing early

The single most useful move is to name it before you are gone, not after. Not an argument about who is right, just a short, plain sentence.

I want to have this conversation and I am starting to go blank. I need ten minutes.

I am not ignoring you. This is what happens to me when I get overwhelmed, and I am trying not to.

I can feel myself going far away. Can we sit down instead of standing up.

It helps enormously to have agreed on this in advance, in calm weather. Tell the person what it is, tell them what it looks like from outside, and agree on a word or a signal that means I am going, not I do not care.

Keep one foot in your body

You do not need to fix the feeling, only to stay reachable. Small physical anchors work better than any attempt to think clearly.

Put both feet flat on the floor and press down. Hold something with texture. Sit rather than stand, where you can. Let your breath out slowly, longer than you take it in, a few times. Look deliberately at three ordinary objects in the room and name them to yourself. Keep your hand on something cool.

Take the break properly

A break only helps if it has an end on it. Ten minutes, and we come back. A break with no return is a disappearance, and it frightens the other person into chasing you next time.

Use those minutes physically, not mentally. Walk. Cold water on your wrists. Stand outside. Do not spend them rehearsing the argument, which keeps the alarm running.

Come back to it later, and do that part well

When you have both landed, finish it. The repair is often more valuable than the argument would have been. Tell them what happened to you, and ask what it was like for them. Agree on the signal for next time. If you went quiet and they said something cutting while you were gone, both of those can be true and both can be addressed.

Expect this to take practice, not a decision

You will not stop doing this by resolving to. What usually happens is that the going-away gets shorter: first you notice it only afterward, then you notice it while it is happening, then you notice it early enough to say something. That is the actual shape of progress, and it is worth more than any single conversation going perfectly.

Look at the conditions too

This happens far more readily when you are exhausted, hungry, or already stretched thin. Hard conversations held late at night, in doorways, on an empty stomach, go the way you would expect. Agreeing with someone that difficult subjects get a sitting-down, daytime, fed version of you is not avoidance. It is a reasonable accommodation for a real thing.

When to get help

If this happens in most conflicts, if it is damaging a relationship you want to keep, or if it started after a frightening time in your life, a therapist can help with both halves: the moments themselves, and the reason your system learned that going away was the safest option. Couples counseling helps too when both of you want to understand what the other is doing. You are not cold, and you are not failing at this on purpose.
