When You Are Sleeping Far Too Much

Eleven hours and still exhausted, naps that eat the afternoon, a bed that is the only comfortable place. Why sleeping too much makes you feel worse, and how to pull the day back.

DepressionHabits Visitor 916 words

You slept from ten until nine, went back to bed at two, and you are still tired. Not sleepy exactly, but heavy, like you are moving through something thicker than air.

Nobody worries about this the way they worry about insomnia, so people tend to carry it quietly and assume it is laziness. It is usually not.

Sleeping more and resting less

The strange part is how little of it feels restorative.

Long sleep with a low mood is often shallow and broken up, so twelve hours can leave you less rested than seven. And the more of the day you spend in bed, the weaker your body clock's signal becomes, which makes the next night worse, which makes the next day heavier.

Bed also becomes the only place that feels tolerable, which is understandable and is a trap: it removes daylight, movement and other people, which are the three things most likely to lift the heaviness a little.

That is the loop to interrupt, and you interrupt it from the daytime end rather than the night end.

Get checked, because several physical things do this

Before treating it as mood alone, see a doctor and say plainly how much you are sleeping.

Thyroid trouble, anemia, low vitamin D, diabetes, long covid and sleep apnea all produce exactly this, and apnea in particular is widely missed. If you snore heavily, wake with a dry mouth or a headache, or someone has seen you stop breathing, say so, because that is treatable and nothing else works until it is.

Medications matter too. Some antidepressants, antihistamines, painkillers and blood pressure tablets are sedating, and the fix is often a change of dose or timing rather than enduring it.

And alcohol is worth an honest look: a drink in the evening reliably produces long, poor-quality sleep.

Fix the getting-up end, not the going-to-bed end

This is the single most effective change, and it is the least appealing.

Pick one get-up time and keep it every day, including weekends, including after a bad night. It does not have to be early; it has to be the same. Everything else follows from that anchor.

Then get light into your eyes within half an hour. Outside is far better than indoors, even in gray weather, even for five minutes on the step. Light is the main signal your body clock uses, and a dark morning in bed tells it there is no reason to start the day.

Have something small and fixed to get up for: the kettle, the dog, the radio at a certain time, a shower, stepping outside. A reason you do not have to invent at eight in the morning.

Make the bed less available in the daytime

You do not have to be heroic about this. You have to make lying down slightly harder.

Get dressed, even into different soft clothes. Make the bed, which sounds like a parent talking and genuinely reduces how often people get back in. Sit in another room, even if you do the same nothing there.

If you nap, make it deliberate rather than accidental: twenty minutes, before mid-afternoon, with an alarm, on top of the covers. A two-hour afternoon sleep is the main reason the evening stretches and the night falls apart.

Put something in the first hour

An empty day pulls you back to bed by lunchtime, so give the morning one small thing with a time attached.

A walk to the end of the road. A shower. One chore. One message to a person. A class, a shift, an errand. Not a productive day; one anchor, which makes the next hour easier to be upright in.

And move your body a little, earlier rather than later. Even a slow ten minutes outside does more for the heaviness than another hour horizontal, which is maddening when you are exhausted and is nonetheless true.

Expect the energy to arrive after the activity rather than before it. Waiting to feel up to something is how weeks disappear.

Be careful how you talk about it

People in this state are usually much harder on themselves than anyone else is.

Lazy, pathetic, wasting my life. Those sentences are the heaviness talking, and they reliably make the next morning worse rather than earlier.

Something flatter is more useful: I am sleeping too much because something is wrong, and the way out is one get-up time and some daylight. Then do the small version and count it.

When to get help

Please talk to a doctor if this has gone on for more than a couple of weeks, if you are sleeping through commitments, if your mood is low alongside it, or if you have lost interest in things that used to matter.

Sleeping far too much is one of the clearest signs that something treatable is going on, whether that turns out to be physical, a medication, or a low mood, and it is worth saying out loud rather than apologizing for. Ask for your bloods to be checked and for a sleep assessment if the snoring or the daytime heaviness fits; ask too about treatment for the mood itself, which responds well, and a therapist can help you rebuild the structure of a day while that works.

And if things have become dark enough that you are not sure you want to be here, please tell a doctor or someone you trust today. In the US you can call or text 988 at any time.

If you are in crisis, call or text 988 (Suicide & Crisis Lifeline, US) or your local emergency number. Theraglee is a wellbeing tool, not a substitute for professional care.
