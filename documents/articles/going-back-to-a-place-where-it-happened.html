Going Back to a Place Where It Happened

The road, the hospital, the house. How to decide whether you need to go at all, what your body will do on arrival, and why a short visit repeated beats one big confrontation.

TraumaResilience Visitor 877 words

The road. The hospital hallway. The house you grew up in. The hotel. The stretch of beach. A place where something happened to you, and you have to go back, or you want to, or somebody has asked you to.

Your body already knows the answer before you have decided anything. That is worth taking seriously rather than overriding, and it is also not the end of the conversation.

Decide whether you actually need to

The first question is the one people skip, because they assume they should be able to.

Sometimes you have to: a funeral, a court date, a treatment, a family event, a route with no alternative. Sometimes you want to, because the place has been growing in your mind and you would like it back. And sometimes somebody else wants you to, which is not the same as either.

There is no prize for going. Avoiding a place for the rest of your life has a cost, and so does forcing a visit you are not ready for. The useful version is choosing deliberately rather than being pushed, by somebody else or by your own idea of how recovered you ought to be by now.

Expect your body to react on arrival

This is the part that frightens people most, and knowing it in advance takes most of the sting out.

Places hold a great deal of sensory detail, and sensory detail is what memory of frightening events is built from. A smell, a particular light, the sound of a door, the feel of the air. Your body can respond to all of that before the thinking part of you has caught up.

So expect a racing heart, a wave of nausea, shaking, a strong urge to leave, tears from nowhere, or a flat numbness. None of that means you are back where you started. It means your body recognized something.

Go with the structure stacked in your favor

How you do it matters more than whether you do it.

Take somebody, if you can, who knows what the place is and does not need you to explain mid-visit. Go in daylight. Go for a short time, decided in advance. Have your own way of leaving, so you are never dependent on somebody else's schedule.

Have something planned for after. Not an empty afternoon. A meal, a walk, a person, something ordinary that puts you back into the present.

And have an exit sentence agreed with whoever you are with, so leaving does not require a discussion. If I say I am done, we go.

Anchor yourself in the present while you are there

The specific difficulty in a place like this is that the past can feel like now, and the counter to that is anything which proves the date.

Say the plain facts out loud or in your head. It is November. I am forty-one. That was eleven years ago. That is over and I am here now.

Use your senses deliberately for the present rather than the past. Feet on the ground. The temperature. Something in your hand. Something new in the view that was not there then, which is often the most convincing evidence available.

Move. Standing still in a place like that is harder than walking through it.

Let it be a short visit that you repeat

The version that tends to work is small and more than once, rather than one big confrontation.

Drive past rather than stopping. Then park outside. Then go in for five minutes. People who get their places back usually describe something like that, done over weeks, with somebody.

If a single long visit is unavoidable, make it as short as the occasion allows and leave when the thing you came for is done. Staying longer to prove something costs more than it pays.

Afterward, treat yourself as having done something strenuous

People go back, cope well on the day, and are flattened for the next two days without connecting the two.

So plan a slower day after. Expect worse sleep, maybe a dream, maybe irritability. Tell one person you went, which does more than almost anything else, because it moves the visit out of your head and into the ordinary world.

And notice what actually happened, specifically. I went. It was hard. Nothing bad happened. I came home. That sentence is the thing that makes the next visit easier, and it is easy to lose in the general feeling of having been through something.

When to get help

Please talk to a therapist who works with trauma before going back, if you can, especially if this is a place connected to something serious and you are being pushed toward it by a date on a calendar. Returning to a place is something therapy does deliberately, in a planned way, with support, and that is very different from doing it alone and hoping. See a professional too if the nightmares, jumpiness, or losing track of time have got worse since the visit, or if you have been avoiding a widening list of places for years. And if you are having thoughts of not wanting to be here, please tell somebody today. In the US you can call or text 988 at any time.

If you are in crisis, call or text 988 (Suicide & Crisis Lifeline, US) or your local emergency number. Theraglee is a wellbeing tool, not a substitute for professional care.
