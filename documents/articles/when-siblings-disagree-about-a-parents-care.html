When Siblings Disagree About a Parent's Care

One of you does the driving and the laundry, another has opinions from three states away. How to handle the old family patterns that surface when a parent needs help.

CaregivingFamily Visitor 968 words

You are the one who takes the calls from the hospital. Your brother thinks the doctors are being too cautious. Your sister has looked something up and thinks you should both be doing it differently.

None of you is wrong exactly, and all of you are frightened. But what comes out is a fight about a medication schedule.

Caring for an aging parent puts a family back into its oldest shapes. Whoever was the responsible one is responsible again. Whoever was protected is still being protected. And nobody has agreed to any of this out loud.

The fight is usually not about the thing

Arguments between siblings in this situation tend to be about logistics on the surface and something else underneath.

Underneath there is often fear, which comes out as urgency or as criticism. There is guilt from whoever lives furthest away, which can look like second-guessing the person doing the work. There is resentment from whoever is doing the most, which can look like refusing help when it is finally offered. And there is grief, early and unnamed, because you are all watching the same person change.

Knowing that does not settle a disagreement, but it changes how you hear one. Your brother questioning the care plan may be the only way he can say he is scared.

Separate the three things you are actually deciding

Most care disputes are three separate conversations tangled together, and untangling them helps enormously.

What is best for your parent, as far as anyone can tell. Who does what, practically. And who pays for what.

Try to take them one at a time, in that order, and say which one you are on. Half of these arguments are two people answering different questions in the same sentence.

Make the invisible work visible

The person doing the day-to-day care is usually carrying far more than anyone else can see: the appointments, the phone calls with insurers, the prescriptions, the laundry, the worrying at two in the morning.

If that is you, write it down. Not as an accusation, as a list. Hours, tasks, who it falls to. A list is harder to dismiss than a feeling, and it turns I am doing everything, which invites argument, into here is what there is, which invites help.

If it is not you, assume the list is longer than you think, and ask for it rather than for a summary. Then take something specific off it, permanently rather than once.

Ask for tasks, not for help in general

Help in general is what people offer and nobody delivers. A named task with a day attached is what actually moves.

So be concrete in both directions. Can you handle all the insurance calls from now on. Can you take Sunday afternoons. Can you be the one who talks to the pharmacy. Can you make the decisions about the house.

And accept that distant siblings can do real work remotely: paperwork, research, bills, scheduling, chasing. Somebody who cannot drive your mother to an appointment can still own everything that happens on a phone.

Hold one meeting, with an agenda

Care decisions made in group chats and in hospital corridors go badly. One scheduled conversation, with everyone on it, does better.

Keep it short and have three or four items. Start with what you all agree on, which is usually more than it feels like. Write down what was decided and send it round afterward, because people remember these conversations differently.

If it goes straight into old grievances every time, bring in a third party: a social worker attached to your parent's care, a geriatric care manager, or a mediator who works with families. That is not a failure. For many families it is the thing that finally allows a decision.

Remember whose life it is

The person most often left out of these arguments is the parent. If your mother can say what she wants, her view outranks all of yours, including when you think she is wrong.

Ask her directly and separately, away from the sibling with the loudest opinion. Ask what matters most to her rather than only what she will accept: staying in her house, not being a burden, keeping her routine, being near the grandchildren.

Where her choices carry real risk, the conversation is about reducing risk within what she wants, not about overruling her. And if her judgment is genuinely failing, that is a medical and legal question, with a formal process behind it, rather than something siblings should settle among themselves.

Accept that it will not be equal

It almost never is. Geography, work, money, and old history mean one person usually does more, and often that is the person it was always going to be.

You can get it fairer, but you probably cannot get it even. What helps most is having it said out loud: I am doing most of this, and I need two specific things from you. Unacknowledged imbalance is what turns into years of resentment; acknowledged imbalance is survivable.

And if a sibling will not help at all, you will get further planning around them than fighting for a version of them that does not exist. That is a loss worth grieving separately, with someone other than them.

When to bring in support

If the disagreements are constant, if you are not sleeping, or if the caring has taken over your own life, this is worth real support rather than endurance. A therapist can help with both the caregiving load and the family patterns underneath it, and many areas have free caregiver support groups where people are dealing with exactly these arguments. A doctor is also the right place to go if your own health has slipped while you have been looking after someone else's.
