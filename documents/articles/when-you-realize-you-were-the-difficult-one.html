When You Realize You Were the Difficult One

Something clicks and you see an old situation from the other side, and you do not come out of it well. What to do with that, how to tell useful remorse from the corrosive kind, and when to apologize.

ShameSelf-Compassion Visitor 906 words

Something makes it click. A comment from somebody, a situation you watch from the outside, a memory that arrives with the sound turned up. And you see a stretch of your own past from the other side, and you were not the one being wronged in it.

The first hour of that is genuinely unpleasant, and most people do not have anywhere to put it.

Why it arrives so suddenly

It is not that you were lying to yourself in any deliberate way.

At the time you had only your own view. Everyone operates from inside their own head, and the reasons for what you did were vivid while the effect on other people was not.

Something had to change before you could see it. Usually it is distance, or a different relationship, or having been on the receiving end of the same behavior from somebody else, which is the most common route by far.

And memory is reconstructed each time. You are not retrieving a fixed recording. You are rebuilding it with what you know now, which is why the picture can genuinely change.

Two kinds of remorse, and only one is useful

This is the distinction worth getting right, because the whole thing turns on it.

The useful kind is about the act. I did that, it affected them, I would do it differently. It is specific, it has an end, and it tends to produce a change or an amends.

The corrosive kind is about the self. I am that sort of person. It is global, it has no end, and it produces nothing but more of itself.

The tell is whether it leads anywhere. Remorse that keeps you up for three nights and changes nothing has stopped being about the other person, which is worth noticing even though it feels like the more serious version.

And the corrosive kind is not more moral. It usually feels like it is, which is why people stay in it. Sitting in self-attack is easier than making a repair and it costs the person you hurt nothing.

What to actually do with it

There is a short list, and most of it is more ordinary than the feeling suggests.

Write down what you did, plainly, without the case for the defense and without the flourishes. Most people find the plain version smaller and more manageable than the one that had been circulating in their head.

Separate what you did from what was happening. You were nineteen, or exhausted, or copying what you had been shown, or in the middle of something nobody knew about. Context is not an excuse and leaving it out is not honesty, it is just a worse account.

Work out whether there is a repair available. Sometimes there is and it is simple. Often there is not, and then the honest answer is to carry it and behave differently, which is less satisfying and still counts.

And notice where the behavior still runs. This is the part with any value in it. A pattern you can name in the past is usually visible somewhere in the present, in a smaller form.

About apologizing, years later

Worth doing sometimes, and it is worth being careful about whose benefit it is for.

Ask what it would do for them. An apology that lands well is one the other person can use. One that arrives out of nowhere and asks them to manage your remorse is a cost to them, not a gift.

Keep it short and make no requests. I have thought about how I behaved then, I was wrong, I am sorry. No explanation, no request for a reply, no suggestion of reconnecting unless they raise it.

Accept whatever comes back, including nothing. Some people will be glad. Some will not want the contact. Both are their call.

And do not do it if it would reopen something for someone who has moved on. Occasionally the generous thing is to carry it yourself.

Being fair to the person you were

This matters, and people in this state are usually extremely bad at it.

The fact that you can see it now is the relevant information. Someone who was simply that sort of person would not be having this reaction.

You are judging a past self with knowledge they did not have, which is not a fair trial. Apply the standard you would apply to anybody else in the same position and you will reach a different verdict.

And the other person's experience being real does not require yours to be fake. Both accounts can be true, and insisting that you must have been wholly in the wrong is just the corrosive version wearing a more honest face.

When it will not settle

If this is costing you sleep, if it has spread into a general sense of being bad rather than having done a bad thing, if you are avoiding people because of something years old, or if the thoughts arrive uninvited and will not go, that is worth taking to someone.

Shame is one of the things a therapist is most useful for, because it is very hard to get out of it alone. The work is practical: separating the act from the self, and working out what, if anything, is actually left to do. Your doctor can point you toward what is available if you are not sure where to start.
