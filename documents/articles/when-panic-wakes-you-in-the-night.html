When Panic Wakes You in the Night

Panic that starts in sleep arrives with no trigger you can name, which makes it frightening in a particular way. What is happening, what to do in the first few minutes, and how to get back to sleep.

PanicGrounding Visitor 997 words

You were asleep. Now you are sitting up in the dark with your heart hammering, your chest tight, and no idea what woke you. There was no nightmare you can remember. There was no noise. One second you were nowhere, and the next you were awake and frightened.

Panic that starts in sleep is one of the most unsettling versions of it. It arrives without a trigger you can point to, which makes it feel like it came from inside you, for no reason at all. That is not quite what happened, and the difference is worth knowing.

Why it feels worse in the dark

When panic hits during the day you usually have something to hang it on. You were in a crowded store. You were about to speak. Even if the reaction seemed out of proportion, there was a moment you can name. At night there is nothing. You wake into the feeling with no story attached, so your mind reaches for the worst available explanation, and at three in the morning the worst available explanation is almost always about your body failing.

The dark also takes away everything you normally use to steady yourself. No daylight, no sound, nobody moving around, nothing to look at. Your attention has nowhere to go except inward, onto your own chest and your own breathing, which is the exact place where attention makes things worse.

What is actually happening

Your body has an alarm system that can fire while you sleep. It runs on its own, without needing your thoughts to set it off. Sometimes it fires because of a shift in your breathing as you moved between stages of sleep. Sometimes it fires because the day left a lot of stress in your system. Sometimes there is no findable reason.

What the alarm does is the same whether it fires at noon or at four in the morning. It pushes adrenaline into your bloodstream. Your heart speeds up. Your breathing turns fast and shallow. Blood moves away from your hands and feet toward the big muscles, which is why the edges of you might feel cold or tingly. Your chest tightens because the muscles around it have braced.

Every part of that is uncomfortable and none of it is damage. The alarm is doing what it was built to do. It is doing it at a terrible time, for no good reason.

The first few minutes

Do not try to make the feeling stop. That is the instinct and it backfires, because trying hard to stop panicking is itself a kind of alarm. Give it somewhere to go instead while it burns off.

Sit up and put your feet flat on the floor. Lying down keeps you in the position your body associates with being stuck inside the feeling, and it keeps your breathing shallow. Upright, feet down, changes both.

Then slow the out-breath rather than the in-breath. Breathe in for a normal count and out for about twice as long, mouth a little open, like you are fogging a window. The long exhale is the part that tells your nervous system the emergency is over. Do it for a minute or two without checking whether it has worked yet.

Turn on a low light. Not the overhead one. A lamp, or the light from another room. You are giving your eyes something outside your body to rest on, which breaks the loop where you monitor your own heartbeat and your heartbeat answers back.

Name five things you can see, out loud if you can. This is not a distraction trick and it is not pretending nothing is wrong. It is handing your attention a job that is not your chest.

Getting back to sleep

Once the worst of it passes you will be wired and exhausted at the same time. Forcing sleep from there usually turns into an hour of lying still and getting angrier.

It works better to get up. Leave the bedroom. Sit somewhere else with a blanket and something undemanding for twenty minutes, then go back when you feel heavy rather than when you think you ought to. You are protecting what your brain has learned about your bed, which is that it is a place where sleep happens. Every hour spent awake and frightened in it teaches the opposite.

Keep your phone out of this. Looking up what a racing heart at night means will not reassure you and it will keep you awake.

What helps in the daytime

Night panic is often fed by things that have nothing to do with the night. Caffeine late in the day, alcohol in the evening, a long stretch of under-sleeping, or weeks of pressure you have been handling by not thinking about it.

The daytime work is unglamorous. Keep your sleep and wake times steadier than feels necessary. Move your body during the day so some of the stress gets spent before bedtime. Pull caffeine earlier. Give the hour before sleep to something that is not a screen and not a hard conversation.

Give the worry a slot while you are awake, too. If part of you spends every evening braced for the next episode, that bracing keeps the alarm sensitive. Ten minutes in the afternoon to write down what you are dreading takes some pressure off the small hours.

When to bring someone in

If this is happening often, if you have started dreading bedtime, or if you are cutting sleep short to avoid it, that deserves more than self-management. A therapist who works with panic can take you through the specific steps that make the alarm less jumpy, and that work is well understood and usually fairly short. One conversation with a doctor is worth having as well, partly to rule out the ordinary physical things that can wake you the same way, and partly so you can stop wondering.
