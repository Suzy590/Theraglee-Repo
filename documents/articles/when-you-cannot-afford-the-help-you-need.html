When You Cannot Afford the Help You Need

Everyone says get help and the hourly rate is more than you earn. Why this is a money problem and not a character one, the price question worth asking, and where lower-cost care exists.

MoneySelf-Compassion Visitor 822 words

The therapist costs more per hour than you earn. The medication is on a list you cannot afford. The specialist has a waiting list measured in months, or takes an insurance you do not have. And the advice everybody gives you is to get help.

That gap between knowing what would help and being able to buy it is its own particular misery, and it comes with a quiet verdict attached: that other people manage this, so there must be something wrong with you.

Name what is actually happening

This is a money problem wearing the costume of a personal failing, and separating those two is the first useful thing.

You are not failing to prioritize your health. You are being asked to buy something that costs more than you have, which is a different situation entirely and not one that willpower addresses.

Say it that way to yourself, because the self-blame is expensive. It makes people stop asking, stop looking, and stop telling anybody, which is what actually keeps them without care.

Ask the price question out loud

Most people never ask, and asking changes the number more often than you would expect.

So ask directly. What do you charge. Do you have a sliding scale. Do you have any lower-fee slots. Is there a payment plan. What is the cost if I pay directly rather than through insurance, which is sometimes lower.

Many therapists keep a few reduced-fee places and do not advertise them. Many practices have a hardship policy that is not on the website. The worst outcome of asking is a no, delivered by somebody who deals with this question weekly.

For medication, ask the prescriber the same way: is there a cheaper version of this, a generic, a different drug in the same family, a longer prescription that costs less per month, a manufacturer program.

Go looking in the places built for this

The paid, advertised version of care is the most visible and not the only one.

Community mental health centers, which charge by income. University training clinics, where supervised trainees see people for a fraction of the usual fee and the quality is often very good. Group therapy, which costs much less than individual and works well for a lot of things. Nonprofits built around a specific condition. Free helplines and text lines, which are not only for emergencies.

If you are employed, check whether there is an employee assistance program, which usually means a handful of free sessions and which an enormous number of people never use because they do not know it exists.

And ask your doctor what exists locally. They know the landscape and are rarely asked.

Use the free things properly rather than dismissively

The things that cost nothing are easy to wave away, and some of them do real work when used seriously rather than idly.

A good self-help book worked through week by week, with the exercises actually done, is a genuine treatment for some problems rather than a consolation prize. Free courses from reputable health services. A support group where people have the same thing you do. Libraries, which often have both the books and the rooms the groups meet in.

The difference between these helping and not helping is almost entirely whether you treat them as a course you are taking or something you dip into.

Do the things that are free and unglamorous

While you are looking, hold the floor. Sleep at the same time. Get outside daily. Move your body. Eat something regularly. Keep one person informed about how you actually are.

None of that is a substitute for treatment and all of it changes how bad the bad weeks get. It is also the part nobody can take away from you or put on a waiting list.

Get on the list anyway

If there is a waiting list, join it today even if it is long, because a long list that you are on beats a shorter one you never called.

Then ask what to do in the meantime, and whether there is a cancellation list, and whether they will tell you if something opens sooner. Ask every few weeks. People move up lists.

When to get help

Please tell your doctor plainly that cost is the obstacle, in those words, because it changes what they offer: a different medication, a local service, a referral into something funded, a letter that gets you seen. Call a free helpline in the meantime rather than waiting until things are worse, since they can also tell you what is available near you, which is often the hardest thing to find out. If you are in crisis, free emergency care exists regardless of what you can pay, and using it is not an imposition. And if you are having thoughts of ending your life, please reach out today rather than waiting for an appointment you cannot yet afford.

If you are in crisis, call or text 988 (Suicide & Crisis Lifeline, US) or your local emergency number. Theraglee is a wellbeing tool, not a substitute for professional care.
