When Meditation Makes You Feel Worse

Everyone says to sit quietly and breathe, and when you try it you get anxious, restless, or flooded. That is a known thing, not a failure. Why it happens and what to do instead.

MindfulnessTrauma Visitor 908 words

You downloaded the app. You sat down, closed your eyes, and followed the instruction to notice your breath. Within two minutes you were more anxious than when you started. Or your chest tightened. Or something you had not thought about in years arrived uninvited and you had to stop.

You concluded that you are bad at meditation. Meanwhile everyone around you describes it as a cure for everything, so you added it to the list of things that work for other people.

It is not a failure, and it is not rare. Sitting in silence is not a neutral activity, and for some people, in some circumstances, it makes things temporarily worse.

Why it can backfire

Turning your attention inward removes the distractions that have been keeping something at a manageable distance. When there is nothing to look at and nothing to do, what surfaces is whatever was underneath. For many people that is mild and passes. For some it is grief, old memories, or a level of anxiety that had been kept down by staying busy.

Attention to the body can be its own problem. If you have lived through something frightening, or have panic, the instruction to notice your heartbeat and your breath points you directly at the sensations you have learned to fear. Watching your heart closely is a reliable way to make it feel alarming.

And stillness itself can be activating. A body that is used to vigilance may read sitting motionless with eyes closed as unsafe rather than restful, and respond accordingly.

None of this means the practice is bad. It means it was delivered to you without the instructions that would have made it usable.

What to change first

Open your eyes. There is no rule requiring closed eyes. Let your gaze rest softly on the floor a few feet ahead. Most people who feel unsafe with eyes closed feel considerably better with them open.

Go much shorter. Two minutes, not twenty. Thirty seconds is a legitimate practice. The goal is to end while it is still tolerable rather than to endure it and dread the next one.

Move instead of sitting. Walking meditation counts. Attention on your feet as they land, on the air, on what you can see. So does washing dishes with full attention, or stretching slowly. Movement gives the body something to do, which for many people makes the attention easier rather than harder.

Point your attention outward, not inward. If the body is a difficult place to look, use the room instead: sounds you can hear, objects you can see, the temperature on your skin. That is still mindfulness. It just does not require you to monitor your own chest.

Anchor somewhere neutral. If the breath is loaded, choose your hands, your feet on the floor, the weight of your body in the chair, or the feel of a stone in your palm. The breath is the traditional anchor, not a required one, and for anxious people it is often the worst choice.

Stop when it gets loud

The instruction to sit with whatever arises is good advice for ordinary discomfort and bad advice when you are getting overwhelmed. If something surfaces that is bigger than the practice, stop. Open your eyes, stand up, look around the room, get a drink of water, go outside.

That is not giving up. Learning when to stop is what makes it possible to keep going at all. A practice you can end safely is one you will return to.

Skip the long retreats for now

Silent retreats, long sittings, and intensive courses can bring up a great deal, and they are a poor place to discover that stillness is difficult for you. Build tolerance in two-minute pieces at home before going anywhere that makes stopping awkward.

Know that you may not need it at all

Mindfulness is one tool among many, not a requirement for a well-lived life. If it does not suit you, the benefits people get from it, calm, perspective, being present, are available elsewhere: walking, swimming, gardening, playing music, praying, knitting, being absorbed in work with your hands. Those count, and for many people they are more effective than any app.

If you are working with old memories

If sitting quietly reliably brings up frightening memories, or leaves you feeling detached, flooded, or unsafe in your body, that is a signal to work with someone rather than on your own. There are trauma-informed approaches to mindfulness, taught by people who understand exactly this problem, and they usually start from the outside in: the room, the senses, the feet, safety first, interior later.

When to get help instead

Please talk to a therapist if quiet time reliably makes you distressed, if memories arrive that you cannot manage alone, if you feel unreal or far away during or after it, or if the practice you took up to feel better has become something you dread. A therapist who works with trauma can help you build the ground floor that most meditation instruction assumes you already have. And if anything that surfaces leaves you feeling hopeless or thinking of harming yourself, please reach out that day. In the US you can call or text 988 at any time. Nothing about this means you are doing it wrong. It means the standard instructions were written for a mind in easier circumstances than yours, and yours needs a different order of operations.

If you are in crisis, call or text 988 (Suicide & Crisis Lifeline, US) or your local emergency number. Theraglee is a wellbeing tool, not a substitute for professional care.
