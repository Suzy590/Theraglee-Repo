When You Want Less Than You Used To

The drive that used to be there has gone quiet, and you cannot tell whether that is wisdom or something wrong. How to tell depletion from a genuine change in what you want.

BurnoutGoals Visitor 862 words

You used to want things. The next role, the bigger project, the plan with dates attached. Now someone asks where you see yourself in five years and the honest answer is that you have not thought about it and do not especially want to.

Which would be fine, except you cannot tell whether this is a sensible change of priorities or something that has gone wrong and is quietly spreading.

The two things this can be

One is depletion. The wanting has not gone, it has been spent. This is what happens after a long stretch of too much, and it shows up as not caring about the thing you used to care about because caring costs energy you no longer have.

The other is a genuine change. The goals you had were real for the person you were at the time, and you have changed, and that set no longer fits. People reach this in their thirties and forties routinely, and it is not a malfunction.

They feel identical from inside, which is why so many people either push through something that needed rest, or rest through something that needed an honest rethink.

How to tell them apart

A few questions separate them reasonably well.

Is it everything or just this. Depletion is usually broad: work is flat, and so are food, friends, music, weekends. A real change in direction is specific. You have stopped wanting the promotion and you still light up about the thing on Saturdays.

What happens after real rest. Not a weekend. A proper break of a couple of weeks. If some wanting comes back, you were tired. If you come back rested and still do not want it, that is information of a different kind.

Is there relief underneath. Imagine it being formally off the table, forever. Depletion mostly produces fear or blankness at that. A genuine change often produces a quiet lift, which is worth taking seriously.

Can you still want anything. If you can picture something you would like, even unrelated and small, the machinery works. If nothing at all produces a flicker, that points toward depletion or something lower, and it is worth a conversation with a professional rather than a change of career.

If it is depletion

Treat it as recovery rather than motivation. The instinct is to find the thing that will make you care again, and that is the one approach that reliably fails, because it asks for output from a system that has none.

Reduce load before anything else. Specifically and actually, not in principle. Fewer hours, fewer commitments, fewer evenings spoken for.

Expect recovery to take longer than feels reasonable, and to be uneven. Months rather than a weekend, for a long stretch of too much.

Do not make big decisions while you are in it. Depleted people quit things they later wanted, because everything looks equally flat.

And look at what actually drained it. Usually it is not the hours alone, it is having no say over how the work goes, or doing a great deal that is never finished, or a long stretch with no visible result.

If what you want has really changed

Then the task is not reigniting the old goal. It is working out what replaced it, which usually takes a while and is not found by thinking harder.

Notice what you do want, in whatever small form it shows up. What you read when nobody is watching. What you talk about too long at dinner. The parts of your week you protect without being asked to.

Expect the new version to be quieter. People often read the absence of the old drive as having no ambition, when what they have is something steadier that does not feel like drive at all.

Be careful about what you dismantle. A change of direction does not have to be dramatic to be real, and plenty of people find the same job becomes tolerable once they stop expecting it to carry their whole sense of purpose.

And give yourself permission to want less. Wanting less is a legitimate outcome, not a failure to sustain ambition, and a lot of people arrive there after a decade of chasing and find it suits them.

Either way

Protect the part of your life that is not achievement, because both depletion and a change of direction get much worse in a life with nothing in it but the thing that has gone flat.

And do not rush the answer. This is one of the few questions where a few months of watching yourself tells you far more than a decision made now.

When to talk to someone about it

If the flatness covers most of your life rather than one part of it, if it has lasted months, or if it comes with poor sleep, low appetite or a sense that very little matters, that is worth taking to a doctor or a therapist rather than treating as a career question. Losing interest in one thing is ordinary. Losing interest in most things is something treatable, and it is a far more common reason to seek help than people assume.
