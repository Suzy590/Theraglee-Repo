Cooking for One Again

The recipes are for four, the portions are wrong, and the point of making dinner has gone. How to feed yourself properly when the household has shrunk.

FoodLife Transitions Visitor 883 words

You still buy the big bag of potatoes. The chicken is for a family. You have made a proper meal twice this month, and the other nights were toast standing up, or cereal, or nothing much at all.

Cooking for one after years of cooking for other people is not a smaller version of the same job. It is a different one, and nobody warns you.

Why the point goes out of it

Cooking for other people has a reason built in: they are hungry, they are coming to the table, they will say something about it.

On your own, all of that goes. Nobody notices, nobody thanks you, and there is no reason the meal has to happen at seven or at all. So it slides, and the sliding is not laziness; the structure that held it up has gone.

If the household shrank because somebody died or left, there is more in it than logistics. Mealtimes are where the absence sits most obviously, and a table laid for one says it out loud every evening.

Start with the table rather than the food

Where you eat changes what you eat, more than any recipe does.

Eating standing at the counter or on your lap in front of a screen tends to produce less food, eaten faster, with no sense of having had a meal. Sitting down at a table, with a plate, for ten minutes, changes the whole thing even when the food is simple.

Put something on: the radio, music, a show you like, a podcast. Many people find company in sound more useful than trying to eat in silence.

And if the table is unbearable because of who used to sit at it, eat somewhere else on purpose. A different room, a chair by the window, the garden. That is a reasonable adjustment rather than giving in.

Cook for two or four and plan the rest

The single most useful change: stop trying to cook single portions.

Make the amount the recipe says, eat one, and treat the others as meals rather than leftovers. Fridge for two days, freezer for later, labeled with what it is.

A pot of something on Sunday is three or four weeknights where the cooking is already done. Nobody who lives alone should be starting from raw ingredients every evening.

Freeze in single portions and in containers you can see into. Future you will eat what is visible and ignore what is a mystery.

Build a short list of easy meals

Five or six things you can make without thinking, from what you keep in. That is enough.

Eggs, in any form. Pasta with something. Baked potato with a tin of something. Soup and good bread. A stir-fry from frozen vegetables. Fish from the freezer with rice. Beans on toast with cheese, which is a meal and not a failure.

Keep the components in: eggs, tinned tomatoes and beans, frozen vegetables, frozen fish, rice, pasta, cheese, something for flavor. A kitchen stocked like that can always produce dinner.

And buy smaller where it saves waste. Frozen vegetables, frozen herbs, half a loaf in the freezer, tinned and dried things that do not go off.

Eat with people on purpose

The hardest part of this is not nutrition. It is that eating alone every night is quietly lowering.

So make some meals social, deliberately: one standing arrangement a week, a lunch rather than a dinner if evenings are hard, a cafe where you sit in, a community meal or lunch club, cooking for somebody occasionally, a video call with a plate in front of you.

Invite people round even though the house is different now. The first few times are strange and it gets easier, and feeding people is one of the better reasons to cook.

Lower the standard without abandoning it

There is a middle between a proper meal and nothing, and that middle is where most of your week should live.

A plate with some protein, something fresh and something warm counts. A sandwich with fruit counts. A shop-bought meal with a side salad is perfectly fine, and so are frozen ready meals if they get you fed.

What matters over months is regular eating rather than good cooking. Three reasonable things a day at roughly the same times does more for your mood than one impressive dinner every couple of weeks.

When it is more than a dull chore

Please talk to a doctor if you have been skipping meals for weeks, if you are losing weight without meaning to, if you have no appetite at all, or if cooking and eating have become impossible rather than tedious.

Those are common after a loss or a big change, and they are treatable; a doctor can also check whether anything physical is involved, and a dietitian or a nurse is a reasonable referral to ask for.

A therapist or a bereavement counselor is the right place for the part that is really about the empty chair, which is usually the actual problem rather than the recipes. And if money is the obstacle, ask at your doctor's office or a local advice service what exists — meal support, lunch clubs and food help are more widely available than most people realize, and widely unclaimed.
