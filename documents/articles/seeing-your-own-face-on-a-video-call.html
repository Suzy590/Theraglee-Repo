Seeing Your Own Face on a Video Call

A small window in the corner showing you, live, for an hour. Why video calls are harder than meetings in a room, and the settings and habits that take the edge off.

Social AnxietyShame Visitor 905 words

The call starts and there you are, bottom right, with the lighting from above and an expression you did not know you made. For the next fifty minutes you are both participating in a meeting and watching yourself do it.

By the end you have no clear memory of what was decided and a very clear memory of your own chin.

Why this is harder than a room

A video call asks you to do something nobody has ever had to do before: hold a conversation while continuously watching your own face.

In a room you cannot see yourself. On a call you can, in a small moving mirror, under unflattering light, from an angle nobody looks at you from in real life.

It also strips out the things that normally make conversation easy. Eye contact is impossible — you are either looking at them or at the camera. The tiny delays make you unsure whether to speak. Everyone is a motionless head, so you lose the ordinary signals that tell you how it is going.

Add a grid of other faces, any of which might be looking at you, and it is no wonder people find an hour of it more draining than a day in an office.

Turn your own picture off

Most platforms let you hide the view of yourself while everyone else still sees you. It is usually called hide self view.

Do that. It is the single biggest improvement available, it takes four seconds, and almost nobody knows it exists.

If you cannot find the setting, cover the corner of the screen with a sticky note. Low technology and completely effective.

And once your own face is gone, notice what happens to your attention: it goes back to the conversation, because self-monitoring was eating it.

Make the picture easier to live with

A few small changes, once, and then you never think about them again.

Put the light in front of you rather than above or behind: a window you face, or a lamp behind the screen. Overhead light is what produces the shadows people hate.

Raise the camera to eye level with a couple of books. A laptop on a desk shoots upward, which is the least flattering angle there is.

Sit back a little so your head is not filling the frame, and have something plain behind you.

None of that is vanity. It removes a real distraction, and most people find they feel noticeably more comfortable after ten minutes of setup.

Handle the attention, not the face

The uncomfortable feeling is largely self-focused attention: your spotlight is pointing inward, which makes every sensation and expression louder.

So point it outward on purpose. Listen to the actual words the other person is saying. Look at their face. Make a note by hand.

Keep your hands busy below the frame if that helps: a pen, a stress ball, your feet on the floor. And breathe out a little longer than you breathe in before you speak.

If you feel yourself heating up or reddening, let it happen rather than fighting it. The flush peaks and fades, and people on a screen notice it far less than you imagine.

Reduce the number of calls that need your face

It is worth saying plainly: not everything has to be on camera.

Cameras off for long internal meetings, audio only for one-to-ones where you are walking, a phone call instead of a video call when it would do. Many workplaces are happy with this and nobody has ever asked.

If a norm requires camera on, a short line usually settles it: I have mine off for the first part, I am listening. Or for a long day: I am going camera off between meetings to give my eyes a break.

And build in gaps. Back-to-back calls with no break is what turns ordinary discomfort into exhaustion.

Do not watch yourself back

Many platforms record, and the urge to watch your own contribution is strong.

Resist it, or watch it once with a specific purpose and then stop. Repeated reviewing is a form of checking: it narrows your attention onto what you dislike, makes it bigger, and produces no useful information.

The same goes for replaying the call in your head afterward. Write down any genuine action points, then do something else with your hands.

What people actually noticed

Almost nothing. They were looking at their own small window, worrying about their own lighting, and thinking about what to say next.

That is not reassurance; it is how attention works. Everyone on a video call is their own most watched participant.

When to get help with it

Please talk to a therapist or a counselor if the dread of being on camera is shaping your work: turning down meetings, avoiding promotion, feeling sick before calls, or spending a long time afterward going over how you looked.

Social anxiety responds very well to treatment, usually in a small number of sessions, and the approach is close to what is above: dropping the self-monitoring, doing the calls, and letting the discomfort fade through repetition rather than avoidance.

And if how you feel about your appearance is keeping you out of ordinary life, or you are spending a long time every day checking or comparing, mention that to a doctor too. Those feelings are common and treatable, and nobody working with them will find them trivial.
