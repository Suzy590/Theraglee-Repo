The Thoughts That Arrive Uninvited

A disturbing thought appears fully formed and you tell nobody for years. Why almost everyone has them, why they are not wishes, and why fighting them is what makes them stick.

Thinking TrapsShame Visitor 840 words

You are holding a knife at the kitchen counter and a thought arrives about using it. You are at the top of the stairs with the baby and your mind produces an image of dropping her. You are in a meeting and something obscene appears, fully formed, about the person speaking.

Then the second wave, which is worse than the thought: what kind of person thinks that. And you tell nobody, possibly for years.

Almost everybody has them

This is the piece of information that changes the most for people, so it is worth stating plainly. Unwanted, disturbing, out-of-character thoughts are a normal feature of human minds. Violent ones, sexual ones, blasphemous ones, ones about harm coming to people you love.

Minds generate a constant stream of material, most of it discarded before you notice. Some of it is strange. The difference between people is not whether these arrive but how much weight gets put on them.

New parents get them constantly, often about harm coming to the baby, which is both terrifying and extremely common. So do people who are exhausted, stressed or unwell.

They are not wishes

The reason these thoughts frighten you so much is that you are treating them as evidence about what you want. They are not.

In fact the pattern runs the other way. The thoughts that stick are the ones most opposed to what you care about, because those are the ones that generate alarm, and alarm is what makes a thought memorable and repeat.

A person who genuinely wanted to harm somebody would not be horrified by the thought. The horror is the proof that this is not who you are, which is exactly backward from how it feels.

Watch what the fighting does

Here is the mechanism that keeps people stuck. The thought arrives, it frightens you, so you try very hard not to have it.

Trying not to think something tells your mind that the thought matters, and your mind supplies more of what matters. The effort is the fuel.

So are the things you do to feel better: checking, avoiding the stairs, not holding the knife, not being alone with the child, mentally replacing the bad image with a good one, asking somebody to tell you you are not dangerous. Each of those brings relief for a few minutes and makes the next thought stickier, because it confirms the thought was a threat worth managing.

Change how you hold it rather than whether it comes

You cannot stop the thoughts arriving. You can change what happens in the next thirty seconds.

Name it flatly. That is an intrusive thought. There it is again.

Let it be there without arguing with it, agreeing with it, or analyzing where it came from. The analysis is another form of grappling.

Then carry on with what your hands were doing. Keep chopping. Keep walking down the stairs. Staying put is the part that teaches your mind the thought is not an emergency.

Expect it to feel worse before it feels better, because not performing the usual reassurance is uncomfortable the first several times.

Say it out loud to one person

The secrecy is doing more damage than the thoughts. People carry these for decades, convinced they are uniquely monstrous, and the moment of saying it to somebody is usually the turning point.

A professional is the best choice, because they will not be surprised and will know exactly what you are describing. A trusted person is also fine.

Keep in mind that this is not the same as asking for reassurance on a loop. Saying it once, properly, to be understood, is different from needing to hear you are safe fifteen times a day.

The honest line about safety

It is worth being clear, because people in this position worry about exactly this. Thoughts you find horrifying and do not want to act on are not the same as intentions, and having them does not make you dangerous.

What is different is a thought you find yourself drawn to, or a plan, or an urge that feels like something you might do. That is a reason to tell somebody today rather than to sit with it, and it is a different conversation from this one.

When to get help

Please talk to a therapist if these thoughts are taking up real time, if you are avoiding people or places because of them, or if you have built a set of rituals to keep them away, because this is one of the best understood and most treatable things a professional sees, and the treatment is specific rather than general talking. Tell your doctor too if you have had a baby recently and the thoughts are about the baby, since that is common and there is good help for it and nobody will think you are a danger to your child for saying so. And if you ever notice a thought you feel pulled toward rather than frightened by, please tell someone the same day.

If you are in crisis, call or text 988 (Suicide & Crisis Lifeline, US) or your local emergency number. Theraglee is a wellbeing tool, not a substitute for professional care.
