Grief Does Not Move in Stages

You may have heard grief has five stages. Real grief is messier, longer, and stranger than that. What it actually tends to look like, and how to get through a day of it.

If you are grieving, someone has probably told you about the stages. Denial, anger, bargaining, depression, acceptance. It is a tidy list, and it is comforting to think grief has a map. So it can be unsettling when your grief refuses to follow it. You feel fine on Tuesday and fall apart on Thursday. You feel acceptance and then, a year later, you are angry again. You wonder if you are doing it wrong.

You are not doing it wrong. The stages were never meant to be a schedule. They were an early attempt to describe something that does not go in a straight line, and they have been misread ever since.

What grief actually looks like

Grief is less like a staircase and more like weather. Some days are heavy and gray from the moment you wake up. Some days are almost ordinary, and that can feel like its own kind of betrayal. A song, a smell, a form that asks for an emergency contact, and suddenly you are back in the first week.

Over time, most people find that the weather shifts. The heavy days come less often, and they pass faster. The ordinary days stop feeling like a betrayal. But the loss does not shrink. What grows is your life around it. That is what people mean when they say you do not get over grief. You get bigger than it, slowly, in a way that leaves room for it.

The things nobody warns you about

Grief is exhausting in a physical way. You may sleep badly, or far too much. You may forget words, lose your keys three times in a morning, reread the same paragraph and take nothing in. That fog is normal. Your mind is doing enormous work in the background, and there is less left over for the surface.

Grief also brings feelings that do not sound like grief. Relief, if the person suffered, or if the relationship was hard. Guilt about the relief. Anger at the person for leaving, which makes no sense and is completely common. Envy of people whose lives kept going. Numbness, which can feel worse than pain because it seems like proof you did not care enough. You did care. Numbness is a way of surviving, not a measure of love.

And grief is not only about death. People grieve the end of a marriage, a friendship that faded, a job that was more than a job, a body that used to do things it cannot do anymore, a future they had planned. If you are grieving something other people do not recognize as a loss, it is still a loss, and you are still allowed to grieve it.

Getting through a day

You do not have to process your grief today. You just have to get through today. That is a smaller job, and it helps to treat it that way.

Eat something, even if it is toast. Drink water. Go outside for a few minutes, even to the mailbox. These sound trivial, and they are, which is why they are possible on a day when nothing else is.

Let people help with specific things. When someone says, let me know if you need anything, most grieving people never call. It is easier to accept an offer that is concrete: can I bring dinner on Wednesday, can I pick up the kids. If a friend asks what they can do, hand them one task. They want one.

Give yourself permission to do less. Grief takes up room, and something else has to give. The house can be messier. The emails can wait. You are not falling behind. You are carrying something heavy, and it is reasonable to carry less of everything else.

Let the waves come

When a wave of grief hits, the instinct is to fight it, to get somewhere private and push it down until it passes. Sometimes that is necessary. But when you can, try letting it move through you instead. Sit down. Breathe. Let yourself cry if the tears are there. Waves have a shape. They rise, they peak, they fall. The one you let through usually passes faster than the one you hold off.

You also do not have to feel it all the time. It is fine to take a break from grief. Watch something dumb. Laugh at a joke. Enjoy a meal. Nothing about that dishonors the person or thing you lost. Rest is part of how you get through.

When the days start to lift

At some point, you will notice you went a whole afternoon without thinking about it. That moment can bring a rush of guilt, as if forgetting for an hour means forgetting forever. It does not. It means your mind is learning to hold the loss and live at the same time. Both things are true, and both are allowed.

You may also find that the loss changes you in ways you did not choose but come to value. People who have grieved often say they are more patient, or more honest, or quicker to say the important things out loud. That does not make the loss worth it. It is just what sometimes grows in the ground it left.

When to reach for more help

Grief is not an illness, and most people move through it with time and the people around them. But sometimes it gets stuck. If months have passed and you still cannot function, if you cannot stop wishing you had died instead, if you are using alcohol or anything else to get through every day, please talk to a therapist or your doctor. Grief counseling exists because this is hard, and you should not have to figure it out alone. In the US you can call or text 988 at any time.

If you are in crisis, call or text 988 (Suicide & Crisis Lifeline, US) or your local emergency number. Theraglee is a wellbeing tool, not a substitute for professional care.
