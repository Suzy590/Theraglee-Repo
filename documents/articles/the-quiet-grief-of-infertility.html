The Quiet Grief of Infertility

Every month is a small loss, and almost nobody around you sees it. Why trying to conceive can be one of the loneliest kinds of grief, and what helps when hope keeps having to restart.

GriefLoss Visitor 863 words

Another month. The test, the waiting, the answer you did not want. And then, somehow, work in the morning, and small talk, and someone's pregnancy announcement in your feed before you have had coffee.

Infertility is a grief that most people carry in private. There is no funeral, no casserole, no one asking how you are holding up, because most of the people in your life do not know. And unlike most losses, it does not happen once. It happens again and again, and each time you have to rebuild hope in order to try, which means each time there is something new to lose.

Name it as grief

A lot of the pain gets worse because people tell themselves they have no right to it. Nothing happened. Nobody died. But something did happen. You are losing a future you had pictured, over and over. You may be grieving the pregnancy you imagined, the child you had already named in your head, the version of your life that was supposed to be happening by now.

That is real loss, and you are allowed to grieve it without apology or comparison.

The body makes it harder

Infertility is one of the few griefs that lives in your own body and often in a medical process. Appointments, injections, blood draws, timed intimacy, waiting rooms. Your body becomes a project with a monthly verdict, and it is easy to start feeling like it is failing you or that you are failing at something everyone else does by accident.

You are not failing. Bodies are complicated, and the reasons are usually far outside anyone's control or effort. This is not a test of how much you want it.

What it does to a couple

If you have a partner, this will press on the relationship. You may grieve at different speeds or in different ways: one wanting to talk about it constantly, the other wanting to think about anything else. One may be ready to stop or to try a different path while the other is not. Intimacy that has become scheduled and clinical can be hard to find your way back from.

Say these things out loud rather than assuming. I need to talk about it tonight. I need one evening where we do not. I am somewhere different from you on this and I do not want us to pull apart over it. Protect some time together that has nothing to do with trying.

Handling other people

Well-meaning people will say painful things. Just relax and it will happen. Have you tried this. At least you can travel. My cousin got pregnant the moment they stopped trying. Most of it comes from discomfort, not cruelty, and none of it needs to be absorbed as truth.

You are allowed to tell people what you need: I am not up for questions about this. Please do not give advice. I love you and I cannot come to the baby shower. You are also allowed to mute pregnancy announcements, skip the gathering, and protect yourself from the parts of the world that are hardest right now. That is not bitterness. It is care.

Let yourself have the bad days

Some days you will be functional and some days a stranger's stroller will undo you. You can be genuinely happy for a friend and devastated at the same time; both are true and neither cancels the other. Let the hard days be hard rather than adding guilt on top.

Find people who understand

This is a grief that eases with company. A friend who has been through it. A support group, in person or online, where nobody needs the background explained. A counselor who works with fertility. Being around people who understand the vocabulary and the calendar is a relief that is hard to describe until you have it.

Make room for questions about what comes next

At some point, many people face decisions about how long to keep trying, whether to pursue treatment or a different path to parenthood, or whether to build a life that does not include children. These are enormous questions and they do not have to be answered this month. When you do get there, it helps to talk them through with someone outside the situation, and to notice that choosing to stop is a decision made out of self-respect rather than defeat.

If it gets very dark

Month after month of loss can wear a person down badly. If you find yourself hopeless, unable to function, or having thoughts of harming yourself, please reach out today. In the US you can call or text 988 at any time.

When to get more support

If sadness is with you most days, if you have withdrawn from people you love, if anxiety is running your life, or if the treatment process has left you feeling numb and mechanical, please talk to a therapist, ideally one who works with fertility and loss. This is one of the hardest things a person can go through quietly, and you should not have to do it alone. Whatever happens with your family, your grief deserves care of its own.

If you are in crisis, call or text 988 (Suicide & Crisis Lifeline, US) or your local emergency number. Theraglee is a wellbeing tool, not a substitute for professional care.
