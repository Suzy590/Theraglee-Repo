The Shoulds You Carry Around

I should be further on. I should not need this much rest. Why rules that sound like standards tend to produce guilt rather than action, and how to turn one into something usable.

CBTEmotion Regulation Visitor 840 words

I should be over this by now. I should not need a nap at four in the afternoon. I should be able to handle my own mother. I should have a house by thirty-five.

Each of those sounds like a standard. Notice what they actually produce: not action, but a dull background guilt that follows you around.

What a should is really doing

A should is a rule you are holding yourself to, usually one you never consciously agreed to.

Most of them were installed rather than chosen: from a parent, a school, a religion, a job, a decade of advertising. And because they feel like obvious truths rather than opinions, they do not get examined the way a decision would.

The tell is the feeling that follows. A useful intention produces a plan. A should produces guilt, and guilt with nowhere to go turns into either paralysis or resentment.

The same rule aimed outward is just as costly: they should have called, he should know better, people should not behave like that. Those produce anger rather than guilt, and they tend to keep you stuck arguing with how the world is.

Say it out loud and listen to it

Take the sentence and say it in full, in your own voice: I should be able to visit my father every week without getting upset.

Said out loud, most shoulds sound less like a law and more like something a particular person once said. That is the point. The rules lose a surprising amount of power when heard rather than assumed.

Then ask three plain questions. Who says? Does this apply to everybody, or only to me? And what does holding this rule actually get me?

Quite often the answer to the last one is nothing except the guilt.

Replace it with a want or a will

The useful move is not to abandon your standards; it is to change the grammar.

I should exercise becomes I want to feel better, so I will walk twenty minutes after lunch. I should visit more becomes I will go every other Sunday and I will call on the weeks I do not.

Would like, want to, will, or choose to. Each of those keeps the value and removes the whip, and the striking thing is how much more often people actually do the thing once it is phrased that way.

And some shoulds should simply be dropped. I should enjoy my twenties. I should be a natural at this. I should not mind. Those are not standards; they are instructions about how to feel, and nobody can follow those.

Watch for the comparison versions

A large share of them are really about other people's timelines.

I should own a house, be married, have been promoted, be settled by now. Those arrive from looking sideways, and the sideways view is always of other people's edited highlights rather than their Tuesday afternoons.

The question worth putting in their place is what you want your life to contain, in your own words, this year. That is answerable. Being on schedule is not, because there is no schedule.

Notice the ones about feelings

The heaviest shoulds are often about emotion rather than behavior.

I should not be this upset. I should be grateful. I should have moved on. I should not be angry with someone who is dead.

Those add a second problem on top of the first: now you have the feeling, and the verdict about having it. And a feeling that is not allowed does not go away; it goes underground and comes out sideways.

The alternative is plain acknowledgment: I am angry, that makes sense given what happened, and I can decide what to do with it. The allowing is what lets the feeling move.

Keep the handful that are worth having

This is not an argument for having no standards at all.

A few rules are worth holding firmly: how you treat people, what you will not do, what you keep your word about. Those tend to feel steadying rather than crushing, which is a useful test.

If a rule makes you more careful, keep it. If it only makes you feel bad about yourself and changes nothing, it is doing no work, and it can go.

When to get help with it

Please talk to a therapist or a counselor if the guilt is constant rather than occasional, if the rules are strict enough that nothing you do ever counts, or if the voice saying them is harsh in a way you would never use with anyone else.

This is one of the most well-mapped areas in therapy: spotting a rule, testing it, and rewriting it is practical work that most people see results from within a handful of sessions. And if the weight of never being good enough has flattened your mood for weeks, mention that to a doctor too; it is common, treatable, and much easier to work on than it feels while the rules are still in charge.
