The Sunday Night Dread

The weekend is not over yet, but by Sunday evening the mood has already sunk. What that dread is actually about, and ways to make Sunday night, and Monday morning, easier to face.

WorkAnxiety Visitor 838 words

It starts somewhere around four in the afternoon on Sunday. A heaviness. The weekend still has hours left, but you cannot enjoy them, because part of you is already at your desk on Monday morning. By evening you are checking email you do not need to check, replaying last week, and lying awake rehearsing the week ahead.

If this is your Sunday, you are in enormous company. It even has a name: the Sunday scaries. It is so common that it is tempting to dismiss it as normal. It is normal. It is also worth taking seriously, because a dread that eats a quarter of every weekend is a lot of life.

What the dread is made of

Sunday dread is anticipation. It is not Monday itself; it is the story about Monday, run in advance, usually in its worst version. And because it is a story, it is vaguer and larger than the real thing. You are not dreading the specific 9 a.m. meeting. You are dreading a fog of everything, all at once, with no edges.

There is also a loss inside it. The weekend was yours, and it is ending. That is a small, real grief, and it gets louder when the weekend was too short, or too full of chores, or not restful.

And sometimes the dread carries a message about the job itself, which is worth listening to. More on that at the end.

Give Monday edges

The fog is worse than anything with a shape. So give Monday a shape, on Friday if you can, or early on Sunday if you cannot. Write down the three things that actually need to happen on Monday. Not the whole week. Monday. Look at the calendar and see what is actually there.

Most people find that the real Monday is smaller than the imagined one. The dread was about everything; the day is about three things.

Close the week before you leave it

Much of Sunday dread is unfinished business from Friday. The email you did not send, the task half done, the thing you were supposed to decide. Take fifteen minutes on Friday afternoon to write a short note to Monday-you: here is where things stand, here is what is next. Then close the laptop. You have handed the week off. There is nothing to carry.

Do not check email on Sunday

This one is simple and hard. Checking work email on Sunday does not reduce the dread. It feeds it. You see the problems without being able to solve them, and your mind chews on them for the rest of the evening. If you must look, set a time, look once, and close it. Better not to.

Put something good on Sunday evening

Sunday night is often left empty, the leftover part of the weekend, which is exactly where dread grows. Fill it with something you actually like. A standing dinner with someone. A show you save for Sunday. A walk. A bath and a book. When Sunday evening has its own pleasure, it stops being merely the on-ramp to Monday.

Make Monday morning easier

Some of the dread is about the friction of restarting. Lower it. Lay out clothes. Pack the bag. Decide breakfast. If you can, put something you enjoy at the start of Monday: a good coffee, a walk before work, a task you like first. Monday morning does not have to be the hardest part of the week. It has just been allowed to become it.

Rest so the weekend counts

Dread is worse after a weekend that did not restore you. If Saturday and Sunday were all errands and screens, you arrive at Sunday night depleted and the week looks impossible. Protect at least one block each weekend that is real rest, the kind that leaves you feeling more like yourself. Then Monday is being faced by a rested person, which makes a large difference.

Name it when it comes

When the sinking starts, say what it is. There is the Sunday dread. It is a feeling about Monday, not a fact about Monday. Then do one of the things above. Naming it does not dissolve it, but it stops it from running the evening unopposed.

When the dread is telling you something

Sometimes Sunday dread is not about weekends at all. It is about the job. If you dread Monday every single week, if the dread is not anxiety about tasks but a deeper sense of wrongness, if you feel relief only on Friday and heaviness every other day, that is information. It may mean the job, the manager, or the workplace is not one you can thrive in, and the question stops being how to survive Sunday and becomes what to do about the week.

That is a bigger question, and it is worth taking to a therapist, who can help you tell the difference between ordinary work stress and a situation that is costing you your health. You deserve a Sunday that belongs to you.
