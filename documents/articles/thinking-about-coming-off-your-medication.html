Thinking About Coming Off Your Medication

You feel better and you want to stop. What makes stopping go well, what makes it go badly, and the questions worth asking the person who prescribed it before you change anything.

DepressionLife Transitions Visitor 992 words

You have been on it a while. Things are steadier. And the thought arrives: maybe I do not need this anymore. Maybe I could do without. Maybe I should find out who I am without it.

That thought is reasonable and extremely common, and it deserves a proper conversation rather than a quiet decision one evening. What follows is not medical advice and cannot be, because this depends entirely on which medication, which dose, how long, and what it is treating. It is about how to approach the conversation and the months around it.

Start with the one rule that matters

Do not stop suddenly, and do not stop without telling the person who prescribed it.

Stopping abruptly is the single most common way this goes badly, and not because of weak willpower. Many of these medications need to come down gradually, and coming off too fast can bring on days or weeks of feeling genuinely unwell, which people then mistake for proof that their illness has come roaring back.

That matters, because stopping badly muddies the water. If you taper slowly and carefully, you and your prescriber can tell what is withdrawal and what is the original problem returning. If you stop overnight, nobody can tell, and you may end up frightened of ever trying again.

Work out why you want to stop

The reason shapes the plan, so it is worth being honest with yourself about which of these it is.

Side effects you can no longer live with. That is a strong reason, and often the answer is a different medication rather than none.

Feeling well and wanting to find out whether you still need it. Also reasonable, and usually a question of timing rather than yes or no.

Cost, appointments, or the difficulty of getting refills. Say this out loud to your prescriber, because there are often cheaper or simpler options.

Other people's opinions. Family who think you should manage without, or who do not believe in it. Their discomfort is not a medical reason, and you are allowed to say so.

Shame about needing it. This one is worth looking straight at, because it is a bad reason to stop and a very human one to hold. Needing medication to function well is not a character failing any more than needing glasses is.

Timing is most of it

A reasonable prescriber will usually talk about how long things have been stable, what else is going on in your life, and what happened the last time you stopped if you have tried before.

Coming off during a calm stretch is a different proposition from coming off in the middle of a move, a separation, a new baby or a bad patch at work. The medication is often doing more work than you can see when life is loud.

Ask for the plan in writing

Go to the appointment with questions and leave with specifics.

What is the schedule, in doses and weeks. What might I feel in the first two weeks of each reduction. Which of those feelings would be withdrawal and which would be a sign the problem is returning. How long does each step take to settle. What do I do if a step goes badly. Can I pause or go back up. When do we next speak. Who do I call if things get frightening.

A plan with named steps and a review date turns this from an experiment into a managed change, and makes it much less likely you will abandon it halfway in a bad week.

Keep a simple record

A line a day is enough: sleep, mood out of ten, anything physical, anything stressful happening. Three weeks of those lines are far more useful at your next appointment than trying to remember how the month went.

Records also protect you from your own memory, which tends to flatten everything into a single verdict. Two weeks of patchy days read very differently when you can see that six of them were fine.

Keep everything else in place

This is the part people skip. If the medication has been holding things up, taking it away while also stopping therapy, sleeping badly and doing nothing you enjoy is three changes at once.

Hold the rest steady, or strengthen it. Keep sleep roughly regular. Keep moving. Keep the people who know what is going on close, and tell at least one of them what you are doing and what to watch for, since others often notice a slide before you do.

Going back up is not failure

A fair number of people reduce, find out they are not ready, and go back to where they were. That is a useful result rather than a defeat: you now know something about what you need, which you did not know before.

The same is true of staying on it for a long time, or for good. Plenty of people do, on their prescriber's advice, and live full and ordinary lives.

Watch for the warning signs together

Agree in advance, out loud, what would mean stop and call. Sleep falling apart. The old thinking coming back. Not being able to work or get out of bed. Any thought of harming yourself.

If that last one appears, please do not wait for the next appointment. Contact your prescriber or an urgent service the same day. In the US you can call or text 988 at any time.

When to get help

Please talk to the doctor, nurse or psychiatrist who prescribes it before you change anything, including skipping doses to see what happens. Ask about a taper rather than a stop, ask for a review date, and say plainly if the real problem is cost, side effects or how it makes you feel about yourself, because all three have answers. If you are already off it and struggling, that is still worth a call today rather than waiting to see.

If you are in crisis, call or text 988 (Suicide & Crisis Lifeline, US) or your local emergency number. Theraglee is a wellbeing tool, not a substitute for professional care.
