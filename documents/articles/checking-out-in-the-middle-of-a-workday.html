Checking Out in the Middle of a Workday

You are at your desk, the screen is open, and you are not there. Why long stretches of the workday go missing, what it tends to mean, and how to get some of yourself back into the hours.

WorkBurnout Visitor 918 words

It is a quarter past four. You have been at your desk since nine. The document is open at the same place it was at two. You were not slacking off and you were not working. You were somewhere in between, staring at a screen with your hands near the keyboard, and the afternoon went past without you.

Then comes the second part, which is often worse: the feeling that you have wasted the day, followed by staying late to make up for hours you cannot account for.

What the checking out actually is

Attention is not willpower. It is a resource, and it runs down. When it runs far enough down, the mind stops engaging with what is in front of it and idles. You are awake, you look busy, and almost nothing is going in.

Work makes this especially likely for a few plain reasons. The tasks are often repetitive enough to need no decisions. The environment barely changes from hour to hour. There is no natural pause between things. And if the work carries any dread, if opening that spreadsheet means facing a conversation you are avoiding, the mind will drift away from it the way you would step back from something hot.

Long-running stress is the other big driver. When you have been braced for weeks, going flat is what a system does to get through the day at a lower cost. That version is one of the more common faces of burnout, and it is often mistaken by the person having it for laziness, which it is not.

Tell the difference between tired and hollow

It is worth knowing which one you have, because the fixes are different.

Ordinary attention fatigue comes and goes with sleep, lifts on a weekend, and responds to a walk and a glass of water.

The hollow kind does not lift. You are checked out in week five of being checked out. You feel nothing much about work you used to care about, you get through the day on a kind of autopilot, and the weekend does not restore you. That one is not solved with a better calendar. It needs a change in the load, and usually some help.

Give the day a shape

Blank stretches grow in long featureless blocks. Break the block.

Work in defined pieces with real edges: forty or fifty minutes on one named thing, then stop and move. Stand up, walk to a window, get water, step outside. The point is less the rest than the boundary; a day with edges in it is far harder to lose.

Name the next action out loud or on paper before you start. Not finish the report. Write the first paragraph of the summary. Vague tasks are the easiest ones to drift away from, because there is no particular moment of being in them.

Make the beginning physical

Starting is where most checking out begins, so set the first thirty seconds deliberately. Close the other tabs. Put both feet on the floor. Say the task. One slow breath out. It sounds like very little and it is the difference between entering the work and hovering near it.

Change something in the room when you feel yourself go

The fastest route back is through the body, not through resolve. Cold water on your wrists or face. Open a window. Move to a different chair or a different room. Walk up a flight of stairs. Eat something. Change the light. Any strong, specific physical input will do more in sixty seconds than another ten minutes of staring will do in an hour.

Look at what you are drifting away from

Sometimes the fog is not general. It arrives exactly when a particular task does. If you track it for a week you may find your blank afternoons cluster around the same piece of work, the same client, or the same looming conversation.

That is useful, because then the problem is not your attention. The problem is the avoided thing, and a hard half hour spent on the avoided thing can hand back several foggy days.

Protect sleep and food before blaming yourself

A lot of what gets diagnosed as a focus problem is six hours of sleep and no lunch. You cannot concentrate your way out of a body that is short on both. If it has been a long stretch of late nights and skipped meals, start there before you try any technique, because the techniques work poorly on a tired system and well on a rested one.

Say something, if it has been going on

If weeks are disappearing, it is worth telling someone at work something true and limited: that the load has been heavy, that you are losing time, that you need to look at what comes first. You do not owe anyone an account of your inner life. You are allowed to renegotiate work that has become impossible to be present for.

When to get help

Please talk to your doctor if you are losing stretches of the day regularly, since sleep problems, thyroid trouble, low iron, medications, and other treatable things do exactly this. If it has lasted months, if the flatness follows you home, or if you have stopped caring about things that used to matter, a therapist can help you tell burnout from depression and work out what needs to change. Losing an afternoon is not a verdict on your character; usually it is a signal about your load.
