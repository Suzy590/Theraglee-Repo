Starting Something You Will Be Bad At

Learning anything new as an adult means being visibly incompetent for a while. Why that is so much harder now, and how to get through the beginner stretch.

PerfectionismMotivation Visitor 955 words

You have wanted to learn it for years. The language, the instrument, the drawing, the swimming, the dancing. You have looked up classes twice and not booked one.

The obstacle is rarely time. It is that you would have to be bad at it, in front of people, for a while, and you are forty-three and have not been bad at anything on purpose since school.

Why beginning is harder than it used to be

As a child you were a beginner at everything, constantly and without comment. Adults are not. You have spent two decades becoming someone who knows what they are doing, and competence becomes part of how you are seen and how you see yourself.

So being a beginner is not just uncomfortable, it is a temporary loss of identity. For an hour a week you are the worst person in the room, and nobody is impressed by anything you do.

There is also simple exposure. You have more to lose in your own eyes, and nobody to tell you that being clumsy at a new thing is normal, because every adult around you is doing things they are already good at.

The beginner stretch is shorter than you think

Here is the thing worth knowing: the genuinely humiliating part is brief.

For most skills, there are a few weeks of being obviously terrible, then a long stretch of being unremarkable, which is much easier to live inside. The painful part is the first bit, and it passes faster than the dread suggests.

So the task is not to tolerate years of incompetence. It is to get through about six sessions.

Pick the setting deliberately

Where you learn matters more than how dedicated you are.

A beginners' class, explicitly labeled as such, is far easier than joining a mixed group. Everybody there is in the same position, nobody is watching you, and the teacher expects nothing.

A class where you know nobody is often easier than one where you do, because being bad in front of strangers costs less than being bad in front of someone who knows you as competent.

And a teacher who is good with beginners is worth more than a brilliant practitioner. Ask directly when you sign up: do you teach a lot of people starting from nothing.

Decide in advance how long you are giving it

The most common failure is quitting in week two for reasons you would not accept on reflection.

So commit to a number of sessions rather than to the activity: six classes, or eight weeks, or ten sessions. Pay for them up front if that helps. Then you are not deciding each week whether you are the sort of person who does this.

At the end of the commitment, decide properly. By then you will know whether you dislike the thing or only disliked not being good at it, and those are very different answers.

Change what you are measuring

If you measure output, you will be miserable. The output is bad; that is the definition of a beginner.

So measure attendance and small specifics instead. I went. I can say four sentences. I did not stop in the middle this time. I got the first bar right.

Keep a two-line record of those, because progress at the beginning is invisible from inside and extremely clear in a log four weeks later.

What to do with the self-conscious feeling

It will arrive, usually in the first ten minutes, and the useful response is not to argue with it.

Name it briefly and keep going: this is the part where I am bad at it. That sentence is more effective than reassurance, because it is accurate and it does not require you to feel differently.

It also helps to notice what is actually happening in the room. Everyone else is thinking about their own hands. The attention you feel on you is almost never there, and in a beginners' class absolutely nobody has spare capacity to judge you.

And if you make a mistake in front of people, the recovery is simply to carry on. Apologizing at length for being a beginner draws more attention than the mistake did.

Be careful with the two shortcuts

Studying it alone first, until you are good enough to go to a class, is the most common one. It sounds sensible and it usually means you never go, because the bar keeps moving.

And doing it only in private, forever, is the other. For many things that is fine. But if you want to play with people, speak to people, or swim in the sea, at some point the public version has to happen, and it is easier early than after years of avoiding it.

What it gives you beyond the skill

Most people who get past the beginner stretch report something they did not expect: being bad at something gets easier in general.

The tolerance you build transfers. It becomes easier to ask basic questions at work, to try things with your children, to be new in other rooms. That is a bigger return than the instrument.

When the block is bigger than nerves

If the idea of being seen as incompetent is intolerable rather than uncomfortable, if you only do things you already excel at, or if you have given up on a long list of things you genuinely wanted because of how the early part felt, that pattern is worth working on with someone.

It is common ground in therapy and it moves. A therapist can help with the part underneath, where being good at things and being acceptable got tied together, and that tends to free up far more than any individual class.
