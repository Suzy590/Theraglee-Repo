Naming What You Feel

Bad, stressed and fine cover a lot of ground. Why putting a more exact word to a feeling changes it, and how to do that when you have no idea what you feel.

Emotion RegulationMindfulness Visitor 926 words

Someone asks how you are and you say stressed. Later you say you had a bad day. Both are true and neither tells you anything you can use.

A feeling you cannot name tends to run the evening. A feeling you can name, specifically, becomes something you are having rather than something you are.

What naming actually does

Putting a precise word to an emotion does a few things at once.

It separates you from it. There is a difference between I am furious and I notice I am resentful about the way that was decided. The second one has you in it, looking.

It points at the cause. Vague bad has no direction. Resentful, disappointed, embarrassed, lonely and overwhelmed each point somewhere specific, which is the first step toward doing anything about it.

And it tends to lower the volume. Describing a feeling precisely is a different mental activity from being carried by it, and people usually report that the intensity drops a little as soon as the word lands.

The words most of us are missing

Most people run on about five: fine, tired, stressed, annoyed, sad. The useful vocabulary is bigger and not very complicated.

Under stressed, there is often: overwhelmed, rushed, pressured, dreading something, resentful, or simply exhausted. Those need completely different responses.

Under annoyed: irritated, offended, disrespected, impatient, or hurt. The last one is the most commonly mislabeled emotion there is; a great deal of anger is hurt wearing something sturdier.

Under sad: disappointed, lonely, grieving, flat, homesick, regretful, defeated.

And under fine: numb, braced, relieved, quietly furious, or genuinely all right, which is also allowed.

Start with the body, not the word

If someone asks what you feel and your mind returns nothing, that is normal and the way in is through sensation.

Where is it, and what is it like. Chest tight. Stomach hollow. Jaw clenched. Shoulders up. Hands restless. Heavy, like being pressed down. Fizzing.

Then guess, loosely. Heaviness and slowness often go with sadness or exhaustion. Tight chest and fast breathing with fear. Hot face and clenched jaw with anger. Hollow stomach with dread or shame.

A guess you can correct is far more useful than nothing. You will usually know within a few seconds whether the word fits.

Ask what it wants you to do

Every feeling comes with an impulse attached, and the impulse is often the clearest clue to the name.

If you want to hide, that is usually shame or embarrassment. If you want to argue, anger. If you want to leave, fear. If you want to stop moving, sadness or exhaustion. If you want to check something repeatedly, anxiety.

So the question what do I want to do right now often answers what am I feeling faster than introspection does.

Expect more than one at once

The most common reason people cannot name a feeling is that there are two, and they conflict.

Relieved and guilty. Grieving and free. Proud and resentful. Loving someone and wanting them to leave the room.

Mixed feelings are not confusion; they are the normal state for anything that matters, and insisting on one answer is what makes them hard to identify. Name both. And is usually the right conjunction.

Say it out loud or write it down

Thinking it is weaker than saying it, which is weaker than writing it.

A sentence is enough: I am disappointed about the meeting and embarrassed that I pushed for it. Three lines in a notebook does more than an hour of turning it over, because turning it over in your head tends to be rumination rather than naming.

If you are telling someone else, the format that lands best is simple: I am feeling X about Y. It is also enormously more useful to the other person than being told you are fine.

What not to do with the name

Do not use it as a verdict on yourself. Noticing jealousy is not a confession of being a jealous person, and the people who name feelings most accurately are usually the ones who treat them as weather rather than character.

Do not stop at the name either. Naming is a first move. The second is deciding whether the feeling is telling you something to act on, something to say, or something to sit with until it passes.

And do not expect it to work during a peak. At the top of a wave, nothing verbal loads. Name it afterward, and use something physical in the moment.

Build the habit cheaply

Once a day, at a fixed time, ask: what am I feeling, in one or two specific words, and about what. Thirty seconds.

People who do this for a couple of weeks tend to report the same thing: fewer evenings hijacked by something they could not identify, and conversations that go better because they can say what is wrong before it comes out sideways.

When you genuinely cannot find anything

Some people reach for a feeling and find a blank, consistently. That has several ordinary explanations: exhaustion, long-running low mood, a childhood where naming feelings was not safe, or the numbness that can follow something frightening.

None of those is a character flaw and all of them respond to help. If you are flat most of the time, if you only notice feelings as physical symptoms, or if looking inward brings distress rather than information, that is worth taking to a doctor or a therapist, who can help you get the vocabulary back rather than leaving you to guess.
