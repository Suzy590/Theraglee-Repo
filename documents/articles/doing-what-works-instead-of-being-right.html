Doing What Works Instead of Being Right

You are correct, and the conversation is going nowhere. A skill for the moments when insisting on the principle costs you the thing you actually wanted.

DBTGoals Visitor 882 words

You are right. The charge really was wrong. He really did say he would do it. The appointment really was booked for Tuesday.

And you have been on the phone for forty minutes, or arguing in the kitchen for an hour, and you are no closer to the refund, the help, or the evening you wanted to have.

Being right and getting what you want are different goals

Most of us are taught that if we are right, pressing it is the correct thing to do. In practice that collapses two separate questions.

One is what is true. The other is what you want to come out of this. Sometimes pressing the first gets you the second, and sometimes it is exactly what stops you.

The skill is noticing which situation you are in, and choosing the one that matters more here. That is not giving in, and it is not pretending you were wrong. It is deciding where to spend your effort.

Name the actual goal first

Before the next sentence, work out what you want when this is over.

The parcel replaced. A peaceful dinner. Your father to accept the handrail. To get out of the shop in ten minutes. Your teenager to still talk to you next week.

Then look at whether what you are about to say moves you toward that or away from it. An awful lot of what people say in these moments is aimed at the record rather than at the result.

And be honest when the goal really is the principle. Sometimes it is: a complaint that should be on file, a line that needs saying out loud. Fine — then pursue that deliberately, in writing, to someone who can act, rather than by repeating yourself to someone who cannot.

What this looks like in practice

With a call center, the person on the phone did not make the policy. Getting them on your side works better than proving them wrong: I know this is not your doing, I am stuck, what are my options?

With a relative who will not accept help, winning the argument about whether they need it generally loses the next six months. Dropping the argument and making the help smaller gets further.

With a teenager, being right about the mess tends to cost you the conversation about the thing that actually matters.

With a partner mid-argument, the moment you start assembling evidence is usually the moment the evening is lost. Who said what three weeks ago is almost never the real subject.

The useful question in all of those: is this working?

Letting go of the last word

The hardest part is the final sentence, the one that proves the point. That sentence is where most of the damage lives, and it is nearly always optional.

Try leaving it unsaid. Not swallowing your view — you can say it once, plainly — but declining to say it a fourth time.

If you need something to do instead: ask a question, take a breath and name the goal to yourself, or say I think we see this differently and I would like to sort the practical bit.

And notice how quickly things move once somebody stops needing to be agreed with. Often the other person concedes the point later, when there is nothing to defend.

Where the urge comes from

If letting a wrong thing stand feels unbearable, that is usually about something older than the parcel.

People who were often blamed unfairly, or never believed, or made to apologize for things they did not do, tend to find being right enormously important, and for good reason. The need to establish the record was once protective.

Knowing that does not make it go away and it does change what you do with it. The question becomes: do I need this person to agree, or do I need the outcome? And sometimes: who can I tell about this afterward who will believe me, so I do not have to win it here?

Decide in advance where you will stop

This works much better planned than improvised.

Before a call, decide what a good result is and how long you will spend. Before a visit, pick the one or two things that genuinely matter and let the rest go visibly. Before a hard conversation, write the sentence you want to land in one line.

Then stop when you said you would, even if the other person is still wrong. Writing the complaint down, escalating it properly, or walking away are all real options and none of them require agreement.

When to get help with it

Please talk to a therapist or a counselor if arguments are taking over your relationships, if you cannot let things go even when it is costing you, or if the need to be believed is strong enough to keep you locked in conflicts with family, neighbors or an organization.

This is standard therapy ground and the skills for it are teachable rather than a matter of character. And if the anger and rumination around it are keeping you awake, or you are drinking to manage them, mention that to a doctor as well; both are common, treatable, and far easier to work on with help than alone.
