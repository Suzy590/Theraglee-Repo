Keeping the Promises You Make to Yourself

The promises nobody else knows about are the ones that quietly decide whether you trust yourself. Why most of them were built to fail, how to make one that holds, and what to do when you break one.

DBTSelf-Esteem Visitor 850 words

You said you would go to bed at eleven. You said this week you would call about the appointment. You said you would not check the account again tonight.

None of these were promises to anybody else, so nothing happened when you broke them. Except that something did, quietly, and after enough of it you start treating your own intentions as suggestions.

What breaking them actually costs

The missed task is usually the smaller half of it.

You stop believing yourself. Once there is a long enough history, saying I will do it tomorrow carries no weight even while you are saying it, which takes the usefulness out of planning.

The evidence stacks up on one side. Most people keep a running account of what they said and did not do, and almost nobody keeps the matching list of what they did.

It gets attached to character. The gap between intention and action is read as a statement about what sort of person you are, rather than as information about a promise that was badly built.

And it spreads. People who have stopped trusting their own word about small things tend to stop making commitments about large ones, which is a quiet kind of shrinking.

The promises were usually impossible

Before treating this as a discipline problem, it is worth looking at what was promised, because most of these are set up to fail.

They are too big. I will exercise every morning, said on a Sunday evening, is a prediction about forty mornings by somebody who has only met one of them.

They are vague. I will be better about money has no first action and no finish, so there is no moment at which it is kept.

They are made in a different state. Promises made in a burst of energy or guilt are written by a version of you who will not be there on Tuesday, and that version systematically overestimates.

And they are secret, so there is no cost to dropping them and nothing to notice when they go.

Building one that holds

The repair is not more resolve. It is smaller promises and better construction.

Make it embarrassingly small, on purpose. One page, five minutes, one phone call. The point at this stage is not the result. It is rebuilding a record of your own word being good.

Name the first action rather than the goal. Not I will sort out the insurance, but I will find the policy number. A promise you cannot picture starting is not a promise.

Attach it to something that already happens. After I brush my teeth, after I make coffee. Time of day is a weak anchor and an existing routine is a strong one.

And say it to one person, or write it where you will see it. Not for accountability in the heavy sense, only so that it exists somewhere outside your head.

When you break one anyway

This is the part that decides whether the whole thing works, and most people get it backwards.

Repair it rather than restarting. Doing a smaller version the next day keeps the record intact. Writing the week off and beginning again on Monday is what turns one miss into a month.

Renegotiate out loud instead of quietly failing. I am not going to do thirty minutes, I am going to do ten, is keeping a promise in a modified form. Silently not doing it is the thing that costs.

Cancel the ones you no longer mean. A promise you have decided against is not a failure, and formally dropping it is much better than leaving it on the list to accuse you.

And notice what a miss does to how you see yourself. The usual response is a short attack on your own character, which has never once produced a kept promise and reliably makes the next one less likely.

What gets rebuilt

Keeping small promises does something out of proportion to the tasks involved.

You get a reason to believe the next one, which is what makes planning worth doing at all.

You get a kind of self-respect that does not depend on anyone else noticing, which is a steadier supply than the sort that comes from other people.

And you get accurate information about what you can actually do, which is more useful than either an inflated estimate or a deflated one.

When it is more than this

Sometimes the reason the promises are not being kept is not the promises.

If you cannot do what you intend because there is no energy for any of it, if everything has flattened out, or if the attacks on yourself have become the loudest thing in your day, that is worth bringing to someone rather than treating as a problem of willpower.

A therapist can help with both halves of this, and the work is specific: what you are actually committing to, and what you say to yourself when it does not happen. Your doctor can point you toward what is available if you are not sure where to start.
