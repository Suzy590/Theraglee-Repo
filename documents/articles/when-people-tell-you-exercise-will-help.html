When People Tell You Exercise Will Help

The advice is true and it skips the hard part. What movement actually does, how to make a first version small enough to be possible, and what to say to the person who keeps suggesting it.

DepressionMovement Visitor 855 words

Someone has told you that exercise helps. Maybe several people have. Maybe an article, or a doctor, or a relative who means well. And you know they are not wrong, which is the irritating part. You have heard it often enough that it has stopped being information and turned into one more thing you are failing at.

This is about what to do with that advice when getting up off the couch is the actual problem.

Why the advice lands badly

Because it skips the hard part. Telling someone whose energy is gone that movement will give them energy is true and useless at the same time, in the way that telling someone with no money that a job would help is true and useless.

It also arrives with a tone. Usually without meaning to, the advice carries a suggestion that you simply have not thought of it, or that you are choosing this. You have thought of it. You think of it most days.

And it sets up a test you can fail. Once movement becomes the thing that would fix everything, every day you do not do it becomes evidence against you. That is heavier than what you started with.

What is actually true, said properly

Movement does change how you feel, and the change is real. The honest version just has conditions that the slogan leaves out.

It works in much smaller amounts than people imply. Not an hour at a gym. Minutes.

It works better as a habit than as an event. The fourth short walk in a week does more than one heroic effort on a Sunday.

It works on the next hour or two, not on your whole life. That distinction matters, because if you expect to feel transformed and you only feel slightly less awful, you will decide it did not work and stop.

And it is not a replacement for treatment. It helps alongside other things. Anyone presenting it as a substitute for help is overselling it.

Making it small enough to be possible

The most common reason this fails is that the first attempt is far too big. Pick something so small it feels faintly ridiculous.

Walk to the end of the block and come back. Not around the block. To the end and back.

Or put your shoes on, go outside the front door, and stand there. That is the whole plan. If you then walk, good. If not, you still did the plan.

Or three minutes of moving around indoors. Putting things away counts. Going up and down the stairs twice counts.

The point of a tiny version is not the exercise. It is to break the rule where anything short of a real workout does not count, because that rule is exactly what keeps people completely still.

Lower the cost of starting

Most of the effort is not the moving. It is the deciding, the changing, and the hunting for things.

Keep shoes by the door. Pick one route you do not have to think about. Put it at the same time every day so there is no decision left to make, and keep it the same length so there is nothing to negotiate.

Do not weigh yourself, time yourself, or track it on anything that produces a number. A number creates a performance to measure against, and measuring is the opposite of what helps here.

What to do with the days you miss

There will be days you do not do it. Expect them and decide now what happens next, because the recovery rule matters far more than any run of good days.

The rule that works is this: never two in a row, and if there are two in a row, do the three-minute version rather than nothing at all. Not as a punishment. As a way of keeping the thing from going completely cold.

And drop the moral language. You were not good today and you were not bad today. You moved or you did not.

What to say to the person who keeps suggesting it

You do not owe anybody a report. But if the advice keeps coming and it is wearing on you, it is fair to say something plain. I know it helps. Getting started is the part I am stuck on. If you want to help, come walk with me on Saturday.

That turns a lecture into a plan, and it gives a well-meaning person something useful to do with their concern.

When this needs more than a plan

If the heaviness has gone on for weeks, if things you used to care about have stopped interesting you, or if your sleep and appetite have shifted, then movement is worth doing and it is not the main treatment. Talk to a doctor or a therapist. Low mood that sticks around tends to respond well to proper help, often better than people expect, and starting that conversation does not commit you to anything. If things feel darker than that, do not wait for an appointment. In the US you can call or text 988 at any time.

If you are in crisis, call or text 988 (Suicide & Crisis Lifeline, US) or your local emergency number. Theraglee is a wellbeing tool, not a substitute for professional care.
