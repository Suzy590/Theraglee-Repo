Testing What You Believe About Yourself

Arguing with a belief like nobody wants me here rarely shifts it. Setting up a small real-world test does. How to design one and read the result honestly.

CBTSelf-Esteem Visitor 978 words

There is something you believe about yourself that you cannot talk yourself out of. If I say what I think, people will think less of me. Nobody actually wants me there. If I ask for help, they will see I cannot cope.

You may even know, intellectually, that it is probably not true. That changes almost nothing, which is the frustrating part. Beliefs about yourself are not held in place by logic, so logic does not move them.

What does move them is evidence you collect yourself, on purpose. In therapy this is called a behavioral experiment, and it is one of the more powerful ordinary things you can do.

Why arguing with it does not work

When you reason with a belief like this, you are producing counterarguments while the belief keeps its grip on your actual experience.

There is a second problem. Most of these beliefs are protected by avoidance. If you never speak up in meetings, you never find out what happens when you do, so the belief is never tested and never updated. It stays intact because it has been kept away from reality.

So the job is not to think better. It is to arrange a specific situation where the belief has to make a prediction, and then to find out.

Write the prediction down first

This step is what separates an experiment from just doing the scary thing.

State the belief in plain words: if I disagree with my manager in the meeting, she will be annoyed and I will look stupid.

Then make it specific and checkable. What exactly do you predict will happen? How likely do you think it is, out of ten? What would you expect to see: her tone, what she says, what happens afterward?

Writing it down beforehand matters because memory is not neutral. After the fact, a belief like this will quietly rewrite what you predicted and what occurred.

Design something small and real

A good experiment is small enough to do this week and real enough to count.

If the belief is about speaking up: say one sentence of disagreement in one meeting. Not a campaign, one sentence.

If it is about asking for help: ask one person for one small thing and watch what they do.

If it is about being unwanted: send one message suggesting a coffee, and see what comes back.

If it is about appearing imperfect: send a piece of work that is good but not polished, and watch what happens next.

Pick the version you would rate about a six out of ten for difficulty. A two teaches you nothing and a nine usually does not happen.

Then run it and watch

Do the thing, and your only job in the moment is to observe rather than to perform well.

Note what the other person actually said and did. Note the facts rather than your reading of them: she asked a follow-up question, he said yes and sent it that afternoon, two people agreed with me, nobody mentioned the typo.

Afterward, write what happened next to what you predicted, and re-rate the belief out of ten.

Be careful of the three ways people spoil a result. Secretly hedging, so the test never really happened. Deciding in advance that any outcome proves the belief. And discounting a good result because they were just being polite, which is the belief defending itself.

Read the result honestly, including the bad ones

Most experiments come back somewhere between fine and unremarkable: the thing you feared did not happen, and nobody treated it as a big moment. That is the usual result, and it is worth sitting with rather than moving straight on.

Sometimes it goes badly. Someone is short with you, or the suggestion is turned down. Then the question is not was I right, it is what actually happened and what does it mean.

Being turned down once is not nobody wants me there. A manager being snappy on a Thursday is not if I disagree I will be humiliated. And even when the outcome is genuinely unpleasant, you usually find you coped with it, which is its own piece of evidence against the prediction underneath.

One test is not enough

A belief that has been running for twenty years does not fall over after one meeting.

So run the same kind of test a handful of times, in different places, and keep the notes somewhere you can read them. The accumulated record is the thing that eventually outweighs the story, and it works precisely because you gathered it rather than being told it.

Over time, notice whether the belief changes shape rather than disappearing: from nobody wants me there to some people do and it is worth asking. That is what success looks like here.

Where this is not the right tool

Do not experiment on something that could actually harm you. If you suspect a manager is genuinely hostile or a relationship is unsafe, the belief may be an accurate reading of a bad situation, and testing it is not the move.

And if the belief sits on something that happened to you, especially early on or something frightening, experiments can open more than you expected. That is worth doing with support rather than alone.

When to do this with help

This is standard cognitive behavioral work, and it goes faster with a therapist, who will help you pick tests that are hard enough to matter and will catch the ways you discount the results.

If the belief is one of the deep ones, if it has been there as long as you can remember, or if it comes with low mood, hopelessness, or avoiding so much of life that there is little left to test, please take it to a doctor or a therapist rather than working at it alone.
