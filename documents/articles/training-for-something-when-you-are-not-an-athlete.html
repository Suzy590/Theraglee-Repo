Training for Something When You Are Not an Athlete

A charity 5K in April and a loud voice saying you are not that person. How to pick the event, train three times a week badly, and get past the weeks that feel worst.

MovementGoals Visitor 824 words

Somebody mentioned a charity 5K in April. Or a charity walk, or a swim, or a bike ride. And a small part of you wants to do it, and a much louder part is pointing out that you are not a person who does things like this.

You do not need to be. Events like these are mostly full of people who were not athletes a few months earlier, and the training plan for an unfit beginner is far gentler than you would guess.

Pick the event for the date, not the distance

The useful thing about signing up is not the distance. It is that a date exists and other people know about it.

So choose something ten to sixteen weeks out, which is enough time to prepare without being so far away that it stays theoretical.

Pick the smallest version available. The 5K rather than the 10K. The shorter route. The walk rather than the run. Finishing comfortably teaches you that you can do this, and struggling badly teaches you that you were right about yourself, so stack the odds.

And tell two people, or put a sponsorship page up. It is slightly embarrassing and it is the strongest commitment device available.

Train three times a week, badly

Beginners do too much in week one, hurt something or exhaust themselves, and stop by week three. That is the single most common way this goes wrong.

So three sessions a week, not five. Two short, one slightly longer. A rest day between each.

Go slower than feels right. For running, slow enough to hold a conversation, which for most beginners means a pace that feels almost silly. Walking and running in alternating chunks is a perfectly respectable way to train and to finish.

Add about ten percent a week to the long one. If a week goes badly, repeat it rather than pushing on.

Follow a plan somebody else wrote. A free beginner plan from a reputable source removes the daily decision about what to do, and the daily decision is what erodes.

Expect it to feel bad before it feels good

The first two or three weeks are the worst of it, and people quit there believing it will always feel like that.

It will not. Somewhere around week four most people notice that the thing that was hard at the start has become merely unpleasant, and that is the point everything changes.

Aches in the muscles are normal. Sharp pain in a joint, pain that gets worse as you go, or pain that is still there the next day is a reason to rest and ask somebody, not to push.

Get shoes that fit, from a shop that watches you walk if you can, because feet and knees are what end most beginner plans.

Sort the small practical things early

The things that stop people are rarely about fitness.

Decide when you are going, in advance, on the calendar, as appointments. Put the clothes out the night before. Know your route. Have a wet-weather version and a dark version.

Eat something an hour or two before. Drink water. Learn where the bathrooms are on your route, which nobody mentions and everybody needs.

Find one person to do it with, or a beginners' group, which most towns have and which is far friendlier than people fear.

Plan the week of the event

Do less, not more. The instinct is to cram a hard session in. Rest instead, with one easy outing a few days before.

Read the information. Know the start time, the parking, where to leave your bag, where you need to be. Nerves on the day are mostly logistics.

Then, during it, go slower than everybody around you at the start. Everyone sets off too fast and the ones who finish comfortably are the ones who did not.

Decide now what happens afterward

The flat week after an event catches people out, and the way to handle it is to have one small thing already on the calendar.

A short outing three days later. Another event, further off. Nothing dramatic.

That is what turns one event into something you keep, which is the actual prize here rather than the medal.

When to get help

Please talk to your doctor before you start if you have a heart or lung condition, chest pain, a joint problem, diabetes, are pregnant, or have been inactive for a long stretch, because the answer is almost always yes with adjustments rather than no, and it is worth having that conversation rather than guessing. See a physical therapist if something hurts for more than a few days or keeps coming back in the same place, since one appointment early saves months. And if exercise has been difficult ground for you in the past, or you notice the training turning into something you punish yourself with, that is worth taking to a professional alongside the physical side.

If you are in crisis, call or text 988 (Suicide & Crisis Lifeline, US) or your local emergency number. Theraglee is a wellbeing tool, not a substitute for professional care.
