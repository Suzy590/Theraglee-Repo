Actually Listening to Someone

Most of us listen while drafting our reply. What changes when you give someone your attention properly, and a few specific habits that make it possible.

MindfulnessCommunication Visitor 922 words

They are telling you about their day and you are nodding. You are also thinking about what you will say back, whether your story is better, and what you need to do before seven.

You will remember roughly the shape of what they said. They will register, without being able to name it, that they were not quite met.

Listening is treated as a passive thing you are already doing. It is actually a specific, effortful activity, and most people almost never receive it.

What gets in the way

A handful of ordinary habits, none of them unkind.

Drafting: using the time they are speaking to assemble your reply. This is the big one, and it means you are hearing only enough to respond to.

Matching: waiting for a gap to say that happened to me too. It is meant as connection and it moves the subject to you.

Solving: deciding what they should do, usually before they have finished describing the problem.

And competing attention: a phone, a screen, a half-finished task. Divided attention is not a smaller version of attention; it is noticeably absent.

Three things that change it immediately

Stop doing the other thing. Put the phone down and out of reach, turn away from the screen, turn your body toward them. That alone is most of it.

Let there be a pause before you speak. One breath after they finish. It feels long and it is the single clearest signal that you were listening rather than queuing.

And do not use the pause to launch your own story. If you want to say something of your own, say it afterward and say it as an addition rather than a replacement.

Ask one more question than feels natural

Most conversations move on one question too early. The second question is where the real content usually lives.

What was that like. What was the worst part. What did you want to happen. How are you now, as opposed to then.

And the most underused question of all: do you want help with this, or do you want to be heard. Asking it directly saves an enormous number of small frustrations, because the answer is very often the second one and almost everybody assumes it is the first.

Say back what you heard

This sounds artificial written down and it is powerful in practice, as long as you keep it brief and in your own words.

So it sounds like the appointment went fine but the waiting was the hard part. That is twelve words and it does two things: it proves you were there, and it lets them correct you, which they often do, and the correction is usually the most important part of the whole conversation.

Keep it to one sentence. Repeating back at length turns into a technique being performed at someone.

Resist the urge to fix

When someone you love describes a problem, the pull to solve it is strong and it comes from caring.

But an unasked-for solution carries a quiet message: this is simple, and you have not thought about it properly. If they have been living inside the problem for weeks, they have thought about it.

So hold the advice until it is requested, and if it is burning a hole in you, ask: I have a thought about this, do you want it now or not. People say yes about half the time, and the half who say no would have resented it.

What to do when you cannot listen

Sometimes you have nothing left. Pretending to listen while depleted is worse than saying so.

Be honest and specific: I want to hear this properly and I have nothing in me right now, can we do it after dinner. Then actually do it at the time you named.

That is a far better outcome than twenty minutes of nodding, and it tells them the conversation matters enough to schedule.

With children, and with people who are upset

Children often need you to be quiet much longer than feels comfortable, and they tell you things sideways while doing something else. The best listening with a child usually happens in a car or over a task, not in a face-to-face conversation about feelings.

With someone who is distressed, drop the questions almost entirely. Short acknowledgments, long pauses, and nothing clever. That sounds really hard, and then silence, outperforms anything else available.

Notice what happens when you do it

Two things usually surprise people who try this for a week.

Conversations get shorter rather than longer. Someone who is actually heard tends to stop sooner, because the repetition in most talking is an attempt to be received.

And people start telling you more real things. Attention is rare enough that when you offer it reliably, you become the person others bring the true version to, which changes your relationships more than any amount of good advice.

When listening is harder than this

If you cannot hold attention on anyone for more than a minute, if your mind is too loud to follow what people say, or if being present with someone else's distress reliably overwhelms you, that is worth knowing rather than treating as a character flaw.

Exhaustion, anxiety, long-running low mood, and the different ways attention works for some people all show up exactly like poor listening. A doctor or a therapist can help work out which of those it is, and that tends to do far more than trying harder in the next conversation.
