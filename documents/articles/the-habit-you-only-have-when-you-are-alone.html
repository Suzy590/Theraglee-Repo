The Habit You Only Have When You Are Alone

Everybody has something they do only when nobody is there. Why the secrecy costs more than the habit, how to tell an ordinary one from a costly one, and how to change it without a campaign.

ShameHabits Visitor 816 words

There is something you do when the house is empty, or when the door is shut, or in the hour after everyone else has gone to bed. It would stop immediately if somebody walked in, and that is the part that tells you most about it.

It might be nothing much. Picking at something, eating standing up at the counter, talking to yourself, hours on the phone you would not admit to, a drink you do not mention, checking something over and over.

Why the hiding does more than the habit

For a great many people the behavior itself is minor and the secrecy around it is not.

Hiding takes maintenance. You arrange time, you listen for the door, you have an explanation ready. That running background job costs more than the thing you are hiding.

It gets its own weight. A small habit that nobody knows about becomes, in your head, evidence of something larger, simply because it has been kept in the dark for years.

And the concealment prevents the ordinary correction. Most habits shrink when they meet daylight, not because of shame but because they lose the specific conditions they need, which are usually privacy and time.

Then there is the loop. You do it, you feel bad, feeling bad is uncomfortable, and the thing that reliably takes the edge off discomfort is the habit. That loop is the engine, and it runs on the shame rather than on the pleasure.

Telling an ordinary one from a costly one

Most private habits are fine. A few are not, and the test is not how embarrassing it is.

Ask what it costs. Time you cannot spare, money you notice, sleep, skin, your ability to do the next day. Embarrassment is not a cost. Those are.

Ask whether it is growing. A habit that takes the same twenty minutes it took three years ago is different from one that has slowly expanded.

Ask what happens when you cannot do it. Mild irritation is ordinary. Genuine distress, or rearranging your plans to get the opportunity, says something else.

And ask whether it is replacing something. A private habit that has quietly taken the place of sleep, food, company or rest is doing more than it looks like.

Changing one without making it a campaign

The all-out approach tends to fail here specifically, because the shame it generates feeds the thing.

Change the conditions before the behavior. Most private habits need a particular combination of time, place and tiredness. Remove one of those and the habit gets markedly harder without any willpower involved.

Put something in the gap. Habits that get stopped without a replacement come back, because the gap is uncomfortable and the old thing is right there.

Aim at a reduction you would not bother announcing. Twenty minutes instead of an hour. Four nights instead of seven. Small targets survive bad weeks, and bad weeks are what end most attempts.

And drop the all-or-nothing frame, which is doing most of the damage. One slip does not undo the two weeks before it, and treating it as though it does is reliably what turns a slip into a return.

About telling someone

Not always necessary, and worth considering rather than dismissing.

Telling one person usually shrinks it immediately, and most people report that the reaction they got was a fraction of what they had braced for.

Pick somebody who will not make it a project. You are looking for an ordinary response, not a campaign manager.

And you do not have to tell anybody who lives with you, or anybody at all, for this to be workable. Privacy and secrecy are not the same thing, and the goal is to stop the second rather than give up the first.

Being realistic about what is left

Some private habits are simply yours and do not need fixing. Plenty of people have something small and harmless they would rather not explain, and that is not a problem to be solved.

The useful question is not whether you would be embarrassed, which is almost always yes, but whether it is taking something from you.

When it is worth bringing to someone

A private habit is worth taking to a professional when it is costing you money, sleep or health, when it has grown, when you have tried to stop and could not, or when the shame around it has become a large part of how you think about yourself.

That includes the ones that are hard to say out loud. Therapists hear these constantly and are considerably less interested in the content than you expect, because the work is the same whatever the behavior is: the loop, the conditions, and the shame that keeps it running. Your doctor is the right person to tell if there is a physical cost, and can point you toward what is available locally.
