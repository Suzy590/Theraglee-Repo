Cooking for Someone Who Cannot Eat Much

You make something and it comes back untouched, and it stings more than it should. Why feeding carries so much, what actually helps when appetite is small, and how to stop keeping score.

FoodCaregiving Visitor 805 words

You made the thing they used to like. It came back with two forkfuls gone, and you took the plate away and said it was fine, and then stood in the kitchen feeling something considerably larger than the occasion seemed to warrant.

Illness, treatment, age, grief, medication. There are many reasons somebody stops being able to eat much, and almost none of them have anything to do with your cooking.

Why feeding carries so much

Worth naming, because people in this position are often surprised by how much it hurts.

It is one of the few things you can still do. When most of what is happening is outside your control, cooking is concrete, and a refused plate is a refused piece of helping.

It reads as a verdict. Logically you know it is not. The part of you that cooked it does not entirely believe that.

It is also a running measurement. Every meal produces a number, and when the number keeps being low, there is nowhere for that to go except into you.

And feeding somebody is close to the oldest form of caring there is, which is why this lands somewhere deeper than a practical disappointment.

What actually helps when appetite is small

Most of the useful adjustments are the opposite of what instinct suggests.

Make the portion embarrassingly small. A large plate is discouraging before anybody has started, and a small one that gets finished is better for both of you than a big one that does not.

Offer food more often rather than in bigger amounts. Six small chances beat three proper meals when capacity is low.

Go for cold, bland and easy before anything rich. Smell is often the problem, and anything strongly scented can end the attempt before the first bite. Cold food smells less.

Keep things within reach that need no preparation. Yogurt, crackers, fruit, a drink with something in it. A lot of what gets eaten is eaten because it happened to be there at the one moment the appetite appeared.

And follow the window rather than the clock. Appetite in illness often arrives at odd hours and does not last. Meeting it when it comes matters more than when dinner is supposed to be.

Taking the pressure out of it

This is the part that changes the atmosphere, and the atmosphere is doing more than the recipe.

Put the food down and go. Watching is well meant and it is the single most appetite-killing thing in the room.

Stop asking how much they have had. They know, they are usually already uncomfortable about it, and the question makes the next meal harder.

Do not negotiate. Three more bites turns eating into a transaction with a loser, and it costs you more than it gains.

And let them refuse without a reaction. The fastest way to make somebody dread meals is to make each one an event with your disappointment at the end.

Looking after your own end of it

Because this grinds people down and it is rarely acknowledged.

Separate the cooking from the outcome. You can control what is offered, how often, and how pleasant it is to be near. You cannot control how much goes in, and holding yourself to that measure is a losing arrangement.

Find another way to give the thing you are trying to give. Sitting with them, a drink made properly, a cold washcloth, reading aloud. The impulse behind the cooking can go somewhere that does not get returned on a plate.

And say it out loud to somebody. Most people carrying this have never mentioned it, because it sounds trivial next to what the other person is going through, and it is not trivial.

Getting the practical help that exists

Ask the medical team directly rather than working it out alone. Appetite is a standard thing to raise, and there is often something specific available: a change in timing, a medication adjustment, supplement drinks, or a referral to a dietitian who does exactly this.

Ask about anything that makes eating physically hard. A sore mouth, swallowing trouble, nausea, constipation. These are treatable and they are frequently the actual obstacle.

When to get support for yourself

If the meals have become the thing you dread, if you are arguing about food, if you are exhausted in a way that sleep does not touch, or if you have started to feel that you are failing at the one job you took on, that is worth taking somewhere.

A therapist who works with caregivers can help, and so can a caregiver support group, which many people dismiss and then find is the most useful hour of their month. Your doctor is the right person to tell if this has started to affect your own health, and can point you toward both.

If you are in crisis, call or text 988 (Suicide & Crisis Lifeline, US) or your local emergency number. Theraglee is a wellbeing tool, not a substitute for professional care.
