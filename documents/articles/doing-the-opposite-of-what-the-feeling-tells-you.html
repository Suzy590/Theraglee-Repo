Doing the Opposite of What the Feeling Tells You

Sadness says stay in bed, fear says avoid, shame says hide. When the urge does not fit the situation, acting against it changes the feeling faster than arguing with it.

DBTEmotion Regulation Visitor 885 words

Every feeling arrives with an instruction attached. Fear says get away. Anger says push back. Sadness says withdraw and stop moving. Shame says hide and tell nobody.

Most of the time following the instruction is exactly right. Fear near a busy road is doing its job. But sometimes the feeling is real and its instruction no longer fits the situation, and following it makes the feeling louder rather than quieter.

The skill for those moments has a plain name: do the opposite. It comes from dialectical behavior therapy, a practical kind of therapy built around skills like this one, and it is one of the few things that reliably shifts a mood from the outside in.

First, check whether the urge fits

This matters, because the skill is not for every feeling. Ask one question: does what this feeling is telling me to do actually fit the facts in front of me?

If a smoke alarm is going off, fear fits and you should leave. If you feel sick with dread about a phone call you have made a hundred times, the fear is real but the instruction does not fit. If someone crossed a line, anger fits and might need a conversation. If you are furious at a slow website, the anger is real and the urge to escalate fits nothing.

Sadness after a loss fits, and it wants stillness; that is worth respecting for a while. Sadness on a gray Tuesday that tells you to cancel everything and lie down does not fit, and following it will reliably make the day worse.

When the urge fits, feel it and act on it sensibly. When it does not, this is where doing the opposite comes in.

What opposite action looks like

The point is to act against the urge in a specific, physical way, not to talk yourself into feeling differently.

Sadness says stay still, stay in, do nothing. The opposite is to get moving and get active, in small ways: stand up, get outside, do something that involves other people, do the task rather than think about it.

Fear says avoid, leave, do not try. The opposite is to approach, and to stay until the alarm quiets down. Go to the thing. Send the message. Stay in the room a little longer than the fear wants.

Anger says raise your voice and lean in. The opposite is to be gentle and step back: lower your volume, unclench your hands, be slightly kinder than you feel, leave the room and come back.

Shame says hide and make yourself small. The opposite, when you have not actually done harm, is to stay visible: keep your head up, keep your voice at normal volume, and tell one person you trust the thing you were going to keep secret.

All the way, not half

Half-measures do not work here, and this is where most people go wrong. Going to the party and standing by the door with your coat on is not approaching; it is avoiding with better scenery.

So commit to the posture as well as the action. If you are approaching something frightening, stand up straight, make eye contact, and let your hands be still. If you are countering sadness, walk at a normal pace rather than shuffling. Your body is part of how the feeling is maintained, so it is part of what changes it.

And stay long enough for something to shift. Feelings drop when they are allowed to peak and pass; leaving at the peak teaches you that the only relief is escape.

Expect it to feel wrong at first

Doing the opposite almost always feels false for the first few minutes. That is not a sign it is going badly. The feeling has not caught up yet.

It usually takes longer than you would like: not thirty seconds, but ten or twenty minutes of acting differently before the mood begins to follow. Most people quit at minute three and conclude it does not work.

So pick one thing and give it a real trial. Not the whole day, not a new personality. One walk, one message, one conversation you do not hide from.

Where it is not the right tool

This is not a way to override a feeling that is telling you something true. If you are exhausted, rest is not avoidance. If you feel unsafe around someone, that fear may fit exactly. If you did harm someone, the answer is repair, not standing tall and ignoring it.

It is also not a way to pretend. You are not acting cheerful. You are acting against one specific urge while letting the feeling be exactly as present as it is.

If almost every urge stops fitting

Doing the opposite is a good tool for a single feeling in a single afternoon. If nearly every day is a fight against an urge to withdraw, if avoidance has quietly taken over your week, or if shame is telling you to hide most of what you are, the pattern is bigger than one skill and much easier to work on with someone else in the room. A therapist, especially one trained in this kind of practical skills work, can help you sort which urges fit and which ones have been running your life unopposed.
