Moving Somewhere You Know No One

A new city, an empty calendar, and nobody to call. What the first six months usually feel like, and what actually builds a life somewhere new.

Life TransitionsLoneliness Visitor 917 words

The boxes are unpacked. The job has started. It is Saturday morning, and you realize there is nobody in this city you could text about getting a coffee.

You chose this. It may even have been the right choice. And it can still feel, in the second month, like you have made a terrible mistake.

Almost everyone who moves somewhere new goes through this. It is not a sign that the move was wrong or that you are bad at making friends.

What you actually lost

It is worth being precise about it, because people underestimate the second half of this list.

You lost your friends, which you expected. You also lost all the background familiarity: the route, the shop where they know you, the gym, the doctor, where to go when something breaks. And you lost the accidental contact that came with all of it, the dozens of small unplanned interactions a week that made you feel situated.

That second kind takes effort to rebuild and nobody warns you about it, which is why the tiredness of a new city surprises people. Everything is a decision for a while.

The shape of the first six months

For most people it goes roughly like this. The first few weeks are busy and strangely fine, because setting up a life is a project. Somewhere around weeks six to ten the project ends and the quiet arrives, and that is usually the worst stretch.

Then, if you keep putting yourself in rooms, things slowly start to repeat. You see the same faces. Somebody remembers your name. By around six months you have acquaintances, and somewhere past a year you have friends.

Knowing the timeline helps, because the low point is not a verdict, it is a stage. The month three version of you is not the one to ask whether the move was a mistake.

Repetition beats effort

Here is the single most useful thing about making friends somewhere new: closeness comes from running into the same people repeatedly, far more than from any individual good conversation.

Which means your best move is not to be interesting at parties. It is to join things that meet on a schedule, with the same people, and then keep turning up.

A class with a fixed term. A team, at any standard. A choir. A volunteer shift. A run club. A language exchange. A regular night at the same bar or library or community center. The subject matters much less than the fact that it repeats.

And then give it longer than feels natural. Most people quit a group at week three, which is one week before they would have started recognizing anyone.

Make the small, specific ask

At some point, acquaintance has to turn into something else, and that nearly always takes someone being slightly brave.

The ask that works is small and specific. A coffee after the class. A walk on Sunday. Coming along to the thing you were already going to. Not a dinner party, not a big plan.

Say yes to everything for the first few months, including things you would normally decline. Not forever, just while you are building the first layer. And expect to extend invitations more often than you receive them for a while, which is not a sign of being unwanted; it is a sign of being new.

Some will not go anywhere. That is the normal rate, not a failure.

Keep the old people, but not instead of this

Call your old friends. They are a comfort and you should not drop them.

But watch for the version where you spend every evening on the phone with another city and none of them in this one. Contact that keeps you going is good; contact that lets you avoid building anything here will quietly stretch the hard stretch out for years.

Visits help if they are two-way. If you go home every weekend, you never get a Saturday here, and Saturdays here are what eventually make it yours.

Build the boring infrastructure on purpose

A good deal of feeling settled is just knowing things.

Find your doctor, your dentist, a barber, the supermarket you like, the walk you do without thinking, somewhere to sit outside. Go to the same cafe until they recognize you.

None of that is friendship and all of it helps enormously. It turns a place where you happen to live into somewhere you know.

Be careful with the first-year story

Two unhelpful conclusions tend to arrive around month three. One is this city is cold and unfriendly. The other is I must be harder to like than I thought.

Both are the loneliness talking, and both are worth holding loosely. Some cities are genuinely harder to break into than others, but almost all of them take longer than people expect, and the number of people in your situation in any given city is enormous.

When it is more than the usual hard

Give it six months of actually trying before judging the move. But if you are not sleeping, if you have stopped going out at all, if the low mood has stopped lifting, or if you are drinking more to get through the evenings, that is worth taking seriously now rather than waiting it out. A doctor or a therapist is the right place to start, and therapy by video means you can often keep someone you already trust while you build the rest of a life somewhere new.
