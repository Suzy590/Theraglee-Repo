Letting Somebody Else Do It Their Way

They are doing it wrong, or they are doing it differently. How to tell which, what correcting a difference actually costs you, and how to hand over an outcome instead of a method.

PerfectionismCommunication Visitor 833 words

You asked them to do it. They are doing it the wrong way. The dishwasher is loaded like that, the spreadsheet has the columns in a different order, the email you would have written differently has already been sent.

And you have the choice everybody in this position has: say something, do it yourself next time, or let it be. The third one is the hardest and it is usually the right answer.

Separate wrong from different

Almost all of this collapses once you sort it properly, and most people never do.

Wrong means the outcome fails. The dish is not clean. The figure is incorrect. The customer was misinformed. That is worth addressing.

Different means the outcome is fine and the route was not yours. The dish is clean and the plates are in the wrong half. The spreadsheet works and you would have sorted it another way.

Say the test out loud: does this actually not work, or is it just not how I would do it. That one question resolves most of it, and the honest answer is usually the second.

Know what you are spending to be right

Correcting somebody over a difference rather than a fault has prices, and they are easy to miss because they arrive later.

They stop volunteering, because being corrected is unpleasant and doing nothing is safer. So you end up doing more of it, which is what you were trying to avoid.

They stop developing, because nobody learns under supervision that fine. Children especially, and new colleagues.

And it corrodes things. At home, a running commentary on how somebody does ordinary tasks is one of the most quietly damaging habits there is, and the person doing it rarely knows how it lands.

Hand over the outcome, not the method

The practical fix is in how you ask. Most of this trouble is created at the handover.

So specify what done looks like and leave the route alone. The kitchen clear by eight. The report with these three figures in it, by Thursday. The children fed and in bed.

Then stop. No step-by-step, no checking in halfway, no hovering.

Say what is actually fixed and why, where something genuinely is: this has to be in this format because the system rejects anything else. People accept a constraint with a reason. They bristle at a preference dressed as one.

Let the standard drop a little

This is the trade, and it is worth being honest about it.

Something done by somebody else will often be done less well than you would do it. That is not a sign the arrangement failed. It is the cost of not doing everything yourself, and it is almost always worth paying.

Decide in advance what level is acceptable rather than judging it against your own. Good enough and done by someone else beats perfect and done by you at eleven at night.

And accept the first few attempts being worse. That is what learning looks like, and stepping in during it is how people end up with help that never improves.

Choose your moment if you do say something

Where it genuinely matters, the timing and the amount matter more than the point.

Not mid-task, standing over them. Afterward, once, briefly, and about one thing rather than four.

Be concrete and avoid the always. Could the figures go in this order next time, because it feeds the other sheet. Not: you never do this properly.

And let them keep the task. Taking it back is the strongest message available and it ends the possibility of them getting better at it.

Notice what is underneath

If this is hard beyond reason, it is often not about the task.

Sometimes it is anxiety: if you do it yourself nothing can go wrong, and handing over means tolerating uncertainty for an afternoon.

Sometimes it is identity. Being the one who does it properly is a real position, and giving part of it away can feel like losing something, especially if being useful is how you have always earned your place.

And sometimes it is that the consequences genuinely do land on you, which is a legitimate thing to say out loud and negotiate rather than to manage silently.

When to get help

Please talk to a therapist if you cannot let other people do things without real distress, if this is causing repeated arguments at home, or if it has you doing far more than your share and resenting it, because the pattern underneath is usually anxiety or perfectionism and both respond well to treatment. See your doctor if the strain of carrying everything has your sleep or your mood down, since that is a real cost rather than a character trait. And if being the one who does everything properly is also how you keep yourself feeling worthwhile, that is worth taking to a professional, because it tends to go back a long way and it is heavy to carry alone.
