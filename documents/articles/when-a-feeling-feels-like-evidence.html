When a Feeling Feels Like Evidence

You feel like a fraud, so you must be one. How to separate the feeling from the claim hiding inside it, where this habit hides most, and why distrusting every feeling is not the answer.

CBTThinking Traps Visitor 818 words

You feel like a fraud at work, so you must be one. You feel unwanted at the party, so you are. You feel like it is going to go badly, so it will. The feeling arrives first, and then the conclusion arrives dressed as a fact.

This happens to everyone, and it is one of the most useful things to learn to catch, because the feeling is real and the conclusion is often wrong.

What is actually happening

Feelings are information about your state. They are not reports about the world, even though they arrive with the same confidence.

So anxiety tells you accurately that your body is braced. It does not tell you that the thing you are braced about is going to happen. Those are two different claims and only the first one is evidence.

The reason this is confusing is that emotions carry certainty with them. A frightened body does not say I notice I am frightened. It says something is wrong, and it says it with total conviction.

Once you can see the two parts separately, the whole thing becomes much easier to handle.

Separate the feeling from the claim

This is the practical move, and it takes about fifteen seconds.

Write it in two lines. The feeling: I feel stupid. The claim hiding inside it: I am stupid, and everyone in that meeting could see it.

Then treat only the second line as something to check. You are not arguing with the feeling, which is both real and allowed. You are examining a statement about reality that happened to arrive attached to it.

People find this freeing rather than cold, because it means you can be frightened without having to believe everything fear says.

Check the claim the way you would anyone else's

Once it is written as a statement, you can ask ordinary questions of it.

What is the actual evidence, as in things somebody could have filmed. What is the evidence against it. What would I say if a friend told me this about themselves. Has this feeling made this prediction before, and what happened.

That last one is the strongest. If dread has told you thirty times that a presentation will be a disaster and it has been fine twenty-eight times, you have a track record, and a track record beats a feeling.

Watch where it hides

Emotional reasoning is easiest to spot in obvious anxiety and hardest in the quiet places.

Guilt is the big one. Feeling guilty is taken as proof of having done wrong, which it is not. Plenty of people feel guilty for resting, for saying no, or for things that were not theirs.

Shame does the same. Feeling worthless is taken as evidence of being worthless, which is circular and still convincing.

So is flatness. Feeling that nothing matters is read as a discovery about life rather than a description of a mood, and that one is worth being especially careful with.

And anger. Feeling wronged is persuasive and is not the same as having been wronged, which is why a cooling-off period saves so many relationships.

Do not go too far the other way

The aim is not to distrust your feelings. That lands people in a worse place, second-guessing every instinct and ignoring real signals.

Feelings are often pointing at something true even when the conclusion is wrong. Dread before a meeting may be accurate about the meeting being difficult. Unease about a person is worth attending to rather than dismissing.

So treat a feeling as a flag, not a verdict. It says look here, which is useful. It does not say and here is what you will find.

Give it a week of practice

Pick one feeling that runs your conclusions most often. Probably anxiety, guilt or inadequacy.

Each time it shows up, write the two lines. Feeling, then claim. Do nothing else for the first few days.

Just seeing it written down, over and over, does most of the work, because the claim looks much less certain in your own handwriting than it sounded in your head.

When to get help

Please talk to a therapist if your feelings are reliably running your conclusions in a way that is shrinking your life, since this is one of the central things cognitive therapy works on and it responds well, usually in a matter of weeks rather than years. See your doctor if the feeling underneath has been a low or hopeless one for more than a couple of weeks, or if anxiety is there most days, because treating that makes everything on this page easier to do. And if what the feeling keeps telling you is that you are worthless or a burden, please do not try to argue with that alone. Take it to a professional, who will have heard it many times before and will not be surprised by it.
