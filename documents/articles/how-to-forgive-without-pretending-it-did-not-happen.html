How to Forgive Without Pretending It Did Not Happen

Forgiveness gets confused with excusing, forgetting, or letting someone back in. It is none of those. What it actually is, why it is for you and not for them, and how to get there in your own time.

RelationshipsEmotion Regulation Visitor 865 words

Someone hurt you. Maybe recently, maybe years ago. And somewhere along the way, someone has probably told you that you need to forgive them, in a tone that suggested you were failing at something by still being angry.

That advice is often well meant and badly explained. Forgiveness, as most people understand it, sounds like saying what happened was okay. It was not okay. So the advice gets refused, the anger stays, and it keeps costing you.

Here is a different way to think about it.

What forgiveness is not

It is not saying the hurt was acceptable. It is not forgetting. It is not pretending it did not happen or minimizing what it cost you. It is not reconciling, or letting the person back into your life, or trusting them again. It does not require them to apologize, or to know that you have forgiven them, or to still be alive. You can forgive someone and never speak to them again.

Once you take all of that off the table, what is left is much smaller and much more possible.

What it actually is

Forgiveness is deciding to stop carrying the debt. It is releasing your hold on the demand that the past be different, and on the hope that your anger will somehow make the other person pay. It is for you. The person who hurt you does not feel your resentment. You do, every time it comes up, and it comes up a lot.

Someone once described resentment as drinking poison and waiting for the other person to get sick. Forgiveness is putting the cup down. It changes nothing about what they did. It changes what you are doing to yourself now.

You do not have to be ready

There is no timeline. Some hurts can be released in an afternoon. Some take years, and some may never fully go. If you are not ready, you are not ready, and pushing yourself to forgive before the hurt has been fully felt tends to produce fake forgiveness, which is just resentment with a smile on it.

The steps below are for when you are ready to start, not a schedule.

Feel it first

You cannot release what you have not acknowledged. Before anything else, let yourself know exactly what happened and what it cost. Write it down if it helps: what they did, what it took from you, how it changed things. Be specific and be honest. This is not wallowing. It is taking your own injury seriously, which is the first act of respect toward yourself.

Separate the person from the act

Try, at some point, to see the whole person, not just the moment they hurt you. Not to excuse them. To be accurate. People who do harm are usually people who were themselves hurt, frightened, limited, or careless, and understanding that does not make what they did right. It makes it human, and human things are easier to set down than monstrous ones.

Decide what you are releasing

Forgiveness is a decision, and it helps to make it explicit. Say what you are letting go of. I am letting go of waiting for an apology that is not coming. I am letting go of needing them to understand what they did. I am letting go of the version of events where I get to be angry forever. Say it out loud, or write it, or tell someone you trust.

Expect it to come back

Forgiveness is rarely a single event. You put the cup down, and a week later you find it in your hand again, because something reminded you. That is not failure. Put it down again. Each time is a little easier, and the gaps get longer. Over time, the hurt becomes a thing that happened rather than a thing that is happening.

Decide about the relationship separately

Whether to let this person back into your life is a completely different question from whether to forgive them, and it deserves its own answer. You can forgive and keep your distance. You can forgive and set firm limits. You can forgive and rebuild, slowly, if they have shown real change. What you cannot do safely is use forgiveness as a reason to skip the question of whether they are safe to be near.

Forgiving yourself

Sometimes the person you most need to forgive is you. The same rules apply. Acknowledge exactly what you did and what it cost. See the whole person you were, not just the mistake. Decide to release the demand that the past be different. And know that self-forgiveness does not mean letting yourself off; it means letting yourself move, which is the only way you get to do better.

When you cannot get there

Some hurts are too big to release alone. Betrayal, abuse, a loss caused by someone's carelessness. If you have been carrying something for years and it still burns, or if the anger is shaping your relationships and your health, please talk to a therapist. Working through a deep injury with someone trained to help is not weakness. It is how people who have been badly hurt eventually get their lives back.
