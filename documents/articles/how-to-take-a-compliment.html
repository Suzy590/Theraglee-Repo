How to Take a Compliment

Someone says something kind about you and you deflect, joke, or hand it straight back. Why compliments are so hard to receive, and how to let one land without squirming.

Self-Esteem Visitor 798 words

Someone tells you the presentation was excellent. You say, oh, it was mostly the slides. Someone says you look great. You say, this old thing. Someone thanks you for your help. You say, it was nothing, anyone would have done it.

Each time, the kindness is offered and you bat it away before it can touch you. It feels like modesty. It is often something else, and it costs more than it seems.

Why it is so hard

A compliment is a small mismatch between how someone sees you and how you see yourself. If your inner picture is unflattering, praise does not fit it, and the mind tries to resolve the mismatch by rejecting the praise. It cannot be true, so they must be being polite, or they do not know the whole story, or they will be disappointed later.

There are other reasons too. Some people were raised to believe that accepting praise is arrogance. Some learned that being noticed was dangerous. Some are so used to being the one who gives that receiving feels like a role they do not know how to play. And for many, a compliment creates a debt: now I have to say something nice back, quickly, to even the score.

What deflecting does

When you refuse a compliment, three things happen. You rob yourself of something nourishing. You gently tell the other person that their judgment was wrong, which is a small insult dressed as humility. And you reinforce the unflattering inner picture, because you have just acted as if it were true.

Do that a few thousand times and you have trained yourself to be immune to good news about yourself, which is not a safe place to live.

The two-word answer

Here is the whole skill, at its simplest: say thank you, and stop.

That is it. Thank you. Not thank you but. Not thank you, though really. Just thank you, and then let the silence sit for a second. It will feel enormous. It is about one second long.

If you want to add something, add something that keeps the compliment rather than shrinking it. Thank you, I worked hard on that. Thank you, that means a lot coming from you. Thank you, I am really pleased with how it turned out.

Notice the urge to give it back

The reflex to immediately compliment the other person is strong. Resist it, at least for a moment. A compliment returned instantly reads as a deflection, and it usually is one. If you genuinely have something kind to say, say it later, in its own moment, where it can be received rather than traded.

Let it in

Taking a compliment well is not just about what you say. It is about what you allow inside. After you say thank you, try, privately, to believe it for ten seconds. Not forever. Just ten seconds of, maybe they are right. Maybe I did do that well.

This will feel strange and slightly fraudulent at first. That feeling is the old inner picture objecting. Let it object. Ten seconds of considering that something good about you might be true is how the picture starts, very slowly, to update.

Practice on small ones

Start with low-stakes compliments: nice shirt, good coffee, thanks for grabbing that. Say thank you and stop. Feel the pull to deflect, and do not follow it. When that gets easier, the bigger ones, about your work, your character, your worth to someone, become possible too.

Keep a record

Some people find it useful to write compliments down, in a note on the phone or a page in a journal. Not to gloat, but as evidence. On a day when the inner critic is loud, the record is a list of things other people have seen in you that the critic refuses to. It is surprisingly hard to argue with a list.

Giving one well, too

Once you can receive, you get better at giving. A good compliment is specific and about the person's effort or character, not just the outcome. You handled that difficult customer so calmly. I noticed how much care you put into that. Specific compliments are harder to deflect and land deeper, and giving them is one of the kindest small things you can do for someone.

When it goes deeper

If accepting kindness feels not just awkward but almost physically wrong, if praise makes you anxious or suspicious, or if you cannot think of a single good thing about yourself that you actually believe, that is worth exploring with a therapist. A deep difficulty receiving good things usually has a history, and understanding that history is the beginning of a kinder relationship with yourself. You deserve to be able to hear that you did well.
