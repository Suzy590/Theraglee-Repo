When Caregiving Ends

Four years built around somebody else, and then it stops. Why the role goes as well as the person, how relief and guilt arrive together, and what the first few empty weeks need.

CaregivingLoss Visitor 922 words

For four years your day was built around somebody else. The medications, the appointments, the two in the morning, the phone always on. And now it has stopped, because they died, or they moved into a home, or they recovered and no longer need you.

Everybody expects relief. Some of it is relief. The rest is a strange hollow week that nobody warned you about, and almost nobody talks about.

The role went as well as the person

This is the part that surprises people most, so it is worth naming first.

You lost a person, or a daily closeness. You also lost a job, an identity, a reason to get up, a structure that organized every hour, and often a whole set of people: the nurses, the pharmacist, the other family who called to check in.

That is several losses arriving at once, and the practical ones are what make the days so difficult to fill even when the grief itself has quiet patches.

Expect the relief and the guilt to arrive together

Almost everybody feels some relief, and almost everybody is ashamed of it.

So say it plainly to yourself. I am relieved that the hardest part is over. I loved them. Both of those are true.

Relief after a long illness is not a verdict on how much you cared. It is the normal response of a body that has been braced for years and can finally stop.

The guilt usually shows up in specifics: the time you were short with them, the day you wished it would end, the thing you did not do. Those are worth saying out loud to somebody rather than running privately for years, because said out loud they shrink to their real size.

The first few weeks are harder than people predict

The structure goes before the new life arrives, and the gap is where people struggle.

So put something on the calendar deliberately. Not a project. One thing a day with a time attached: a walk, a shop, a coffee with somebody, a swim.

Sleep will be strange. Many people wake at the hour they used to be needed, for months. That fades, and it is not a sign of anything wrong.

And your body may collapse a little. Years of being on alert tend to present their bill once the alert is over, which is why so many carers get ill in the first month afterward. Take that as predictable rather than alarming.

Do not rush to fill it, and do not leave it empty

Both extremes cause trouble.

Some people take on a new caring role, or a huge project, within weeks. Often that is avoidance, and it tends to end badly.

Others stay in the house with nothing, and the days close in.

The middle version is small and reversible. Try things without committing. A class for six weeks, not a qualification. Volunteering every other week, not twice a week. Something you used to like before all this, which may feel oddly unfamiliar at first.

Go looking for the person you were

If this went on for years, you may genuinely not know what you like anymore, and that is a common and disorienting thing to discover.

So treat it as a question rather than a failure. What did I used to read. Who have I not seen in three years. What did I stop doing. What have I always wanted to try.

Expect some of the answers to have changed, and expect to feel like a beginner for a while.

Let the practical things take their time

There is usually a pile: the equipment to return, the room to clear, the benefits to cancel, the paperwork.

Some of that has deadlines and some does not. Do what has a date on it, and let the rest wait until you can face it. Clearing a room is a different kind of task from clearing a cupboard, and nobody has to do it in the first month.

If the person is still alive in a home, this is its own particular version: the role has changed rather than ended, the guilt is sharper, and the visits are the new shape of it. Much of this still applies, and so does the part about looking after yourself.

Keep some of the people

The network built around the caring tends to evaporate, and that loneliness catches people out.

So keep one or two of them on purpose. Message the other relative. Stay in the carers' group for a while even though you are no longer one, since the people there understand this stretch better than anybody.

And tell your friends what you need, because they will not know whether to mention it. Most people are glad to be told either way.

When to get help

Please talk to your doctor if the flatness has lasted more than a couple of months, if you are not sleeping or eating, if you are drinking more, or if your own health has slid while you were looking after somebody else, which is extremely common and worth a proper appointment rather than a mention in passing. A grief counselor or a bereavement service helps with this particular kind of loss, and many carers' organizations offer support after the caring ends, not only during it, which people rarely realize. And if you have had thoughts of not wanting to be here, please tell someone today. In the US you can call or text 988 at any time.

If you are in crisis, call or text 988 (Suicide & Crisis Lifeline, US) or your local emergency number. Theraglee is a wellbeing tool, not a substitute for professional care.
