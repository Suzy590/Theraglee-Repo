Making Plans for After You Are Gone

The will, the passwords, the medical decisions: the list sits undone for years because every item asks you to picture yourself gone. Why that happens, what is actually on it, and how to get through it in pieces.

Life TransitionsWorry Visitor 916 words

There is a short list of things people are supposed to sort out, and most of us do not. A will. Who makes decisions if you cannot make them. Where the passwords are. What you want done at the end. The list is not long, and it sits undone for years.

The reason is not laziness. Every item on it asks you to picture yourself gone, and the mind will not hold that picture for long. So you open the drawer, feel something unpleasant, and close it again.

Why it is so easy to put off

Because it is not one task. It looks like one line on a to-do list and it is really six or seven separate errands, some of which need another person.

Because nobody teaches it. Most people have no idea what the documents are called, what they cost, or who you are supposed to ask, and a task you cannot picture the first step of does not get started.

Because superstition gets involved, even in people who do not believe in it. There is a quiet sense that writing it down invites it, which is not true but is extremely common and worth naming rather than arguing with.

And because of the age thing. The list reads as something for old people, so every year you can plausibly say not yet, and the year you cannot say it is usually the year something has already happened.

What tends to happen when people finish it

The part that surprises almost everyone is the relief.

The worry has somewhere to go. Open-ended dread is hard to carry. A folder with the answers in it converts a vague fear into a finished errand, and finished errands stop taking up room.

The people you love are spared the worst version. Families that have to guess at your wishes argue, and the arguments happen at the worst possible moment and last for years. A plain sentence from you ends arguments that would otherwise go on and on.

And the picture gets less frightening with use. The first hour is uncomfortable. The second is noticeably less so. Avoidance keeps a thing at full strength, and contact wears it down.

What is actually on the list

Shorter than it sounds, and most people's version fits on one page.

A will, which says who gets what and who is in charge of carrying it out. For simple situations this is not expensive, and plenty of people do it with a lawyer in a single appointment.

Someone named to make medical decisions if you cannot, and someone to handle money and paperwork. These are two separate jobs and they do not have to be the same person.

A note on what treatment you would and would not want at the end. This is the one people skip and the one families most need.

Where things are. The accounts, the policies, the deed, the password manager. A single sheet saying where to look is worth more than most of the documents.

What you want at the funeral, if you have a view. Burial or cremation, music, who you want told.

And who to call. A short list of names and numbers, because the people who have to make the calls often do not know each other.

Doing it in small pieces

The whole thing in one sitting is how it stays undone. Pick the smallest piece.

Write the where-things-are sheet first. It takes twenty minutes, needs nobody's help, and is the single most useful item on the list.

Then make one appointment. The appointment is the hard part, and it is also the part that puts the rest on a schedule that is not yours to keep postponing.

Give it a boring slot. A Tuesday evening, an hour, with something ordinary planned afterward. Doing this on a quiet Sunday with nothing after it is how people end up sitting in it.

And accept a rough version now over a perfect one later. A simple will is enormously better than an intention, and you can change it whenever you like.

Telling people

The documents do half the work. The conversation does the other half, and it goes better than people expect.

Keep it short and unceremonious. I have written it down, it is in this drawer, you are the one I named. Three sentences.

Do not spring it as a big announcement. People hear a dramatic opening and start looking for bad news.

And tell the person you named before they find out from a document. Being named is a job, and it is fair to let someone say that they would rather not do it.

If the worry does not stop afterward

For most people, finishing the list quiets things down. For some it does the opposite, and the paperwork becomes one more thing to keep checking and revising.

That is worth noticing. If you are rereading the documents, redoing them, or finding that thoughts about dying now arrive more often rather than less, the planning has stopped being practical and turned into something the worry is feeding on.

A therapist can help with that directly, and it is well-trodden ground. The same is true if the list has become impossible to look at, or if thinking about the end of your life has started to feel less like planning and more like something you want. Say that out loud to a doctor or a therapist rather than carrying it alone.
