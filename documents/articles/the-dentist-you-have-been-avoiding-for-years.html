The Dentist You Have Been Avoiding for Years

Eleven years away, and now the shame is part of the problem too. How to name the specific fear, what to say before you are in the chair, and the stop signal that changes everything.

PanicSelf-Care Visitor 875 words

You know the tooth needs looking at. You have known for a while. There is a dentist five minutes away and you have not been in eleven years, and the gap itself has become part of the problem, because now there is the state of your teeth to be ashamed of as well as the appointment to be frightened of.

This is extremely common. Dental fear keeps a great many people out of a chair for decades, and it is one of the most treatable fears there is, because the profession knows all about it.

Name the specific fear

Dental fear is usually not general. It is one or two particular things, and knowing which changes what you ask for.

The needle. The drill, or its sound. Gagging or not being able to breathe. Being unable to say stop. Not knowing what is happening. Pain itself. The loss of control of lying back with your mouth open while somebody works.

Or the shame, which for people who have been away a long time is often the biggest one, and the one they never mention.

Write down your two. The conversation afterward gets much easier.

Say it before you are in the chair

This is the thing that changes everything, and almost nobody does it.

Call, or email, and say it plainly: I am very anxious about dental treatment and I have not been for a long time. Can I come in and talk first, without any treatment.

Good practices do this constantly. They will book you a consultation where nothing happens except talking and perhaps a look. You keep your coat on. Nothing goes in your mouth unless you agree to it.

Ask directly whether they see nervous patients, and how. The answer tells you whether you are in the right place, and it is entirely reasonable to try a different practice if the answer is dismissive.

Agree a stop signal

A large part of the fear is being unable to stop what is happening, and the fix is simple and routine.

Agree a hand signal before anything starts. You raise your left hand, everything stops immediately, no questions. Ask them to confirm it.

Then use it early, on purpose, in the first few minutes of the first appointment, so you have proof it works. People who do that describe it as the moment the fear dropped by half.

Ask for the sequence out loud too: tell me what you are about to do before you do it. Unpredictability is most of what makes it unbearable.

Go in stages

You do not have to solve eleven years in one visit, and trying to is what makes people cancel.

A workable order: a talk with nothing done. Then a look and perhaps a photograph. Then a clean. Then the actual work, in short appointments, starting with the easiest thing.

Book the next one before you leave, while you are feeling brave. The gap is where the avoidance rebuilds.

Morning appointments tend to go better, because the dread does not have all day to grow.

Ask about what is available for the fear itself

There is more on offer than people assume, and asking is normal rather than a special request.

Numbing gel before the needle, so you do not feel it. Nitrous oxide, the gas that takes the edge off while you stay awake and in control. Sedation, where you are conscious but barely remember it afterward. Longer appointments at a slower pace. Headphones with your own music, which is particularly good for drill sounds.

Ask what the practice offers and what it costs, because the answer varies and some of it is cheap or free.

Put the shame down

The people who work there have seen it. All of it, many times, every week, and they are not shocked and do not think less of you.

What they actually want is for you to come in, and the longer you stay away the more they end up having to do. Arriving after eleven years is a good day for them, not an awkward one.

If it helps, say the sentence out loud at the start and get it over with: I know this is bad, I have been too frightened to come. Then it is said, and the rest of the appointment is about teeth.

When to get help

Please see a dentist sooner rather than at your own pace if you have pain that keeps you awake, a swelling in your face or gum, bleeding that does not stop, or a broken tooth, because those are urgent and treatable and they get worse while they wait. For the fear itself, ask your doctor or the practice about referral to a service that works with dental anxiety, since a few sessions with a professional alongside a patient dentist resolves this for most people. And if the dread of medical and dental appointments is keeping you from care in general, or if panic attacks are part of it, that is worth taking to a professional in its own right. If you have had thoughts of not wanting to be here, please tell someone today. In the US you can call or text 988 at any time.

If you are in crisis, call or text 988 (Suicide & Crisis Lifeline, US) or your local emergency number. Theraglee is a wellbeing tool, not a substitute for professional care.
