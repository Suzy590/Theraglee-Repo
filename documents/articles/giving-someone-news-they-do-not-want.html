Giving Someone News They Do Not Want

A decision has gone against them, or a job is ending, and you have to say so. Why softening the words causes the damage, and how to be clear, steady and genuinely kind instead.

CommunicationAnxiety Visitor 881 words

You have to tell somebody something they will not want to hear. A decision has gone against them, a job is ending, a plan is off, a diagnosis is what it is, an answer is no.

You have been rehearsing it for days, you have thought of nine ways to soften it, and all nine involve not quite saying it. That is the part worth fixing, because the softening is usually what causes the damage.

Say it in the first thirty seconds

The most common mistake is the long run-up. You start with context, you explain the process, you set out the reasons, and the person opposite you already knows and is now waiting in a state of dread while you talk.

So say it early and plainly. I have some difficult news. The position is being closed. Or: the scan showed something we need to talk about. Or: I have decided not to go ahead.

Then stop talking. The silence after is not a gap you need to fill. It is where they catch up.

Use clear words rather than kind-sounding ones

Soft language is not kindness. It forces someone to decode a sentence while in shock, and people routinely leave these conversations unsure what they were told.

Say ending, not transitioning. Say no, not probably not right now. Say died, not passed. If the answer is final, say that it is final, because a person who hears maybe will spend weeks on the maybe.

One clear sentence they can repeat to someone else that evening is the goal.

Do not borrow their reaction

You will want to apologize a great deal, explain how hard this was for you, or say you know exactly how they feel. All of that moves the conversation to you, and they then have to look after you.

One honest sentence is enough: I am sorry, and I wish I had better news. Then stay quiet and stay in the room.

If they get angry, let them. Anger in the first minute is almost never about you, even when it is pointed at you. Do not defend, do not match it, do not correct small inaccuracies. Just stay.

Let there be a reaction you did not expect. People laugh, go blank, ask about something trivial, or say thank you. None of that means it did not land.

Give the facts, then the next step

Once it has registered, people want two things: whether anything can change, and what happens now.

Answer the first honestly. If the decision is open to appeal, say how. If it is not, say so clearly rather than leaving a door ajar out of pity.

Then make the next step small and concrete. I will email you this today. We will meet again on Thursday. Here is who to call. Here is what happens to your pay.

Write it down and send it. Almost nobody remembers detail after the first sentence, and the reason they ask you the same question tomorrow is not that they were not listening.

Set it up so it can be survived

Where you do it changes how it goes.

In person if you can, by phone if you cannot, never by message for anything serious. Privately, with the door shut and your phone away. With time after it, so they are not turned out into a hallway thirty seconds later.

Think about what happens next for them. Who is outside. How they get home. Whether somebody should be with them. A conversation that ends with a person driving themselves home in shock is one you can plan better.

And give them a way to come back. Call me tomorrow if questions come up, and they will.

Handle your own part separately

Dreading this is not a character flaw. It is what it feels like to have power over an outcome somebody cares about, and the dread tends to produce exactly the behaviors that make it worse: delay, hints, over-explaining, and a promise you cannot keep.

So do not delay it to a better day, because there is not one, and the delay usually leaks. Do not hint beforehand to lessen the blow. Do not promise anything to make the moment easier.

Afterward, expect to feel bad even though you did it well. Tell someone, outside the situation and without the details, and give yourself the rest of the day at a lower setting.

If this is a regular part of your job, that accumulates. It is a real weight and it deserves acknowledging rather than absorbing.

When to get help

Please talk to a therapist if the dread of conversations like this is affecting your sleep or keeping you from doing your job, or if delivering bad news is a frequent part of your work and it has started to flatten you. If the news you are carrying is also your own loss, a diagnosis in the family or the end of something you wanted, you are doing two hard things at once, and talking to a professional about the second one is not an overreaction. Where you are supporting someone through what you told them, your doctor or a local service can point you both toward practical help rather than leaving it all with you.

If you are in crisis, call or text 988 (Suicide & Crisis Lifeline, US) or your local emergency number. Theraglee is a wellbeing tool, not a substitute for professional care.
