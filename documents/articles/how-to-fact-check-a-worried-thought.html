How to Fact-Check a Worried Thought

A worried thought arrives sounding like the truth. It usually is not. A simple, five-question way to check a thought before you let it run your afternoon.

CBTWorry Visitor 829 words

A thought shows up. They did not reply because they are annoyed with me. This headache is something serious. I am going to mess up the presentation and everyone will know. It arrives fast, sounds certain, and brings a rush of feeling with it. Within a minute, you are living inside it.

Here is the most useful thing to know about worried thoughts. They are guesses. They feel like reports from reality, but they are predictions made by a mind that is trying to keep you safe by assuming the worst. Guesses can be checked. That is what this article is about.

Catch it first

You cannot check a thought you have not noticed. Most worried thoughts run in the background, felt as a mood rather than heard as a sentence. So the first step is to catch the sentence. When you notice a wave of dread or tension, ask: what did I just tell myself?

Write it down if you can, in the exact words. I am going to get fired. She thinks I am boring. Seeing it on paper already changes it a little. It becomes a thing you are looking at, rather than the water you are swimming in.

Then ask five questions

You do not need all five every time. Even one is often enough to loosen the grip.

What is the actual evidence? Not the feeling, the facts. What do I know for certain? What am I filling in? Usually the known facts are small, such as a message went unanswered for an hour, and the story built on top of them is large.

What else could explain this? Come up with at least three alternatives, even silly ones. She is in a meeting. Her phone died. She read it and meant to reply later. The point is not to find the right one. It is to show the mind that its first guess is one of many.

What would I say to a friend? If someone you cared about told you this exact thought, would you agree with it? Probably not. You would say something fairer and kinder, and that fairer version is usually closer to the truth.

What is the worst case, and could I handle it? Worry lives on vague catastrophe. Make it specific. If the worst really happened, what would it actually look like, and what would you do? Most worst cases, spelled out, turn out to be survivable, and the mind relaxes a notch once it knows there is a plan.

Is this thought helping me right now? Some thoughts are true and still not worth holding. Even if the presentation might go badly, does rehearsing that make it go better? If not, you are allowed to set the thought down and put your attention on what you can do.

Write a fairer thought

After the questions, write a second sentence, the balanced version. Not a fake-cheerful one. A fair one. She has not replied yet, and I do not know why. I am nervous about the presentation, and I have prepared, and nervous is not the same as failing.

The fair thought will not feel as vivid as the worried one. It rarely does. But notice what happens to the feeling. It usually drops from a nine to a five, and a five is a feeling you can act inside.

Do it on paper at first

This feels clunky at the start, and it is worth doing anyway, with an actual pen or a note on your phone. The worried mind is fast and practiced. The checking mind is slow and new. Paper gives the slow one a chance. After a few weeks, you will find the questions starting to run on their own, in the moment, and the paper becomes optional.

What this is not

Fact-checking a thought is not arguing with yourself, and it is not positive thinking. You are not trying to force a happy conclusion. Sometimes the check comes back and says, yes, this is a real problem, and then the next step is a plan, not a pep talk. The goal is accuracy, because accurate thoughts, even uncomfortable ones, are easier to live with than inflated ones.

It is also not a way to make worry vanish forever. Worried thoughts will keep arriving. What changes is how much authority they have when they do.

When the worry is bigger than the tool

If you find that no amount of checking touches the worry, if the thoughts loop for hours or wake you at night, if you have started avoiding things to keep the worry quiet, that is when an article stops being enough. This same method, practiced with a therapist who can help you apply it to your specific patterns, is one of the most effective things there is for anxious thinking. Please reach out to a professional if the worry has taken more of your life than you want to give it.
