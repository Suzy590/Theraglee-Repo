When You Set a Goal You Are Afraid to Want

Some things you want badly enough that you will not say them out loud. Why wanting feels risky, what the hedging costs you, and how to go after something without betting everything on it.

GoalsSelf-Esteem Visitor 798 words

There is something you would like. Not a small thing. And you have never said it in a full sentence to anybody, including yourself, because saying it makes it real and real things can be lost.

So it stays in the half-light, worked toward occasionally and never named, and you tell people you are keeping your options open.

Why wanting feels risky

This is not timidity, and it is worth seeing the logic in it, because there is some.

An unnamed want cannot fail. If you never said you were trying, nothing happened when it did not work. That is genuine protection and it is why the arrangement persists.

Hope has a cost that disappointment makes you pay later. Anybody who has wanted something badly and not got it has learned that the wanting hurt as much as the losing, and the lesson sticks.

Being seen to want something is its own exposure. It tells people what you think you might be capable of, and that feels like a claim you could be caught out making.

And sometimes it belongs to somebody else. A want that would disappoint a parent, or not fit the person your friends know, comes with a second problem attached to it.

What the hedging costs

The protection is real. So is the bill, and it mostly arrives quietly.

Half-effort reads as evidence. When you never fully try, the result is always ambiguous, and ambiguity gets interpreted as you not being up to it. You end up with the conclusion you were avoiding, without the attempt.

You cannot get help with something nobody knows about. Most of what moves a goal along comes from other people, and they can only offer it if they know what you are doing.

Vague goals cannot be worked on. Something in its own words, with a next step, is a project. A feeling you will not name is just a weight.

And it takes up room anyway. An unspoken want is not quiet. It runs in the background for years and costs more than the stated version would.

Wanting it without betting everything on it

The useful move is not to want it less. It is to stop making it a verdict on you.

Say it once, out loud, to one person who is safe. That is the whole exercise and it does more than anything else here. People consistently report that the sentence is far less dangerous out of their mouth than in their head.

Separate the goal from what it would prove. I would like to do this is workable. If I cannot do this then I am nothing is not a goal, it is a bet, and it is the bet that makes it unbearable.

Name what you can actually control, which is the work and the attempts, not the outcome. Nearly every large want depends partly on luck and other people, and holding yourself to the part you do not control is the arrangement that breaks people.

Write down what counts as it having been worth doing even if the main thing does not happen. Skills, people met, work made, finding out. This is not a consolation prize. It is the part you are guaranteed to keep.

And make the next step small enough to actually do this week. Big wants stall on big first steps.

If it does not work out

Worth thinking about in advance, calmly, because the fear of this is what is driving the whole thing.

Not getting something you tried for is a specific grief, and it is a real one. It passes at the speed grief passes and not faster, and it is survivable in a way the imagined version never looks like it would be.

The people who try and miss are reliably glad they tried. The regret that lasts belongs almost entirely to the version where it was never attempted.

And a want can change honorably. Discovering you no longer want it is a result, not a failure, and it only becomes available once you have gone far enough in to find out.

When it is heavier than this

Some of this is ordinary caution and some of it is older.

It is worth talking to someone if you cannot name a single thing you want, if wanting anything brings on a strong sense of shame, if the fear is tied to somebody who made ambition unsafe for you, or if the stuckness has lasted years and nothing in it has moved.

That is ground a therapist works on directly, and it is usually less about goal setting than about what wanting came to mean along the way. Your doctor can point you toward what is available locally if you are not sure where to start.
