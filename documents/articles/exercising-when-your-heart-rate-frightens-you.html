Exercising When Your Heart Rate Frightens You

If a fast heartbeat sets off alarm, exercise becomes something to avoid. Why that happens, and how to get moving again in a way that teaches your body the opposite.

PanicMovement Visitor 984 words

You walk up the hill, your heart picks up, and something in you reads it as the start of something. By the top you are checking your pulse, and the next day you take the flat route.

This is extremely common and almost never discussed. If you have had panic attacks, or a frightening physical episode, the ordinary sensations of exertion stop being neutral. A fast heartbeat is a fast heartbeat whether you are running or panicking, and your alarm system cannot always tell which one it is looking at.

Why exercise becomes the thing you avoid

Panic teaches a specific lesson: certain body sensations mean something terrible is happening.

Exercise produces exactly those sensations on purpose. Racing heart, breathlessness, sweating, a hot face, sometimes light-headedness or a shaky feeling afterward.

So you climb some stairs, the sensations arrive, your mind asks is this panic or is this a problem, and the asking adds adrenaline, which adds more sensation. People end up with a panic attack that started as a brisk walk, and then avoid the walk.

Avoiding it also means losing fitness, which means the same hill produces more symptoms next time, which confirms the fear. That loop is the real problem, and it is fixable.

First, get it checked once

Before you treat this as anxiety, see a doctor and say exactly what happens, especially if it is new, if you have risk factors, or if the sensations come on with exertion in a consistent way.

Chest pain brought on by effort and relieved by rest, fainting during exercise, a very irregular heartbeat, or breathlessness far beyond what the effort explains all deserve medical assessment rather than reassurance from an article.

Getting that done matters for two reasons. The obvious one, and the fact that mid-exercise reassurance works badly when a real question is still open.

The counterintuitive part: the sensations are the treatment

Once you have been cleared, the useful approach is the opposite of avoidance.

Deliberately producing the sensations in a safe setting, repeatedly, is how the alarm learns they are not dangerous. Clinicians sometimes do this formally; you can do a gentler version of it yourself, and movement is the most natural version there is.

The aim is not to be calm while your heart races. The aim is to feel your heart race, stay with it, and have nothing bad happen, over and over, until the sensation stops meaning what it currently means.

How to build it up

Start low enough that it is comfortable, then raise it deliberately rather than avoiding the symptoms.

Week one: walk for twenty minutes at a pace where you notice your heart but can talk easily. Do that until it is boring.

Then add something that clearly raises your heart rate for a short period: one hill, one flight of stairs, thirty seconds faster, two minutes on a bike. Short on purpose, so the rise happens and then passes.

When the surge arrives, do not stop immediately. Keep going at a gentler pace and let it come down while you are still moving. Stopping dead at the first sensation teaches escape; continuing teaches that it settles.

Then repeat the same thing for a week before adding more. Boredom is the sign of success here.

What to do with the frightening moment

Three things, and they are all small.

Keep breathing out slowly rather than gulping air. Over-breathing is what produces the tingling and the unreal feeling that frighten people most.

Say something accurate rather than reassuring: this is exertion, my heart is doing what it is supposed to do, it will come down in a few minutes. Flat and factual beats positive.

And resist the checking. Taking your pulse, timing it, watching a monitor: all of it narrows your attention onto the sensation and amplifies it. If you wear a tracker and find yourself staring at the number, take it off for a few weeks.

Pick activities that help the learning

Anything rhythmic and controllable, where you can change pace easily: walking, cycling, swimming, rowing, dancing in the kitchen.

Early on, familiar places and company help. Later, deliberately do some of it alone and some further from home, because the fear often attaches to being far from help, not to the exercise itself.

Avoid two things at the start: the fasted early-morning session, which produces more light-headedness, and exercising after a bad night or a lot of caffeine, both of which raise your resting arousal and make everything feel worse.

Normal versus not

It helps to know what ordinary exertion feels like, because many people in this loop have forgotten.

Normal: heart beating hard, breathing fast, warm, legs heavy, a flushed face, a shaky feeling after something hard, taking a few minutes to come down.

Worth stopping for and taking to a doctor: chest pressure, pain radiating to the arm or jaw, fainting or near-fainting, a heartbeat that stays very high long after you stop, or breathlessness wildly out of proportion to the effort.

Knowing the second list makes the first list much easier to sit with.

When to get help with it

If panic is happening regularly, if you have stopped moving much at all, or if you have been avoiding exercise for months because of what it does to your body, this responds very well to treatment.

Cognitive behavioral therapy for panic does exactly this work in a structured way, usually in a small number of sessions, and a therapist can take you through the sensations faster and more thoroughly than you will manage alone. A doctor is the right first stop for the physical check and can refer you.

And if things have become dark enough that you are not sure you want to be here, please tell a doctor or someone you trust that same day. In the US you can call or text 988 at any time.

If you are in crisis, call or text 988 (Suicide & Crisis Lifeline, US) or your local emergency number. Theraglee is a wellbeing tool, not a substitute for professional care.
