Deciding Whether to Stay or Go

A job, a city, a relationship: some decisions sit unresolved for years. A few questions that move a stay-or-go decision forward when pros and cons have stopped helping.

Decision MakingValues Visitor 927 words

You have had this conversation with yourself so many times that you know your own arguments by heart. Some evenings you are certain you are leaving. By the morning you are not.

Stay-or-go decisions are their own category. A job, a city, a relationship, a course, a house. They are hard not because you lack information but because both options cost something real, and no amount of thinking makes that untrue.

What follows is not a way to be certain. It is a set of questions that tend to move a stuck decision further than another round of pros and cons.

The list has stopped working for a reason

Pros and cons treat a decision as arithmetic. Stay-or-go decisions are rarely arithmetic, because the items are not the same kind of thing and you are the one assigning the weights.

You can feel this happening. You write the list, the list says leave, and you do not leave. That is not weakness or confusion. It is a sign that something not on the list is carrying most of the weight, and that is the thing worth finding.

So instead of another list, try questions that get underneath it.

Is this the situation, or is it me right now

The same job feels different when you are sleeping badly, grieving, burned out, or three weeks into something hard at home.

Ask honestly whether you are judging the situation from a normal week or from your worst one. If you have been running on empty for months, almost everything looks intolerable, and a decision made from that place often gets remade a year later.

This is not a reason to do nothing. It is a reason to fix what can be fixed first, if anything can, and to notice whether the answer changes when you are rested.

What have I actually tried

Many stay-or-go decisions have a third option nobody has tested: stay and change something specific.

Have you asked for the different role, the reduced hours, the conversation you have been avoiding, the arrangement that would make it workable? Often the honest answer is no, because asking feels risky and the fantasy of leaving feels safer.

Trying the specific thing gives you real information either way. If it works, you have your answer. If the answer is no, your decision just got much clearer, and you will not spend years wondering.

Which version of this do I keep imagining

Notice where your mind goes when it wanders. Do you picture yourself still here in two years, or gone? Which picture brings relief and which brings dread?

This is not proof, and it should not be the only input. But the imagination is quite good at revealing a preference you have already formed and have not admitted, especially about the option you keep describing as impossible.

The reverse question is useful too. If someone told you the choice had been made for you, which decision would you be secretly relieved by?

Am I staying for a reason or out of a cost already paid

Be careful with sentences that start with everything I have put into this. Years already spent are gone whichever option you pick; they can be a reason to grieve, but they are a poor reason to choose.

The same goes for staying because leaving would mean admitting you were wrong, or because of what people would think. Those are real pressures, and they belong in the open rather than dressed up as practical arguments.

A useful test: if you arrived at this situation fresh today, knowing what you know, would you choose it? If not, what is actually keeping you is worth naming precisely.

What would I be moving toward

Leaving to escape something is very different from leaving toward something. Both can be right, but escape tends to produce a decision you repeat, because the thing you were escaping often travels with you.

So ask what you want more of, not just what you want less of. Then ask whether the thing you want is available where you are. Sometimes it plainly is not, and that is the clearest answer this whole process can give you.

Make the decision revocable where you can

Not everything has to be settled at once. Many stay-or-go decisions can be turned into an experiment: a leave of absence, a trial arrangement, a fixed period with a date to review, a conversation with one honest question in it.

Give it a deadline and a specific thing you are watching for, or the trial quietly becomes another year of the same.

And set a real date for the decision itself. Unmade decisions cost more than most people think: they take up the space you would otherwise use to live in the situation or leave it.

If you cannot get near it

Sometimes the decision is not stuck because it is hard. It is stuck because looking at it straight on brings up something heavier: fear of being alone, guilt, a sense that you do not get to want things, or a relationship where the real question is whether you are safe.

That is not a thinking problem and no list will touch it. A therapist can be genuinely useful here, not to tell you what to do, but to help you see what you are afraid of finding out. And if what is keeping you is fear of what someone else will do if you leave, please talk to a professional who works with that specifically before making any move.
