When You Start Feeling Better and Do Not Trust It

A good week arrives and instead of relief there is a flinch. Why the lift gets doubted, what the waiting costs you, and how to use a good stretch rather than brace your way through it.

DepressionThinking Traps Visitor 878 words

Something has lifted. You got up without the usual argument, you noticed the weather, you laughed at something. And instead of relief there is a flinch, because you have been here before and you know what came next last time.

So you hold still. You do not say anything to anyone. And you spend the good week waiting for it to end, which is a strange way to spend a good week.

Why the recovery gets doubted

This is common enough to be nearly universal, and almost nobody mentions it, which makes people think it is their own peculiar fault.

You have evidence. If it has lifted before and come back, then caution is a reasonable response to your own history rather than pessimism.

Good stretches have felt like this before. A real recovery and a temporary lift feel identical from the inside on day four, so there is nothing to check against.

Saying it out loud feels like tempting fate. People hold off telling anyone they are better because announcing it seems to guarantee the fall, which is superstition and extremely widespread.

And there is the drop itself, which is genuinely worse after a good patch. A low week after a low month is ordinary. A low week after two good ones has a sharper edge, and the mind remembers that.

What the waiting costs

The doubt is not neutral. It takes most of what the good stretch had to offer.

You cannot use it. Better weeks are when people rebuild the things that help, see friends, start moving again, fix something at work. Spent on watching for a relapse, the week passes and nothing gets rebuilt.

You check constantly. Am I still all right, was that a dip, was that thought the beginning. Monitoring a mood reliably makes it worse, in the same way that watching for sleep keeps you awake.

And you keep the support at arm's length, because you have not told anybody, which means if it does come back you are starting from further away.

How recovery actually looks

Worth knowing, because people measure a recovery against a picture that nothing ever matches.

It is not a line. It goes up and comes partway down and goes up further, and the dips inside a recovery are not the thing returning. Expecting the dips removes most of their power.

It is usually uneven across the parts. Sleep improves before mood, or energy comes back before interest does, or you can work for weeks before you can enjoy anything. Being better at some things and not others is the normal shape.

And it is often noticed in hindsight rather than at the time. Most people realize they have been better for a while rather than feeling it begin.

What to do with a good stretch

The useful stance is neither celebrating nor bracing. It is using it.

Do the unglamorous rebuilding now, while it is possible. Make the appointments, answer the messages, get food into the house, restart the one bit of movement. This is the work a good week is for.

Tell at least one person. Yes, it feels like tempting fate. It also means somebody knows where you actually are, which matters a great deal in both directions.

Write down what has been different, plainly. The medication, the sleep, the hours, the person you started talking to. In a low stretch nobody can remember what helped, and a list written now is worth more than any amount of trying to recall later.

And make a short plan for a dip while you are well enough to think clearly. Who to call, what to stop doing, what you have agreed with your doctor. Having it written down takes some of the fear out of a dip arriving.

Loosening the monitoring

Checking is the habit to break, and it responds to the same approach as any other checking.

Put the question on a schedule rather than answering it all day. Once a week is plenty, and a short note works better than a running assessment.

Notice that bad days have not stopped existing. An ordinary bad day inside a recovery is still an ordinary bad day, and reading every one as evidence is the thinking trap doing the work.

And stop comparing today with the best day of the good stretch. That comparison makes every normal day look like a decline.

Talking to someone about it

This is worth raising with whoever is treating you, and it is useful information rather than a complaint.

If you are taking medication, say that you are feeling better rather than stopping, because this is the point at which many people quietly stop, and it often does not go well.

If you are in therapy, the fear of relapse is a good subject in itself, and there is specific work for it that is practical rather than reassuring.

And if the fear is the dominant feeling, if you find yourself unable to enjoy a good stretch at all, or if a dip arrives and brings thoughts of not wanting to be here, tell your doctor or your therapist right away rather than waiting for the next appointment.
