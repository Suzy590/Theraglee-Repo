Deciding You Already Know How It Will Go

The interview will go badly, they will say no, it will not work out. How a confident prediction about the future quietly stops you trying, and how to test one instead of believing it.

Thinking TrapsMotivation Visitor 823 words

You do not apply, because they will pick someone else. You do not ask, because they will say no. You do not mention the idea in the meeting, because it will get shot down.

None of that feels like avoidance. It feels like being realistic, which is why it is so effective at keeping your life smaller than it needs to be.

The prediction arrives as a fact

The reason this one is hard to catch is that it does not announce itself as a guess.

It arrives in the same flat, certain tone as a memory: that will not work. Nobody will come. She will be annoyed. And because it feels like knowledge, you do not examine it, you plan around it.

A tired or low mood makes the predictions bleaker, and a frightened one makes them more specific. Neither adds any actual information about Thursday.

Where it costs you

The damage is rarely the thought itself; it is the thing you quietly did not do.

The job you did not apply for, the invitation you did not send, the price you did not ask for, the apology you did not offer, the appointment you did not make. Each one is a small decision that felt sensible and removed the only route to a different answer.

And because you never ran the test, the prediction is never contradicted, so it stands. That is the real trap: avoidance protects the belief that made you avoid.

Write the prediction down properly

Vague gloom cannot be checked, so make it specific enough to be wrong.

Not it will go badly, but: they will interrupt me in the first two minutes, I will have no answer, and they will say no by Friday. Not nobody cares, but: I will invite six people and fewer than two will reply.

Give it a number if you can, and a date. The act of writing it this plainly often takes some of the certainty out on its own, because stated flatly, most predictions sound more like a fear than a forecast.

Then keep the paper, which is the part people skip.

Run the smallest possible test

You do not need to bet the whole thing. You need one piece of evidence.

Send the message to one person instead of six. Ask the question to the colleague you find easiest. Apply for the version of the job you are not attached to. Raise the idea at a small meeting rather than the big one.

Then compare what happened to what you wrote, in plain terms, without editing it afterward into a near miss.

What usually comes back is a messy, partial result: they did not say no, but they did not say yes; two people replied and one was keen; the question was fine and the answer was unhelpful. Reality is almost never as clean as the prediction, and that in itself is the lesson.

Count the times you were wrong

People are good at remembering the predictions that came true and losing the rest, which keeps the habit well fed.

So keep the running list for a few weeks. Prediction, what actually happened, one line each. Most people find that a surprising proportion of theirs were either wrong or roughly half-right, and that the ones that came true were rarely as bad as the forecast.

Notice too what you do with a good outcome. If the explanation is always that you got lucky, or they were being kind, or it does not count, then the evidence is being thrown away before it can change anything.

Separate the prediction from the plan

Being realistic has a real job to do, and it looks different from this.

A plan says: this might not work, so here is what I will do if it does not. It stays in contact with the actual odds and it leaves the attempt intact.

A prediction says: it will not work, so there is no point starting. It feels like the same caution and it removes the attempt.

The test is whether the thought leads to a precaution or to a cancellation. Precautions are useful. Cancellations are the thing to be suspicious of.

When it is more than a habit of thought

If this has settled into most areas of your life, if the certainty is so heavy that trying anything feels pointless, or if the predictions are mostly about yourself rather than the situation, that is worth working through with someone.

Please talk to a doctor or a therapist if a flat, hopeless certainty about how things will go has been with you for weeks, or if it has stopped you doing things you care about. This is one of the most well-mapped areas there is: a counselor will work through exactly the steps above with you, and having someone keep you honest about the tests makes a large difference to how fast it shifts.
