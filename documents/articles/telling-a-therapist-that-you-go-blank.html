Telling a Therapist That You Go Blank

Describing dissociation out loud is hard, and the words sound alarming. How to explain the far-away feeling in plain language, what a good professional does with it, and what to do when it happens in the room.

CommunicationTrauma Visitor 918 words

You have decided to mention it. And now you are trying to find words for something that resists them, and every sentence you try sounds either trivial or frightening. I go somewhere else. I am not really there. I lose time. Sometimes my hands do not look like mine.

So people say nothing, or say the smallest possible version, and then feel unseen in a room they went to specifically to be seen. It is worth knowing that this is one of the more common things a therapist hears, that nobody who does this work will be shocked by it, and that you do not need the right technical word for any of it.

Describe it in ordinary language

You do not have to know the term. Plain description is more useful to a professional than any label, because the details are what shape the help.

Try the version you would use with a friend you trusted completely. Sometimes in the middle of a conversation the room goes flat and I feel like I am watching from a few feet away. Or: I lose stretches of time and cannot say what I did. Or: I go numb when my partner raises his voice and afterward I cannot remember what was said.

If describing it live is too hard, write it down beforehand and read it out, or hand over the paper. That is a completely legitimate way to start, and many people do it.

Bring the details that matter

A few plain facts turn a vague account into something workable.

How long it lasts: seconds, minutes, hours. How often: daily, a few times a week, twice a year. When it started, and whether anything was happening in your life around then. What is usually going on just before: tiredness, conflict, a particular person, a certain place, nothing you can see. What it costs you: driving, work, parenting, relationships. What you have found that helps, even slightly.

You do not need all of this at the first appointment. Two lines in a note on your phone, written during the week, is already more than most people arrive with.

Expect questions, not alarm

A good professional will ask practical things, including some about safety, and about whether you have lost time, found yourself somewhere unexpectedly, or heard things others did not. Those questions are routine. They are how someone works out which of several ordinary explanations fits, so that the help matches the problem. They are not a search for evidence that something is deeply wrong with you.

Expect to be sent to a doctor too, if you have not been. Sleep problems, migraine, thyroid trouble, some medications, and certain seizure types can produce exactly these experiences, and they are worth ruling out before anything else.

Say if it happens in the room

It often does, especially once you start talking about hard things, and this is useful rather than a setback. It gives you both something to work with directly.

Agree in advance on what you will do, so you do not have to find words from inside the fog. A hand raised. The word pause. Looking away. Agree what the therapist does then: stop asking questions, change the subject to something practical, open a window, help you orient to the room, sit in silence for a minute.

When you come back, say what you can. I went off for a bit, I am coming back, give me a moment. A therapist who works with this will slow the pace and may suggest keeping one foot in the present while you talk: eyes open, feet on the floor, cold water nearby, talking about the hard thing at a lower resolution rather than all of it at once.

Go at the pace that keeps you present

The most common mistake in early sessions, and plenty of people make it, is telling the whole heavy story in one go because you finally have permission to. If that takes you out of your body for three days afterward, it has cost more than it gave.

You are allowed to say I want to talk about this, and I want to go slowly. You are allowed to say I would rather talk about how it affects me now before I talk about what happened. A good professional will be glad you said it; staying present matters more than covering ground.

Ask what the plan is

It is fair to ask directly: what do you think is happening, what are we going to do about it, and what should I do when it happens this week. The answers tend to be practical and reassuring. If you leave several sessions in a row with no sense of a plan, or if you feel pushed faster than you can stay present for, it is reasonable to say so, and reasonable to look for someone else. Fit matters in this work.

When to get help

If you have been carrying this privately, please take it to your doctor and to a therapist who works with trauma and dissociation. Ask whether they do; most will tell you straightforwardly, and a referral is normal. If any of this comes with thoughts of harming yourself, tell someone today rather than waiting for an appointment. In the US you can call or text 988 at any time. Saying the awkward first sentence out loud is the hardest part of this, and it is usually the thing that starts it getting better.

If you are in crisis, call or text 988 (Suicide & Crisis Lifeline, US) or your local emergency number. Theraglee is a wellbeing tool, not a substitute for professional care.
