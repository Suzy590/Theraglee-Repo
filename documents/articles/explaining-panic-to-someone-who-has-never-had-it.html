Explaining Panic to Someone Who Has Never Had It

People who have never had a panic attack reach for the nearest thing they know, which is ordinary nerves. What to say so the difference lands, and what to ask them for.

You try to describe it and you can see them translating as you speak. They are matching it to the most nervous they have ever been, because that is the only file they have, and what they say back is kind and completely beside the point.

It is not their fault, and it is still exhausting. Having to argue for the size of your own experience is a second job on top of the first.

Why the translation goes wrong

Everyone has been anxious. Almost nobody who has not had a panic attack has any idea it is a different category of thing.

The word is the problem. Panic in ordinary speech means flustered, and the listener hears a familiar word rather than a description of something they have not met.

It also sounds like it should be controllable, because their version was. They talked themselves down before a speech, so they assume the same move is available to you, only harder.

And the physical part does not come across. They imagine a feeling. They do not imagine a heart rate that would worry a doctor, hands that will not work, or genuinely believing they are about to die, because nothing in their experience includes those.

What tends to land

A few approaches work better than an argument about severity, which never goes anywhere.

Lead with the body, not the fear. My heart goes to a hundred and sixty, I cannot feel my hands, I cannot get a full breath. Physical facts are hard to downgrade, and they stop the conversation being about how brave you are.

Say what the thought actually is. At the time I am certain I am dying. People hear worried and substitute mild, and the real sentence corrects that instantly.

Name that it is not about the situation. It happened in the car outside my own house. That cuts off the search for a cause, which is where most listeners go next and where nothing useful is.

Use a comparison from their life if you need one. Describe the half second after a near miss on the highway, then say it is that, for twenty minutes, with no car. Most people have had that moment and it gets closer than anything else.

And give it a shape. It climbs for a few minutes, peaks, and comes down, and afterward I am wrung out for hours. Knowing it has an arc helps them more than any adjective.

What to ask them for, specifically

This is the part most people leave out, and it is the part that changes anything.

Tell them what helps in the moment, concretely. Stay, do not ask questions, say it will pass, keep your voice low. People default to problem-solving and need to be told not to.

Tell them what does not. Being told to calm down, being handed water as a project, being asked what is wrong, having an audience assembled.

Tell them what you need afterward, which is usually different. Most people want quiet and no analysis for an hour, and then possibly to talk about it later.

And tell them the plan for a public one. Where to take you, what to say to anyone who asks, whether to call anyone. Settling that in advance removes the worst part for both of you.

When they still do not get it

Some people will not, and it is worth deciding how much of your energy to spend on that.

You do not owe anybody a successful explanation. If three attempts have not landed, the useful move is usually to stop explaining and start stating what you need, which does not require them to understand it.

Written things carry further than conversation for some people. A short page from a reputable source does what an argument cannot, because it is not you making a claim about yourself.

And notice the difference between not understanding and not believing you. The first is ordinary and fixable with time. The second is a different problem and it is worth naming, because managing panic around someone who thinks you are exaggerating is much harder than managing it alone.

Talking to someone who already knows

The relief of talking to a person who does not need the explanation is hard to overstate, and most people underestimate how much it helps.

A therapist is one version of that, and panic responds well to treatment that is practical and usually short. Your doctor is worth telling too, both to rule out anything physical and because they can refer you. And if the attacks are getting more frequent, if you are rearranging your life to avoid places, or if a bad one leaves you frightened in a way that does not lift afterward, take that to a doctor or a therapist rather than carrying it alone.
