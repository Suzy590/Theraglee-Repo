Spending the Holidays With Family You Find Hard

Four days in the house you grew up in, with the people who can still reduce you to fifteen. How to plan the visit so you leave it intact, and what to do when it goes wrong anyway.

BoundariesFamily Visitor 941 words

The dates are booked. You will be in that house, with those people, for several days, sleeping in a room that still has your old posters in it. And you already know roughly how it will go: the comment about your job, the thing your brother always does, the moment you hear yourself answering in a voice you have not used since you were fifteen.

Family visits are hard in a specific way. Everyone reverts. The roles you were assigned as a child are still lying around the house, and within an hour everyone has picked theirs back up.

Here is how to plan one that costs you less.

Decide in advance what you actually want

Not what a perfect visit looks like. What would count as a good enough one? Usually something modest: I want to see my parents, I want to leave without a fight, I want two real conversations.

Naming that ahead of time is useful, because in the moment you will be tempted by other goals, being understood, winning the argument, getting someone to finally admit something, and those are the goals that turn a visit into a wreck.

Shorten it and get your own exit

The single most effective change most people make is going for less time and staying somewhere else. Three days instead of six. A hotel or a rental instead of the spare room. Your own car rather than being driven.

This feels like a rejection and it usually improves everything, because it means the visit has an end you control and you can leave when you have had enough, which makes the time you are there far more pleasant for everyone.

Plan the day with structure

Unstructured hours in a tense house are where things go wrong. Put things in the day: a walk after lunch, a specific errand, a movie in the evening, an hour when you take the dog out alone. Activity gives everyone something to do besides each other, and gives you a legitimate way to step out.

Have your lines ready

You know which topics come up. Prepare a short, warm, unhooked answer for each, and use it every time rather than engaging.

We are not discussing that this week, but tell me about the garden. I know you see it differently. That is a bigger conversation than today. I am not going to talk about that, I love you, can you pass the salt.

The trick is to be pleasant and completely unmovable, and to change the subject immediately. Do not explain your reasons; explaining invites debate. Repeat the same sentence calmly as many times as needed.

Plan your exits, too

Know in advance where you can go for ten minutes: outside, the store, a walk, the car. Use them before you need them, not after. Most blowups happen at hour six of unrelieved contact.

Watch for the moment you revert

There is usually a specific moment when you drop back into the old role: the peacemaker, the scapegoat, the one who manages everyone's feelings, the child who defends themselves. When you feel it, try naming it silently: there it is, I am doing the old thing.

That small noticing is often enough to give you a choice about what to do next, which is the whole of it.

Decide what you will not do this year

You do not have to relitigate the past at Christmas dinner. You do not have to correct every false claim about your life. You do not have to attend every event, cook everything, or be the one who smooths it over. Pick one or two things you are letting go of before you arrive.

If you have a partner or children with you

Agree on a signal and a plan before you get there: how long you are staying, what you will do if it gets bad, a word one of you can say that means help me get out of this conversation. A partner who knows the plan is a huge asset. A partner who is surprised in the middle of it is another problem to manage.

If your children are present, remember they are watching how you handle it, and that protecting them from the sharper edges of your family is entirely within your rights.

Plan the landing

The day after can be worse than the visit. Leave a buffer: do not fly home and go straight to work. Arrange something restorative, and arrange to talk to someone who knows what the visit is like for you. Expect to feel flattened for a day or two; that is the cost coming due, not evidence that you handled it badly.

When it is right not to go

Some families are not simply difficult. If visiting means exposure to someone who abused you, to genuine cruelty, or to a level of contempt that leaves you unwell for weeks, you are allowed not to go. Not this year, or not at all. That choice comes with guilt and with pressure from relatives, and it is still yours to make.

When to get help

If the approach of a family visit produces dread for weeks, if you come back from them depressed or unable to function, or if being around your family stirs up memories that are hard to handle, please talk to a therapist, ideally before the trip rather than after. Working out in advance what you will and will not do, with someone who has no stake in your family, makes an enormous difference. You are an adult now, even in that house, and you get to decide the terms.
