When Someone Tells You Something Heavy

A friend tells you what happened to them, and you freeze. What actually helps in that conversation, what not to say, and how to carry it afterward without it flattening you.

TraumaBoundaries Visitor 890 words

It comes out suddenly, usually sideways, usually at the end of an ordinary evening. Something that happened to them years ago, or last month. Abuse, an assault, a death they have never described, a thing they have told nobody.

And you sit there with your heart going, aware that whatever you say next matters, and completely unsure what it should be.

You do not have to fix it

The commonest mistake is reaching for a solution, because the discomfort of just listening is considerable. But nobody tells you something like this because they want it solved in the next ten minutes.

What they want is almost always simpler: to be believed, and to find out that telling you did not cost them the relationship.

So the most useful thing you can do is stay in the room, stay steady, and let them talk at the speed they are talking.

The sentences that help

Short and plain beats eloquent. These do most of the work.

Thank you for telling me.

I believe you.

That should not have happened to you.

I am glad you are still here.

What would help right now, me listening or me saying something.

Then be quiet and let the silence sit. People filling silences is why a lot of these conversations go wrong.

The ones that land badly

Not because the speaker is unkind, but because of what they do to the person telling you.

Why did you not tell anyone, or why did you stay, which both sound like questions about their judgment.

At least it was not worse. Everything happens for a reason. You need to forgive them to move on. Time heals.

My cousin went through the same thing, and then twenty minutes about your cousin.

I always knew there was something wrong with that man, which turns it into your insight.

And the one that does the most damage: pushing them to report it, confront someone, or tell a family member, right now, when they have just told you.

Ask before you act

This is the piece people get wrong with the best intentions. Having heard something awful, you want to do something, and you start suggesting police, lawyers, phone calls, telling a parent.

Control is often exactly what was taken from them. So ask rather than organize. Would you like me to help you look into what the options are. Would you like me to come with you if you ever want to talk to someone. And then respect a no, including a no that lasts years.

The exception is a child or someone who cannot protect themselves being at risk now, or someone in immediate danger. There, you do have to act, and it is better to say so plainly: I have to tell someone about this because a child is involved, and I will tell you exactly what I am doing.

Do not make them manage your reaction

Crying, going pale, getting furious, or saying I cannot bear to think about it puts the person who was harmed in the position of looking after you. Many people decide never to tell anyone again after that experience.

Feel all of it, and feel most of it later. In the moment, your job is to be the steady one for half an hour.

Ask what they need afterward, in specifics

Do you want me to check in about this, or would you rather I never mention it. Is it all right to ask how you are. Is there anything you want me to not do.

That conversation is worth more than any promise of support, because it tells you what being helpful looks like for this particular person.

Keep it

Unless someone is in danger, what they told you is theirs. Not for your partner, not for the family group chat, not for the friend who knows them.

If you need to talk it through, and you may well, do it with a therapist or a helpline rather than someone who knows them.

Then look after yourself, because this does land on you

Hearing something terrible about someone you love has a weight. People commonly feel shaken for days, sleep badly, find the images intrusive, or feel guilty for not having known.

That is a normal response to secondhand horror rather than weakness. Take it seriously: get sleep, move, talk to somebody outside the situation, and give yourself a few days of lower demands.

If it stirred up something of your own, which is common, that is worth attention in its own right rather than being pushed down because this is not about you.

When to get help

Please encourage them toward a therapist who works with trauma when they are ready, and offer practical help with finding one rather than a push to go. Say plainly that help exists and that it works, because many people assume it is too late or too long ago. For yourself, talk to a therapist or your doctor if you are not sleeping, if the images will not leave you, or if it set off something from your own life. And if they ever tell you they are thinking of ending their life, stay with them and get help the same day. In the US you can call or text 988 at any time.

If you are in crisis, call or text 988 (Suicide & Crisis Lifeline, US) or your local emergency number. Theraglee is a wellbeing tool, not a substitute for professional care.
