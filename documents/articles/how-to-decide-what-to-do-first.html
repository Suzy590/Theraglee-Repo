How to Decide What to Do First

Everything on the list is urgent, so you do none of it. A few quick ways to sort an overwhelming day into an order you can actually start on.

Decision MakingStress Visitor 926 words

There are fourteen things on the list. Four of them have deadlines. Two of them you have been avoiding for a week, which is why they are now urgent. You have read the list three times and done nothing.

This is not a time management failure. It is what happens when a list exceeds what a person can hold at once: the sorting itself becomes the hardest task, and sorting has no satisfying end point.

So the aim here is not a perfect order. It is to get to one thing you can start.

Get it out of your head first

You cannot prioritize a list you are holding in your memory, because the holding uses the attention you need for the deciding.

So write everything down, fast and badly, including the small things and the things that are only slightly on your mind. Five minutes, no sorting.

Most people find the list is shorter than it felt. A dozen items written down is manageable; a dozen items circling is not. Even if it is long, you can now look at it rather than carry it.

Ask the four questions, in order

Then go through the list once with these, which take about a minute each.

Is this actually mine? Some items belong to somebody else and have attached themselves to you. Hand them back, or at least note who else could own them.

What happens if it is late? Be honest rather than anxious. Some things have real consequences by Friday. Many have none, and the deadline was invented by you or by a long-dead email.

Is anyone else blocked on it? Anything that unblocks somebody else usually goes early, because its cost multiplies while it waits.

Is it quick? Anything under two minutes, do it now rather than ranking it. A list cluttered with tiny items is harder to face than a shorter list of real ones.

That pass alone usually cuts the list in half and puts three or four things at the top.

Pick the one that unlocks the most

If several items survive, the useful tie-breaker is not importance in the abstract. It is which one, once done, makes the others easier or unnecessary.

The call that tells you whether the whole project is going ahead. The decision three people are waiting on. The thing you have to look at before you can estimate anything else.

That is usually the right first task, even when it is not the most urgent, because the rest of the list is partly a fog that it clears.

The avoided one deserves a different rule

Some items are not on the list because they are hard; they are hard because they have been on the list for three weeks.

Those have a cost you are already paying: the low-level dread, the daily decision not to do it, the thing that is quietly getting worse.

So give the oldest avoided item a slot early in the day, at a size you will actually do: fifteen minutes, or just the first step. It pays back more than its size because it stops the drain.

When everything genuinely is urgent

Sometimes the honest answer is that there is more than one person can do in the time, and no order fixes that.

Then the task is not prioritizing, it is telling someone. Go to whoever sets the work with the list and a specific question: here are the five things, here is what I can realistically do by Thursday, which two matter most.

That conversation feels risky and is nearly always welcome. Managers would far rather choose than discover on Friday. And a decision you made alone about what to drop is much harder to defend than one they made with you.

Where you cannot escalate, make the trade visible in writing: I am doing A and B first, which means C moves to next week. Said plainly and in advance, that is almost never a problem.

Protect the first hour

An order is only useful if something gets done in it, and the beginning of the day is where the day is decided.

So put the chosen task first, before messages. Email reorders your list according to other people's priorities, and once that has happened, your own ranking is gone.

If your work makes that impossible, protect any one consistent hour instead, and spend it on the chosen thing rather than on whatever is loudest.

Re-sort once, not continuously

A list you re-rank six times a day is a way of avoiding all of it.

Do the sorting once in the morning and once at the end of the day, and in between let the order stand even when something new arrives. New items go on the list, not to the front of it, unless something is genuinely on fire.

That one rule saves more time than any system, because the constant re-deciding is where most of the day leaks out.

When the paralysis is the real problem

If you look at the list and cannot start anything, if this happens most days, or if the fog is thick enough that the sorting itself is impossible, the obstacle may not be the workload.

Exhaustion, long-running low mood, high anxiety and the different ways attention works for some people all show up exactly like this. Those are worth a conversation with a doctor or a therapist rather than another system, especially if the list has stopped moving for weeks and the dread about it is affecting your sleep.
