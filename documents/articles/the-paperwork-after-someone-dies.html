The Paperwork After Someone Dies

Accounts to close, forms that ask the same question nine times, and hold music while you are barely upright. How to get through the administration of a death.

LossResilience Visitor 981 words

You are on hold to a utility company, explaining for the fourth time this week that your father has died. The person is kind. The system is not. You have a folder on the kitchen table that is somehow both empty and overwhelming.

Nobody warns you that grief comes with a job attached, and that the job arrives at the exact moment you are least able to do anything.

Why this is harder than it looks on paper

The tasks themselves are mostly ordinary: forms, phone calls, certificates. What makes them brutal is everything around them.

You are doing administration while in shock, which is a genuine state rather than a figure of speech: concentration goes, short-term memory goes, and simple decisions take far longer than usual.

Every call means saying the sentence again. Each one is a small reopening, and by the eighth you are either numb or in tears on hold, and both feel wrong.

And there is a false urgency in the air. It feels as though everything must be done immediately. Very little of it must.

Sort it into three piles

Before doing anything, split the list, because treating it all as urgent is what flattens people.

Now: registering the death, the funeral arrangements, and anything to do with a tenancy or pets or a house standing empty. Those have real deadlines.

Soon: notifying banks, pensions, benefits, insurance, employer, landlord or mortgage lender, utilities, and starting probate if there is any. Weeks, not days.

Later: closing social media accounts, subscriptions, the car, the clothes, the house itself. Months, and often longer.

Write the three lists on paper and keep them in one place. The folder on the table is doing more work than you think; the point is that nothing has to live in your head.

Get more copies than you think you need

Almost every organization wants proof, and photocopies are frequently refused.

So order several certified copies of the death certificate when you register the death. Four or five saves many weeks of posting one document around the country.

Ask each organization the same two questions: what exactly do you need from me, and is there a bereavement team I should be speaking to? Most large companies have one, they are far better trained than the general line, and almost nobody knows to ask.

Make the calls easier on yourself

Batch them, two or three at a time, in the morning, with tea and a pen, not at night when you are tired.

Have a script and read it: I am calling because my mother died on the twelfth, I am her executor, I need to know what you require. Reading it rather than improvising takes a surprising amount out of it.

Keep one notebook with the date, who you spoke to, the reference number, and what happens next. Everything will go round again at least once, and the notebook is what stops you repeating the whole process from memory.

And let someone else do some of them. A friend, a sibling, an adult child can make a list of calls with your authority, and people who want to help are usually relieved to be given something concrete.

Get help with the parts that are genuinely technical

Probate, inheritance tax, pensions and anything involving a business or a property abroad are not common sense, and guessing is expensive.

A solicitor or an accountant for a few hours is often cheaper than a mistake, and in many cases the estate pays. If money is tight, free advice exists: bereavement charities, legal aid clinics, the probate office's own helpline, and your own bank's bereavement team.

If there is no money in the estate, say so out loud to each creditor. Debts do not pass to relatives in most circumstances, and nobody should be paying a dead person's credit card out of their own wages without advice.

Do not decide the big things yet

In the middle of all this you will be asked, often by well-meaning family, what you are going to do about the house, the money, the car, the move.

Where you can, answer: not yet. Decisions made in the first months are made by someone who is not at full capacity, and almost nothing is improved by rushing.

The exceptions are real deadlines and anything losing money. Those are worth doing on a timetable somebody else helps you set.

Expect the admin to carry feelings in it

You will be fine for an hour and then undone by the smallest thing: their handwriting on a direct debit form, their voice still on the answering machine at the bank, a letter arriving addressed to them in November.

That is normal and it is not a sign that you are handling this badly. Stop when it happens. Nobody has ever been harmed by a form being posted tomorrow.

And set a limit: one or two tasks a day, then nothing. This work expands to fill whatever you give it, and it will happily take months of your evenings.

When to get help

Please talk to a doctor or a therapist if you are not sleeping, not eating, unable to work, or finding that weeks are passing with no change at all in how heavy it is.

Bereavement services will often see you without a referral, and they are used to people who are three forms into probate and cannot stop crying on the phone; so are the bereavement teams at banks and utilities, more than you would expect. Grief counseling is a reasonable thing to ask for while the administration is still going on, not only afterward.

And if it has taken you somewhere dark, and you are not sure you want to be here, please tell a doctor or someone you trust today. In the US you can call or text 988 at any time.

If you are in crisis, call or text 988 (Suicide & Crisis Lifeline, US) or your local emergency number. Theraglee is a wellbeing tool, not a substitute for professional care.
