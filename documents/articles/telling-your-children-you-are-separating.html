Telling Your Children You Are Separating

The conversation most separating parents dread. How to plan it, what to say in the first thirty seconds, which three things to state outright, and what to leave out of it entirely.

ParentingLoss Visitor 960 words

You have decided, or it has been decided, and now there is a conversation to have with people who did not choose any of it. Most parents dread this more than any other part of separating.

You cannot make this painless. What you can do is make it clear, kind and free of things they have to carry for you, and that difference lasts for years.

Plan it before you say it

Agree the words with the other parent first, in writing if talking is hard. What you are saying, what is happening next, and what you are both not going to say.

Tell them together where that is possible, even if being in a room together is unpleasant for an hour. Hearing it from both of you at once says this is a shared decision and not a side to pick.

Pick a time with space after it. Not bedtime, not before school, not on the way out. A weekend morning with a free afternoon lets the questions come in waves, which is how they actually come.

Tell all the children at once if they are close in age, then follow up one to one. A teenager and a six-year-old need the same facts and completely different conversations.

Say the plain version first

Children need the headline before the explanation, and adults tend to do it the other way around.

Something like: we have decided to live in different homes. This is not going to change. We both love you and that will not change either.

Then stop and let it land. The silence is unbearable and it is theirs to fill.

Keep it short. Three or four sentences is the whole announcement. Everything else is answering what they ask.

Answer the questions they actually have

Their questions are rarely the ones you rehearsed. They are almost always logistics, because logistics is where a child's safety lives.

Where will I sleep. Will I change schools. What about the dog. Will I still see Grandma. Can I still do swimming on Tuesdays. Who will take me.

Answer what you know plainly, and for what you do not know, say that: we have not worked that out yet, and when we do, we will tell you straight away. A child can hold not knowing. What they cannot hold is sensing that they are being managed.

Say the three things out loud

Children fill gaps with themselves, so say these explicitly rather than hoping they are obvious.

This is not your fault, and nothing you did or said caused it.

You cannot fix it, and it is not your job to try.

We both love you, and you are allowed to love us both.

That last one matters more than it sounds. Children will hide their affection for one parent to protect the other if they are not told plainly that they do not have to.

Leave the adult reasons out

The reasons are real and they are yours. A child who is handed them is handed a verdict to reach about a parent they love, and that is too heavy for anyone.

So no blame, no detail about money, nobody else's name, no reading them messages, no asking what the other parent said. However unfair that feels, and sometimes it is very unfair, you are not giving up the truth. You are keeping a child out of a courtroom.

If someone has behaved badly, children usually work it out in their own time, as adults, with the whole picture. That version does them far less damage than yours.

Expect reactions that do not look like sadness

Some children cry. Many say nothing, go to their room, or ask if they can go and play, which parents often read as it did not sink in. It did.

Watch for anger at whoever is nearest, going quiet, going backward in sleep or behavior, suddenly becoming very good and helpful, or stomach aches and headaches with no cause.

All of that is normal in the early weeks. Keep the ordinary things running, because a familiar Tuesday does more for a frightened child than any conversation.

Then say it again. Once is an announcement. The same conversation, picked up casually over months, is how it gets believed.

Look after your own state

You will be grieving while being the steady one, which is the hardest ask in this. Children cope best when at least one adult near them is holding together, and holding together is not the same as being fine.

So have somewhere for your own feelings that is not your child, your child's hearing, or the other parent. A friend, a sibling, a therapist, a support line. Crying in front of them sometimes is honest and fine. Being comforted by them regularly is not, because it quietly makes them the parent.

Tell the school. One email so a teacher knows why the homework slipped and can keep an eye out.

When to get help

Please talk to your doctor or a family therapist if a child's distress is not easing after a couple of months, if they are not sleeping or eating, if they are refusing school, or if their mood has dropped and stayed down. Family counseling is often short and practical, and a few sessions with a professional who works with separating families can take a great deal of weight off the way you talk to each other. For yourself, do not wait until you are at the end of it to ask for support. If you are having thoughts of ending your life, or of not being here, please tell your doctor or someone close to you today. In the US you can call or text 988 at any time.

If you are in crisis, call or text 988 (Suicide & Crisis Lifeline, US) or your local emergency number. Theraglee is a wellbeing tool, not a substitute for professional care.
