Grieving Someone Nobody Else Knew

An online friend, an ex, a colleague, someone you had not seen in years. How to mourn a person your own life has no official place for.

GriefLoneliness Visitor 944 words

Someone died and you are not sure who to tell. They were a friend from a group online you have never mentioned. Or an ex you still spoke to. Or a colleague from another job, or an aunt the family fell out with, or someone you loved years ago.

There is no funeral you are invited to, nobody at work who knows to be gentle with you, and no obvious reason you should be this upset. And you are.

Grief does not check your credentials

The depth of a loss has very little to do with how it would look on paper.

Someone you spoke to every day for three years in a message thread can matter more than a relative you saw twice. A person who got you through a bad patch holds a particular place regardless of how long ago it was.

What you are grieving is a relationship, and only you know what was in it.

Why the lack of recognition hurts separately

Most of what makes grief bearable is other people doing things: knowing, asking, bringing food, sitting with you, marking the date.

When nobody around you knew the person, none of that machinery starts. You carry it in a normal week, among people who have no idea why you are quiet, which is a second loss sitting on top of the first.

There is often an extra sting too: the sense that you are not entitled to this. That belongs to the family, you were only a colleague, you had not spoken in years. Disenfranchised grief is the term, and the useful part of knowing it has a name is that it stops you arguing with yourself about whether you are allowed.

Tell at least one person

The silence is the part you can actually change, and it changes more than you would expect.

Pick someone who will not require an explanation of the whole history: a partner, a friend, a sibling. Say it plainly. Somebody died who mattered to me and I do not really have anywhere to put it.

If there is nobody close who fits, the people who shared the person are often the right ones — the group chat, the forum, the old team. Grief shared with people who knew them does something no amount of general sympathy can.

And where the only option is a stranger, a bereavement helpline or a counselor is a reasonable choice rather than an overreaction.

Make your own marker

Without a funeral or an anniversary that anyone else observes, the loss can stay oddly unfinished.

So do something deliberate. Write to them, in full, and keep it or burn it. Go somewhere you associate with them. Cook the thing they liked. Plant something. Give to something they cared about. Put their photograph somewhere for a week.

Pick a date and mark it in your diary — their birthday, the day you heard — so the year has a place for them rather than ambushing you.

Rituals look small from outside. They do a surprising amount of the work.

If you want to reach the family

Sometimes you want contact, and sometimes you are not sure it is your place.

A short note is almost always welcome and almost never intrusive: I worked with him for four years and he was kind to me when I was new, I am so sorry. Families keep those. You are giving them a piece of their person they did not have.

You do not have to attend anything, ask for anything, or explain who you are beyond a line. And if the relationship was one the family did not know about or did not approve of, it is reasonable to stay away and do your grieving privately; that is a protection rather than a denial.

Expect it to be complicated

Many of these losses are tangled, which is part of why they are hard to speak about.

An ex you still loved, or did not. Someone who hurt you. A person you had cut off. Someone you meant to contact and did not, which brings guilt sitting next to the sadness.

All of that can be true at once. Grief about a complicated person is usually a mixture rather than one feeling, and trying to resolve it into something tidy is what keeps people stuck.

Allow it the time it needs

Because nobody knows, there is a temptation to give yourself a weekend and then carry on.

That rarely works. Plan for it to turn up unexpectedly for a while: in the car, at the supermarket, in the hour before sleep. Lower the week's demands if you can, the way you would if the loss were recognized.

And if you need a day, take a day. You do not have to say whose funeral it is.

When to get help with it

Please talk to a doctor or a therapist if weeks are passing with no change, if you cannot work or sleep, if you are drinking more, or if the feeling of being unentitled to the grief is keeping you from saying it out loud to anyone.

Bereavement services will see you for any loss, including one with no official status: an online friend, an ex, a pet, a colleague, someone you never met in person. Nobody there will ask whether you qualify, and counseling for exactly this is both common and effective.

And if it has taken you somewhere dark, and you are not sure you want to be here, please tell a doctor or someone you trust today. In the US you can call or text 988 at any time.

If you are in crisis, call or text 988 (Suicide & Crisis Lifeline, US) or your local emergency number. Theraglee is a wellbeing tool, not a substitute for professional care.
