How to Speak Up in a Meeting

You have the thought, your heart speeds up, and by the time you have rehearsed it the moment has passed. Why speaking in groups feels so risky, and small ways to get your voice into the room.

Social AnxietyWork Visitor 844 words

You know the feeling. Someone raises a question in the meeting and you have a good answer. You start to form the sentence in your head. You check it for mistakes. You wonder if it is obvious. You wonder if it will sound stupid. Your heart is going faster now. And then someone else says something, the conversation moves on, and your answer stays inside you, unsaid, again.

Afterward you are annoyed with yourself, and maybe a little sad. You have things to say. You just cannot seem to say them.

Why the room feels dangerous

Speaking in a group is a small act of exposure. For a few seconds, every face is on you, and the part of your brain that watches for social danger treats that attention as a threat. It is not being dramatic. Being judged by the group was, for most of human history, a real risk. Your body is responding to an old alarm.

The alarm gets louder if you have ever been laughed at, cut off, or corrected sharply in front of others. It gets louder still if you tend to believe your thoughts have to be perfect to be worth saying. That belief is the real trap. It sets a bar nobody in the room is actually holding you to.

Nobody else is polished either

Listen closely in your next meeting to what the confident people actually say. Half-finished sentences. Restarts. Obvious points. Ums and ahs. They are not smoother than you. They are just less worried about it, so they keep talking through the mess. The polish you think you need does not exist in the room. Only the fear of needing it does.

Say something early

The longer you stay silent in a meeting, the harder it gets to speak. Silence builds pressure, and after twenty minutes, your first words feel like they have to justify the wait. So say something in the first five minutes, and let it be small. Agree with a point. Ask a clarifying question. Repeat back what you heard. It does not have to be brilliant. It just has to break the seal, so the second contribution is easier.

Lower the bar deliberately

Before you speak, replace the question, is this good enough, with a different one: is this true and relevant? If yes, it qualifies. You are not auditioning. You are contributing to a conversation, and conversations need ordinary, honest contributions far more than they need brilliance.

Have a few openers ready

Part of the freeze is not knowing how to get in. Keep a few phrases in your pocket that make entering easy.

Can I add something to that?

I have a slightly different read on this.

One thing I am wondering about is...

Before we move on, can I go back to the point about...

These buy you a second of floor time, and the sentence that follows them can be simple.

Let your body help

Right before you speak, put both feet flat on the floor, exhale slowly, and lower your shoulders. Then speak a little slower than feels natural. The racing heart pushes you to rush, and rushing makes you feel out of control. Slowing down, even slightly, tells your body that you are in charge of this moment.

If your voice shakes, let it. People notice shaky voices far less than you think, and when they do, they mostly feel warmth, not judgment. A shaky voice that says something worth hearing beats a steady silence every time.

Use writing when you need to

If speaking in real time is too much right now, contribute in other ways while you build up. Send your thought in the chat. Put it in an email after the meeting. Ask for a minute at the start of the next one. Getting your ideas into the room in any form counts, and it makes the spoken version easier later, because people already know you have things to say.

Expect it to be uncomfortable for a while

Speaking up will not feel good the first several times. It will feel like standing on a ledge. That is not a sign you are doing it wrong. It is what the skill feels like while it is being learned. What changes over weeks is not that the ledge disappears; it is that you stop believing you will fall. Each time you speak and the room does not collapse, the alarm gets a little quieter.

When it is bigger than meetings

If the fear of being seen shows up everywhere, not just at work, if you avoid social situations to escape it, or if you replay every interaction for hours looking for what you did wrong, that is worth more than a set of tips. Social anxiety is very common and very responsive to help, and a therapist can work with you on it in a way that changes the pattern rather than just managing the next meeting. You deserve to be heard, and to find that easier than it is right now.
