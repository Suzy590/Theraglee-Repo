Living in Fear of the Next Panic Attack

The attack itself lasted minutes. The fear of the next one has lasted months. Why the dread between attacks can shrink your life more than the attacks do, and how to take it back.

PanicWorry Visitor 948 words

The panic attack was terrible, and it ended. What has not ended is the watching. You scan your body for the first flutter. You avoid the grocery store where it happened, then the highway, then anywhere you could not leave quickly. You carry water and a plan and a quiet dread, and you organize your days around the possibility of something that may never come.

If this is you, you have discovered the part of panic that nobody warns you about. The attacks are frightening, but they are short. The fear of the next one is what moves in and stays.

How the fear takes over

After an attack, the mind does what minds do after a scare: it tries to make sure it never happens again. It starts monitoring. Every heartbeat, every breath, every slight dizziness gets checked against the question, is this the start of one?

The trouble is that a body under this kind of surveillance produces plenty to notice. Hearts skip. Breathing changes. Standing up quickly makes anyone lightheaded. Each ordinary sensation becomes a possible alarm, the alarm creates real fear, and the fear produces exactly the sensations you were watching for. The watching becomes the trigger.

Then the avoidance starts. You skip the place where it happened. Relief. You skip a similar place. More relief. Each avoided place teaches your mind that the place was dangerous, and the list grows. Eventually the fear of panic has taken more from you than any single attack did.

What an attack is, and is not

It helps to hold onto the facts. A panic attack is your body's emergency system firing without an emergency. It floods you with adrenaline, speeds your heart, quickens your breath, and tells you to run or fight. It is intensely uncomfortable, and it is not dangerous. It does not cause heart attacks, fainting, or losing your mind. It peaks within minutes and then subsides, because the body cannot hold that state for long.

You have survived every attack you have had. That is not luck. It is how they work.

Stop scanning

The first thing to change is the monitoring. When you catch yourself checking your body, name it: I am scanning. Then deliberately put your attention somewhere outside yourself. What can you see in the room? What is someone saying? What does the task in front of you need? You will drift back to scanning; that is fine. Redirect again. You are teaching your attention that it does not have to stand guard.

Let the sensations be there

When you notice a flutter or a skipped breath, try a different response than alarm. Oh, there is a fast heartbeat. That is a sensation. It can be here. The goal is not to make it stop, because trying to make it stop is what turns it into panic. The goal is to let it pass through without a fight. It will, usually faster than you expect.

Some people find it useful to practice this on purpose: run up the stairs to get the heart going, spin in a chair to feel dizzy, breathe through a straw for a minute. Feeling the sensations in a safe setting, and learning that they fade on their own, takes away a lot of their power. If you try this, go slowly, and consider doing it with a therapist's guidance first.

Take back the places one at a time

Avoidance keeps the fear alive, so the way out is to go back, gradually and on purpose. Make a list of the places and situations you have been avoiding, from easiest to hardest. Start with the easiest. Go, stay until the discomfort drops even a little, and leave when you decide to, not when panic decides for you. Then do it again. Then move up the list.

You are not trying to prove you will not panic. You are proving that if you do, you can handle it. That is the belief that ends the fear.

Drop the safety props

The water bottle, the pills you never take, the friend who has to be there, the seat by the exit. These feel protective and they keep the fear going, because every time you get through a situation with the prop, your mind credits the prop rather than you. Try leaving one behind at a time. Notice that nothing changes except the story.

Let the attack come if it comes

The deepest shift is a strange one: stop fighting to prevent the next attack. Say to yourself, if it comes, it comes; I know what it is, and I know it ends. This is not giving up. It is refusing to make an enemy of your own body. People often find that the attacks come less once they stop being braced for them.

If you have thoughts of not wanting to be here

Living in this kind of fear can wear a person down until it feels hopeless. If you ever have thoughts of harming yourself, please tell someone right away. In the US you can call or text 988 at any time.

When to get help

If the fear of panic is shaping where you go, what you do, or who you can be with, please talk to a therapist, ideally one who works with panic and anxiety. This is one of the most treatable problems there is, and the treatment is essentially a guided version of what is described here. A doctor can also check that nothing physical is going on, which is worth doing once so you can stop wondering. You do not have to live braced. That is a life you can get back.

If you are in crisis, call or text 988 (Suicide & Crisis Lifeline, US) or your local emergency number. Theraglee is a wellbeing tool, not a substitute for professional care.
