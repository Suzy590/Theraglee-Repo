The Hour Before You Have to Perform

The talk is at eleven and nothing you do now changes your preparation. Why to stop reading your notes, what to do with the adrenaline, and how to plan for a wobble in advance.

StressMindfulness Visitor 829 words

The talk is at eleven. The recital is at seven. The exam starts in forty minutes and you are sitting in a hallway with a folder you have already read.

The preparation is done, or it is not, and either way nothing you do in the next hour will change it much. What the hour can change is the state you walk in with, and most people spend it making that worse.

Stop preparing

This is the hardest instruction and the most useful one.

Re-reading your notes in the last hour does almost nothing for recall and a great deal for panic, because you will find a gap, and then the gap is all you can think about.

So close the folder. Put it away physically. Decide that what you know is what you are walking in with, which is almost certainly more than the last-minute version of you believes.

The exception is one page: a short list of your opening line and your main three points. Glance at that, once, then put it away too.

Do something with your body

Adrenaline is already in your system and it has nowhere to go, which is what produces the shaking, the dry mouth and the racing thoughts.

So use it up a little. Walk briskly for five minutes. Climb a flight of stairs. Shake your hands out. Push against a wall for ten seconds, twice.

Then slow your breathing deliberately, with the out-breath longer than the in. In for four, out for six or seven, for a couple of minutes. That is the one thing that reliably takes the edge off a braced body.

Warm the instrument you are using. Voice, hands, body, whatever the thing requires. Cold is part of why first minutes go badly.

Rename what you are feeling

The sensations of dread and the sensations of excitement are close to identical: fast heart, tight chest, restlessness, heightened attention.

What differs is the label, and the label changes the performance. People who say to themselves I am excited do measurably better than people who try to calm down, partly because trying to be calm when your body is at full speed is a fight you cannot win.

So say the true version. My body is ready. This matters, so it has turned everything up. That is a reframe rather than a trick, because the physiology genuinely is the same.

Handle the practical things first, early

A surprising amount of pre-performance misery is logistics arriving late.

So get there earlier than you need to. Find the room, the bathroom, the water, the chair, the lectern, the socket. Check the slides open and the sound works. Know where you walk in from.

Eat something small if there is time, and go easy on coffee. A third cup of coffee on an already racing body is the most common unforced error in this hour.

Keep your phone away from the last twenty minutes. Nothing arriving there will help you now.

Narrow your attention

A wide anxious focus takes in the whole room, the stakes, the audience and the future. A narrow one takes in the next thing you have to do.

So shrink it on purpose. Feel your feet. Notice three things you can see. Then think only about your first thirty seconds: the walk in, the first line, the first bar, the first question.

Everything after the first thirty seconds tends to take care of itself, because the preparation is in there and it comes out once you are moving.

Decide in advance what you do when it wobbles

The hour goes better when the plan includes something going wrong, because then a stumble is not a catastrophe.

So pick your recovery now. If I lose my place, I pause and look at my page. If my voice goes, I drink water. If my mind blanks, I say let me come back to that. If I shake, I put my hands on the lectern.

Having rehearsed that once is what keeps a small wobble from becoming the story of the whole thing.

And let the last two minutes be quiet. Not more preparation, not conversation, not a final look at the notes. Just standing somewhere, breathing out slowly, waiting.

When to get help

Please talk to a therapist if the dread before performing is big enough to shrink what you do, such as turning down opportunities, dropping a subject, or leaving something you love, because performance anxiety is specific, common and treatable, often in a short course of sessions. Talk to your doctor if the physical side is severe, if you have had panic attacks around these occasions, or if you are using alcohol to get through them, which is a common and risky route worth replacing with something that works. And if an exam or audition season has your sleep and eating badly disrupted for weeks, that is worth mentioning to a professional rather than enduring on your own.

If you are in crisis, call or text 988 (Suicide & Crisis Lifeline, US) or your local emergency number. Theraglee is a wellbeing tool, not a substitute for professional care.
