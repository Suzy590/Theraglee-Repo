The Month You Move

Everything has to be sorted, packed, canceled and re-registered, and all of it lands at once. How to get through the month of a move without losing your temper or your sleep.

StressLife Transitions Visitor 877 words

There is the move itself, which is one day, and then there is the month around it, which is where the damage happens. Boxes in the hall. A hundred small decisions about things you have not looked at in nine years. Utilities, schools, addresses, deposits, a van, keys, and somebody asking you a question while you are holding a lamp.

And you cannot explain to anybody why you are close to tears about a kitchen drawer.

Why moving is harder than its parts

A move is an unusually dense pile of small decisions, and decisions are the expensive part. Should this be kept, where does it go, who do I tell, when. Hundreds of them, most of them trivial, all of them needing you.

Underneath that there is often a loss nobody acknowledges. Even a move you chose means leaving a place where things happened. If the move came with something harder, a separation, a death, a job ending, money, then you are grieving and doing logistics at the same time, which is why the smallest thing can tip you over.

Put everything in one place, out of your head

The most useful hour you can spend is writing one list of everything the move requires, then giving each item a week.

Address changes: bank, doctor, dentist, insurance, work, school, licenses, subscriptions, anything that bills you.

Services: electricity, gas, water, internet, with the dates.

Logistics: the van or the movers, keys, parking, elevator booking, where the cat goes, who has the children that day.

Money: deposits, final readings, overlapping rent.

One list is less frightening than the same list scattered across your mind at three in the morning.

Pack an open-first box, and pack it first

Not last. The thing that makes the first night bearable is one clearly marked box you can find immediately.

Kettle, mugs, tea, toilet paper, soap, towels, phone chargers, a change of clothes, bedding, any medication, scissors, a bulb, and whatever makes the place feel like yours. Everything else can wait.

Make the bed before you do anything else in the new place. People who do this consistently report the first night as fine, and people who do not are assembling a bed frame at eleven at night in a bad mood.

Do not sort while you pack

This is the single biggest time sink. Opening a box of photographs or old letters while packing turns a twenty-minute job into a three-hour afternoon that leaves you sad and behind.

Pack the obvious, bin the obvious, and put anything requiring a decision into clearly labeled boxes to sort after the move. Keep one box for sentimental things and give it its own evening, later, with a drink and no deadline.

Guard the last week

The last week before a move is where sleep, food and patience go. Protect three things.

Sleep, by stopping at a set time even with boxes unfinished. The boxes will still be there and you will be faster tomorrow.

Food, by giving up on cooking entirely for a few days without guilt.

One small ordinary thing a day: a walk, a shower, ten minutes outside. It will feel like the thing you do not have time for and it is the thing keeping you reasonable.

Expect to be short with the people you love

Nearly everyone who moves with a partner has a bad argument in the final week, usually about something absurd. It is not a sign about the relationship. It is two tired people making hundreds of decisions in a house full of boxes.

It helps to say so in advance: this month is going to make us both snappy, and when it happens let us notice it rather than taking it as evidence. Divide territory rather than deciding everything together, since most of these fights are about the overhead of joint decisions.

Say goodbye to the old place on purpose

Walk through the empty rooms before you hand the keys over. Take a photograph of the room that mattered. Say thank you if you are the sort of person who would.

This sounds sentimental and it does a job: it marks the ending, which makes the new place feel less like limbo.

Then expect the strange weeks afterward

Arriving is not the end. Most people have a stretch of feeling flat, displaced, or quietly homesick, even in a better house, and are surprised by it because the hard part is supposed to be over.

It settles faster if you unpack the kitchen and the bed first, get out and walk the local streets, find one place you can go regularly, and let the rest of the boxes sit for a while.

When to get help

Please talk to your doctor if the stress of a move has turned into not sleeping for weeks, chest pain, headaches or a mood that has not lifted once you are in. If the move came with a loss, a separation or a job ending, that is grief as well as upheaval and a counselor is worth it rather than waiting to feel better. And if money is the part keeping you awake, a free debt or housing adviser will do more good than any amount of coping on your own.

If you are in crisis, call or text 988 (Suicide & Crisis Lifeline, US) or your local emergency number. Theraglee is a wellbeing tool, not a substitute for professional care.
