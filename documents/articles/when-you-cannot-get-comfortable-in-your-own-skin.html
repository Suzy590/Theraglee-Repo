When You Cannot Get Comfortable in Your Own Skin

Jumpy, restless, unable to settle to anything, and sitting still makes it worse. What that agitated feeling usually is, and why moving works better than trying to relax.

MovementMindfulness Visitor 881 words

You cannot sit down. You have started three things and finished none of them, you are irritable with everyone, and your legs want to be somewhere else. Lying down is worse. So is the television.

It is not quite anxiety and not quite anger. It is the feeling of being wired, and the usual advice to relax is useless, because relaxing is exactly what you cannot do.

What the restlessness usually is

Energy that has been mobilized and has nowhere to go.

Your body prepares for action under pressure: heart rate up, muscles ready, attention scanning. If the pressure is a difficult email rather than something physical, none of that gets used, and you are left with a system revved up and parked.

Common feeders, often several at once. A tense day with no movement in it. Too much caffeine. Short sleep, which makes almost everyone jumpy by mid-afternoon. A conversation you are dreading, or one you have just had. Alcohol wearing off. Too many hours on a screen. And for some people, a change in medication or a dose, which is worth mentioning to a doctor rather than pushing through.

Why trying to relax backfires

Sitting down and attempting to be calm asks an activated body to go from ten to one in one step, and it does not work that way.

Worse, when you sit still with nothing to do, your attention goes inward, notices the restlessness, and treats it as a problem to be solved. That adds a layer of frustration to the original agitation.

The ladder has a middle. You come down through movement, not straight to stillness, which is why a walk settles people when a meditation cushion sometimes does not.

Use up the energy first

Do something physical, briefly and without turning it into a project.

A fast ten-minute walk, preferably outside. Stairs. A few sets of something heavy. Scrubbing a floor, digging, carrying the shopping, cycling to the shop. Shaking your hands and arms out hard for thirty seconds, which looks ridiculous and works.

Push against something with real effort for ten seconds, then let go. Press your palms together, push a wall, squeeze a cushion. Tension followed by release gives the body the completion it was waiting for.

The aim is not fitness. It is to let the system finish the thing it started.

Then come down through something rhythmic

Once the edge is off, rhythm settles the rest.

Walking at an easy pace, swimming, kneading dough, sweeping, folding laundry, knitting, playing an instrument badly. Anything repetitive with a bit of physical involvement.

And let your breathing lengthen on its own as you go, rather than making breathing the task. Longer out-breaths than in-breaths will arrive by themselves once your body has moved.

Give your attention something to hold

Agitation is partly a lack of anywhere to put your attention, so hand it something real.

Feel your feet hitting the ground, one, two. Name five things you can see, four you can hear. Hold something cold. Notice the temperature of the air on your face. These are not tricks to feel better; they are places for attention to rest that are not the inside of your chest.

If you want to sit with it rather than move, make it short and make it kind: two minutes, with your feet on the floor, letting the buzzing be there without trying to flatten it. Restlessness tolerated usually fades faster than restlessness fought.

Take the inputs down

Afterward, make the next few hours easier rather than harder.

Caffeine has a long tail; something at four in the afternoon is still working at ten. Alcohol settles you for an hour and then raises your arousal in the second half of the night. Screens and news keep the alarm topped up.

Eat something if it has been hours, because low fuel and jitteriness are hard to tell apart. Get outside in daylight if you can; it does more for the wired feeling than most people expect.

Look for the thing underneath

Restlessness that keeps returning is usually pointing at something.

An unsaid sentence. A decision you are avoiding. A deadline. Anger you have not admitted to. Something you need to leave, or start.

So ask plainly: what am I not doing, or not saying? Quite often the agitation drops noticeably as soon as the real thing is named, even before anything is done about it. Writing for ten minutes with a pen is a good way to find it.

When to get help with it

Please talk to a doctor if this has been constant for weeks rather than coming and going, if it arrived with a new medication or a change in dose, if it comes with a racing heart, weight changes, tremor or heavy sweating, or if you cannot sit still at all. Thyroid trouble, some prescriptions and withdrawal from alcohol can all produce exactly this, and all are worth checking rather than enduring.

And talk to a doctor or a therapist if the restlessness is coming with a low mood, with thoughts moving unusually fast, or with sleep that has broken down. Those are treatable, the help is practical, and being wired and unable to settle is a perfectly good reason to make an appointment.

If you are in crisis, call or text 988 (Suicide & Crisis Lifeline, US) or your local emergency number. Theraglee is a wellbeing tool, not a substitute for professional care.
