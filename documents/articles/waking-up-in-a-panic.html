Waking Up in a Panic

You come out of sleep with your heart hammering and no idea why. Night panic is frightening and poorly understood by most people who have it. What it is, and what to do at three in the morning.

PanicSleep Visitor 838 words

You were asleep. Now you are sitting up in the dark with your heart pounding, sweating, short of breath, certain something is terribly wrong. There was no nightmare you can remember. Nothing happened. And the worst part is the conviction, in that moment, that this is a heart attack or that you are about to die.

It passes. Then you lie awake for two hours, and the next night you go to bed slightly afraid of your own sleep.

Night panic is more common than most people realize, and almost nobody talks about it, partly because it sounds unbelievable in daylight.

What is happening

This is a panic attack that begins during sleep, usually in the earlier, deeper part of the night rather than out of dreaming. The same alarm system fires: adrenaline, racing heart, fast breathing, sweating, a feeling of unreality or doom.

Because you were asleep, there is no triggering thought to explain it, which makes it more frightening, not less. Your mind wakes into a body already in full alarm and immediately starts looking for the emergency. Finding none in the room, it concludes the emergency must be inside you: your heart, your breathing, your brain.

It is not dangerous. It peaks within minutes and subsides, the same way a daytime attack does. But the fear of it can quickly reorganize your whole relationship with going to bed.

What to do in the moment

Name it, out loud if you can. This is a panic attack. It is not dangerous. It will pass in a few minutes. Naming it gives the frightened part of you something accurate to hold.

Do not fight it. Trying to force your heart to slow or your breathing to normalize adds effort and usually prolongs it. Let the wave be there.

Breathe out longer than in. In for four, out for six or eight. The long exhale is the part that signals safety to your nervous system. Do not take big gulping breaths; over-breathing makes the dizziness and tingling worse.

Get your feet on the floor. Sit on the edge of the bed. Press your soles down. Name five things you can see in the dark room.

Cool your face or hands. Cold water at the sink is one of the fastest physical interrupts there is.

Get up rather than lying there. Go to another room, low light, something dull. Lying in bed waiting for it to pass teaches your brain to associate the bed with panic.

What to do afterward

Do not go back to bed until you feel sleepy. Sit somewhere quiet with a book or something undemanding, no bright screens, and go back when your body is ready. Twenty minutes of boredom is better than two hours of lying there tense.

In the morning, resist the urge to spend the day analyzing it. Note it and get on with the day. The more significance the episode is given, the more likely it is to repeat.

Reduce the conditions that make it likely

Night panic gets more frequent with a few reliable things: alcohol in the evening, which fragments the second half of the night and spikes adrenaline; caffeine too late; sleep deprivation, which makes the alarm system twitchier; and a stretch of unrelieved stress with no outlet during the day.

Look at all four honestly. Many people find the frequency drops substantially by changing one or two of them.

Stop bracing at bedtime

Once you have had a few, you start going to bed watchful, checking your heart, alert for the first sign. That vigilance is itself arousing and makes an episode more likely, which is the loop that turns two episodes into a pattern.

It helps to have a plan you trust, which is most of what is written above, and then to go to bed intending to sleep rather than to monitor. If it comes, you know what to do. That stance does more than any amount of trying to prevent it.

Get the physical causes ruled out, once

A few physical things can cause similar night-time symptoms, including sleep apnea, thyroid problems, heart rhythm issues, reflux, and some medications. It is worth one conversation with your doctor to check, partly for your health and partly so you can stop wondering at three in the morning. Do this once and then let it be settled.

When it goes further

If the episodes are frequent, if you have started dreading sleep, if you are avoiding going to bed, or if daytime panic has begun too, please talk to your doctor or a therapist. Panic responds extremely well to treatment, usually within months, and the treatment is largely a structured version of the approach above. And if the sleeplessness and fear have you feeling hopeless, or if you ever have thoughts of harming yourself, please reach out that day. In the US you can call or text 988 at any time. Sleep is supposed to be the safest part of your day. It can be that again.

If you are in crisis, call or text 988 (Suicide & Crisis Lifeline, US) or your local emergency number. Theraglee is a wellbeing tool, not a substitute for professional care.
