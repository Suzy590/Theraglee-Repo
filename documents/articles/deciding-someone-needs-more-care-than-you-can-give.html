Deciding Someone Needs More Care Than You Can Give

You promised you would never put them in a home, and now you are not managing. How to think it through honestly, whose judgment to listen to, and how to live with the choice afterward.

Decision MakingValues Visitor 909 words

You said you would always look after them. Perhaps you said it out loud, to them, years ago. And now you are getting up three times a night, you cannot leave the house, something nearly went badly wrong last week, and the thought you keep pushing away has started arriving anyway.

Then comes the part that makes it almost impossible to think about: the feeling that even considering it is a betrayal.

The promise was made without the facts

When people promise never to let it come to that, they are imagining frailty, not what is actually in front of you now. They are not imagining a hoist, or wandering at three in the morning, or needing two people to manage a bathroom safely.

Holding yourself to a promise made in ignorance of all of that is not loyalty. The thing you were really promising was that you would make sure they were looked after well and treated with dignity. That promise can be kept in more than one way, and sometimes the only way to keep it is with help you cannot provide alone.

Ask a better question than whether to give up

The question is not am I willing to keep going. You have already shown you are. The useful question is: what does this person need now, and where can it be met safely and kindly.

Write down, plainly, what they actually need in a day: the lifting, the medication, the night-times, the supervision, the company. Then write down what you can provide, honestly, including how much you are sleeping. Seeing the two lists next to each other converts a moral agony into a practical comparison, which is where it belongs.

Get the facts before you decide

A lot of this decision is made on assumptions, and the assumptions are often wrong.

Have a proper assessment done, by a doctor, a nurse or a social worker, so you know what level of care this is. Find out what exists between the two options you are imagining: more hours at home, day centers, respite weeks, night cover, adaptations, a different medication schedule, a live-in arrangement. The choice is rarely only you or an institution.

Then visit places, more than one, at different times of day, and ask the unglamorous questions. How many staff tonight. How long do people stay working here. What happens when someone will not eat. Can I come whenever I like. A good place feels unhurried and the residents look attended to.

Also find out what things cost and what help exists with that, because money is a legitimate part of this and pretending otherwise leads to worse decisions.

Include them as much as you can

Wherever the person can take part, they should, even partly. Ask what matters most to them: their own room, a garden, being near you, not being a burden on you, familiar faces. People often surprise their families here, and many say plainly that they do not want their child's life swallowed up.

If they cannot take part, then the standard is what they would have wanted and what keeps them safe and comfortable, not what quiets your guilt fastest.

Notice whose voice is loudest

Relatives who visit twice a year often have the strongest opinions and the least information. Neighbors have opinions too. None of them are doing the nights.

The people worth listening to are the ones who know the case: the district nurse, the doctor, the social worker, the people who have seen this many times. And the one person who has been there every day, which is you.

Expect grief, and do not mistake it for a mistake

If you go ahead, you will probably grieve, even if the decision was plainly right and everyone agrees. Grief is not evidence of error. You are losing the version of this you hoped for, and that is a real loss which deserves to be felt rather than argued with.

The guilt tends to be loudest in the first couple of weeks and then to settle, especially once you see them being looked after by people who are not exhausted.

Your own life is part of the arithmetic

Caregivers often leave themselves out of the calculation entirely, as though their health did not count. It counts. A carer who collapses helps nobody, and a great many people keep going until something gives.

Needing to sleep, work, see your own family and stay well is not selfishness. It is part of what makes long-term care possible at all.

Afterward, keep showing up

Whatever you choose, the relationship continues. If they move, you are still their person: visiting, noticing, asking questions, advocating when something is not right. Families often find that once they are no longer doing the physical work, there is more of them left for the company, and the time together gets better rather than worse.

When to get help

Please ask for a needs assessment and a carer's assessment through your doctor or local services, since both change what is available to you. Talk to your own doctor if you are exhausted, not sleeping or low, because caregivers get ill doing this and it is worth saying out loud. A counselor or a carers' support group helps a great deal with the guilt, which is usually the heaviest part, and talking it through with someone outside the family often makes a decision that felt impossible become clear.

If you are in crisis, call or text 988 (Suicide & Crisis Lifeline, US) or your local emergency number. Theraglee is a wellbeing tool, not a substitute for professional care.
