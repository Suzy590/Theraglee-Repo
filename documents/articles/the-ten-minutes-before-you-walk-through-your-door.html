The Ten Minutes Before You Walk Through Your Door

The handover between the day and home happens in about ninety seconds, and it goes wrong at the same time most evenings. How to use the gap you already have, and how to arrive on purpose.

GroundingEmotion Regulation Visitor 875 words

You pull up outside, or you reach the last corner, and whatever the day was is still running. Then the door opens and there are questions, and a bag to put down, and somebody who has been waiting all day to tell you something, and you are answering from inside the shift you just left.

Half an hour later there has been a small argument about nothing, or you have been short with someone who did not deserve it, and you cannot entirely explain it.

Why the handover goes wrong

The day does not end when it stops. Whatever state the last few hours put you in takes time to come down, and that time is usually spent on the drive home, or not spent at all.

Arriving is a hard transition. You go from one set of demands to a completely different one in the time it takes to open a door, and nothing about the day prepared you for the switch.

You are often at your lowest capacity exactly then. Late afternoon and early evening is when most people have least left, which is also when the most is being asked of them at home.

And the people inside have their own handover happening, usually at the same moment, which is why so many households have a bad half hour at the same time every day.

Using the gap you already have

The drive, the walk from the car, the stairs. There is nearly always a few minutes, and most people fill them with a phone.

Spend the last stretch doing nothing in particular. No calls, no messages, no catching up. The emptiness is what lets the day settle.

Mark the end of the day deliberately. Close the laptop and say it is done, or leave the uniform in the car, or sit for two minutes before turning the engine off. Rituals sound silly, and they work because they give the brain a boundary it can use.

Name the state rather than the events. I am wound up, I am flat, I am still angry about that call. One accurate sentence does more than replaying the day.

And get something physical into the gap if you can. The last part on foot, a flight of stairs, standing outside for a minute. Movement shifts a state faster than thinking about it does.

Arriving on purpose

The first three minutes inside set the tone, and they are mostly automatic for everybody involved.

Go in and do nothing useful first. Greet people before you deal with the bag, the mail, the dishwasher. The order matters more than the amount of time.

Say what you have got, honestly and briefly. I need ten minutes, or I am all right, only tired. People respond well to information and badly to a silent person who seems annoyed.

Do not open the hardest topic in the first ten minutes, and agree that with whoever else lives there. The money conversation, the thing the school said, the plan for the weekend. All of it is better twenty minutes in.

And lower the expectations of the first half hour generally. This is a transition, not a time at which anything good gets decided.

If there is no gap at all

Some people go from a shift straight into picking somebody up, or come home to a person who needs care, and there is no ten minutes anywhere.

Make the gap smaller rather than giving it up. Thirty seconds in the car with the engine off is a real thing, and people who do it consistently report that it changes something.

Use a hard edge instead of a stretch of time. Washing your hands, changing your shoes, a glass of water drunk standing still. One clear action can do the work of a longer pause.

And say what is happening out loud if you cannot get the pause at all. I have just walked in, give me a second, is both true and easier for everyone than the alternative.

What it is not for

Worth being clear about, because the idea gets oversold.

It will not fix a job that is doing you real harm. A transition routine helps with the handover and does nothing about the thing you are handing over from.

It is not a reason to come home late. Taking the gap from the people waiting for you defeats the point of it.

And it is not a performance. If it becomes one more thing to do correctly, it has turned into work, which is the opposite of what it was for.

When the evenings are the hardest part of the day

If you dread arriving, if the first hour at home is regularly where things go wrong, or if you notice you are drinking to make the change happen, that is worth paying attention to rather than managing indefinitely.

A therapist can help with both ends of it, the state you are arriving in and what happens once you get there. And if the day itself is what is doing the damage, that is worth raising with your doctor as well, because long stretches of this tend to show up in your health before they show up anywhere else.
