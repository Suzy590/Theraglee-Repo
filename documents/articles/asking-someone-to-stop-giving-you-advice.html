Asking Someone to Stop Giving You Advice

Unasked-for suggestions from a parent, a friend or a colleague, over and over. Why the advice keeps coming, how to ask for it to stop without a row, and what to do when it does not.

AssertivenessBoundaries Visitor 796 words

You mention that you are tired and you get a list: have you tried going to bed earlier, have you had your iron checked, you know what you should do.

It is meant kindly, usually. And after the fortieth time you stop telling that person anything, which is a loss neither of you chose.

Why it keeps coming

Most unasked-for advice is anxiety with its sleeves rolled up.

When someone cares about you and cannot fix what is happening, suggesting things is the available action. It settles them, which is why it keeps arriving regardless of whether it helps you.

Some of it is habit from an older arrangement: a parent who solved your problems for twenty years does not notice when the role expired. Some of it is how a person shows love, badly. And a little of it is enjoying being the one who knows.

None of those require you to keep receiving it.

Decide what you actually want instead

This is worth a minute's thought, because "stop giving me advice" leaves a gap and people fill a gap with more of what they were doing.

Usually what you want is one of a few things. To be listened to without a solution. To be asked before suggestions arrive. To talk about something else entirely. Or to be helped with a specific thing and nothing else.

Name it to yourself first, then you can ask for something rather than only object to something, which is much easier for the other person to do.

The short version to say

Keep it brief, warm, and about you rather than about their character.

I know you are trying to help. What I need at the moment is just to talk it through, not to be given things to try. Can we do that?

Or: I will ask if I want suggestions. Until then, assume I have thought about it.

Or, for the recurring topic: I have made my decision on this one. I would rather not keep reopening it.

Then stop talking. The urge to soften it with three more sentences is strong and it usually undoes the request.

Say it when nothing is happening

Timing matters more than wording.

Said in the middle of an argument, or straight after a piece of advice, it lands as a rebuke. Said on a walk, in the car, over a cup of tea, when the subject is nowhere near, it lands as information.

And say it once, properly, rather than in small irritated pieces over months. Hints do not work on this; they read as moods.

What to do when it keeps coming

Expect to repeat yourself a few times, and have a short line ready so you are not inventing one each time.

Thank you. I am not looking for ideas on it. Then change the subject in the same breath, because a pause is where the next suggestion goes.

You can also stop supplying the material, which is not sulking, it is sensible. If every mention of your job produces a lecture, talk about other things with that person and talk about your job with someone who listens well. Not everyone gets to be your sounding board on everything.

And notice the difference between someone who forgot and someone who heard you and carried on. The first needs reminding. The second needs less access to the subject.

If it is a parent

This is the hardest version, and a slightly different one.

Advice from a parent often carries an old message underneath it: that you are not quite managing. That is what stings, more than the content, so it is worth saying the real thing rather than arguing about the suggestion.

Something like: when you tell me how to handle my own house, it makes me feel about fifteen. I know you do not mean it that way.

Expect it to take several goes, and expect some hurt. Most parents adjust when the request is specific, repeated, and not delivered as an accusation. Some do not, and then the work is on your side: shorter updates, fewer topics, and letting the suggestion go past you without a reply.

When it is wearing you down

If this is coming from someone you cannot avoid, if it is part of a wider pattern of being overridden or criticized, or if you dread seeing a particular person because of it, that is worth taking seriously rather than tolerating.

Please talk to a therapist or a counselor if saying no to this person feels impossible, if you leave every conversation doubting your own decisions, or if the advice is really control with a kind voice. Those patterns are workable, and a professional can help you find the lines and hold them.
