Joining a Group That Has Been Going for Years

Everyone has inside jokes and a shared history and you have just walked in. Why the first visit is always the worst, why getting a job beats conversation, and when it really is them.

Social AnxietySelf-Esteem Visitor 849 words

Everybody in the room already knows each other. They have inside jokes, a shared history, and a way of doing things nobody has explained. They were all here last year, and the year before. And you have just walked in.

A choir, a running club, a team at work, a book group, a congregation, a class. The activity is not the hard part. The hard part is being the only person without a history in the room.

What is actually happening in that room

It helps to know that most of what you are experiencing is structural rather than personal.

An established group has shortcuts: references, routines, a seating arrangement nobody decided. None of that is aimed at you and all of it is invisible to them, because people stop noticing their own shortcuts within a few months.

So the warmth you are not getting is usually not coldness. It is a group operating at its normal speed, which happens to be a speed you cannot join yet.

And they are not watching you nearly as closely as it feels. Your newness is the biggest thing in the room to you and roughly the fourth most interesting thing to everybody else.

Go more than three times before deciding

This is the single most useful rule, because the first visit is always the worst one and people quit on the evidence of it.

By the third or fourth time, you recognize faces, you know where things are kept, and somebody says hello before you have to start the conversation. Almost nothing about the group has changed. What changed is that you are no longer processing everything at once.

So decide in advance that you are going four times, and treat the first three as data collection rather than a verdict.

Get a job

The fastest route into an established group is almost never conversation. It is usefulness.

Carry something. Set up the chairs. Make the tea. Keep the score. Mind the door. Take the photos. Offer a lift to somebody who needs one.

A job gives you a reason to speak to people, a place to stand, and something to do with your hands, which solves the three hardest problems of being new at once. It also makes you legible: people know who you are because you are the one who does that.

Aim at one person, not the group

Trying to be accepted by a whole group is a large and vague project. Getting to know one person is small and achievable, and it is how it actually happens.

So pick the most approachable person there, which is often somebody slightly on the edge themselves, and talk to them properly. Ask them things. Remember what they said and ask about it next time.

One person who says hello changes the entire experience of walking in. Two is enough to belong.

Ask about the history rather than pretending to share it

Newcomers often try to blend in, which means nodding along to references they do not understand and getting quieter as the evening goes on.

The opposite works better. Ask. Who is that in the photograph. Why do you always do it this way. What happened last year that everybody keeps mentioning.

People love explaining their group's history, it gives them a role, and asking marks you as interested rather than clueless. It also gets you the information you actually need.

Let the first few conversations be boring

You do not have to be interesting. You have to be present and easy to talk to.

So answer the ordinary questions straightforwardly, ask one back, and let it be unremarkable. The friendships in that room were built out of a hundred unremarkable exchanges, not one impressive one.

And resist the urge to establish your credentials by explaining what you did before, which is a very common new-person move and lands as distance rather than connection.

Notice when it is the group, not you

Most groups are reasonable and a few are closed, and it is worth being able to tell the difference rather than concluding it is you.

Signs it is them: nobody introduces anybody, questions go unanswered, there is an unacknowledged hierarchy, newcomers arrive and leave regularly.

In that case you have learned something useful for the price of a few evenings, and leaving is a decision rather than a failure. There are other choirs.

When to get help

Please talk to a therapist if the dread of walking into rooms like this is keeping you out of things you genuinely want to do, since social anxiety is common, specific and responds well to treatment, often in a short course of sessions. Talk to someone too if the feeling in that room connects to something older, such as having been left out as a child, because that connection is worth understanding rather than fighting in every new room. And see your doctor if avoiding people has been narrowing your life for months, or if the loneliness underneath has pulled your mood down, because both of those are worth proper attention.

If you are in crisis, call or text 988 (Suicide & Crisis Lifeline, US) or your local emergency number. Theraglee is a wellbeing tool, not a substitute for professional care.
