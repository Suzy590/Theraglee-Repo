Fighting the System for Someone You Love

Forty minutes on hold while the care someone needs sits behind it. Where to aim the anger, why writing everything down changes how you are answered, and how to last the distance.

AngerCaregiving Visitor 888 words

You have been on hold for forty minutes. The form was sent and nobody has it. The person who promised to call back has not. And the care that somebody you love actually needs is sitting behind all of it.

The anger you feel is not a problem with you. It is the correct response to being obstructed while someone you love goes without. The question is only what to do with it so that it works for you rather than against you.

Know what the anger is for

Anger exists to get obstacles moved. That is useful here, because you are facing an obstacle and nobody else is going to move it.

What it is not good at is choosing the target. Spent on the receptionist, the nurse on the phone, or the clerk who answered, it tends to close the door you need open, because most of the people you reach have very little power and plenty of ways to make your day longer.

So aim it at the process and keep it off the person in front of you. Cold, specific and relentless beats hot every time.

Write everything down

This is the single most effective habit, and it is the one most people start too late.

Keep one notebook or one file. Every call: the date, the time, who you spoke to, what they said, what they promised, and by when. Every letter and email in one folder.

Then use it out loud. On the fourteenth of this month, Jane told me the referral was sent. Can you tell me where it is. A record turns a disagreement about what happened into a fact, and it changes how you are answered.

Ask for things in a form they cannot lose

Verbal answers evaporate. Written answers get acted on.

So after a call, email a short summary: thank you for your time, my understanding is that you will do X by Y, please correct me if that is wrong. Ask for decisions in writing, with the reason. Ask who is responsible by name and what their timeline is.

Ask the two questions that unlock most things: what is the next step, and what is this called. Knowing the name of the form, the team or the process is often what lets you ask for it properly.

Find the lever rather than pushing harder

Repeating the same request to the same person is where most of the exhaustion goes.

So go sideways. Ask who makes this decision. Ask for a supervisor politely and specifically. Ask whether there is a complaints process and what the timescale is. Ask about an appeal and the deadline for it, and mind that deadline, because missing it costs more than any argument.

Then find the people whose job this is. A patient advocate, a case manager, a social worker, a charity that works on this exact condition, a benefits adviser, your elected representative's office. They know the names of things, and they have made this journey before.

A single clear letter to the right office tends to do more than ten calls to the wrong one.

Protect your own supply

This can go on for months, and the version of you that is doing it in month six matters.

So set hours. Make the calls between two and four, not at ten at night, and not in the gaps of a working day where they eat everything. Have somebody else take a shift if there is anyone at all. Accept slower progress in exchange for still being standing.

Watch for what carrying this does to your body and your sleep. Long fights are physically wearing, and people routinely arrive at the end of one in worse shape than the person they were fighting for.

And say it plainly to someone. Not the detail, which nobody can follow, but the weight. I am so angry and so tired, and I cannot stop.

Hold on to what is actually yours

The hardest part is that you cannot guarantee the outcome. You can be organized, persistent and right, and still be refused by a system that has its own reasons.

So measure yourself by what is in your hands. You asked. You wrote it down. You appealed. You found the advocate. You did not let it go quietly.

That is the whole job, and it is a great deal. Whatever the answer turns out to be, the person you love had someone making noise for them, which is more than many people get.

When to get help

Please talk to your doctor if this fight has left you not sleeping, permanently on edge, or low for weeks, because the toll of advocating for someone is a real one and it is worth naming in an appointment rather than absorbing. A therapist can help with the anger when it starts coming out at home, or at the people who did not deserve it, which is common and does not make you a bad person. For the fight itself, a patient advocate, a case manager, a social worker or a charity that works in this area is practical help rather than sympathy, and asking for one of them earlier rather than later is the thing most people in your position say they wish they had done.
