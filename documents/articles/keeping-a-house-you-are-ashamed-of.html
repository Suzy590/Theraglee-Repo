Keeping a House You Are Ashamed Of

It got away from you, and now nobody can come in. Why the standard you hold makes starting impossible, and how to get a room back without a transformation.

PerfectionismShame Visitor 932 words

The dishes are from more than one day. There is a chair nobody has sat on in months because of what is piled on it. You have stopped letting anyone past the hallway, and when someone suggests coming over you hear yourself inventing a reason.

And you know how to clean. That is the confusing part. You are not confused about where things go. Something else is in the way.

Perfectionism is usually what is in the way

This sounds backward until you see it from inside. The person whose house has got away from them very often has an extremely high standard, not a low one.

If the only version of cleaning that counts is the whole house, properly, sorted and wiped and finished, then there is no point starting with twenty minutes. Twenty minutes cannot produce the acceptable outcome, so twenty minutes feels like nothing, so it does not happen. The standard is what makes the work impossible, and then the mess grows, and then the standard feels further away, and so on.

The shame does the rest

Once the house is past a certain point, it stops being a chore and becomes a statement about you. Lazy. Disgusting. Not coping. An adult who cannot manage what everyone else manages.

Shame is a terrible motivator. It makes people avoid the thing rather than approach it, so the hours you spend feeling bad about the kitchen are hours taken from the kitchen. And it isolates you, which removes the one thing that would help most, which is another pair of hands or even just company.

If this started with something

For many people the house got away during something: an illness, a new baby, a bereavement, depression, a stretch of working impossible hours, a chronic pain problem, a separation. The mess is the residue of a hard time, not evidence of a character defect, and it is worth saying that to yourself in those words.

Low mood, pain and exhaustion all specifically remove the energy for maintenance, which is why the house is often the first visible sign and the last thing to come back.

Start much smaller than makes sense

The useful unit is not a room. It is a surface, or a bag, or ten minutes.

One kitchen counter. The clothes on the chair, not the closet. One trash bag of obvious garbage, not sorting. The path from the door to the sofa. Ten minutes with a timer, then stop even if you want to keep going, because stopping while it still feels possible is what makes tomorrow likelier.

Pick the thing you will see most. A cleared counter you look at forty times a day does more for your sense of possibility than a sorted closet nobody opens.

Separate the three jobs

Most stuck houses mix up three different tasks, which makes all of them harder.

Garbage is garbage: it needs no decisions, so it is the best place to start. Things that live somewhere just need carrying there, which is not sorting. Decisions, the things you do not know what to do with, are the slowest part, and they are what stalls people, so give them their own box and their own separate time rather than letting them halt the clearing.

Lower the standard on purpose

Decide, deliberately, what good enough looks like on an ordinary week: clear floors, clean kitchen, bathroom usable, laundry moving. Not photographic. The aim is a house that works, not one that would survive inspection.

Dishwasher or dishpan rather than hand-washing everything. Laundry in and out even unfolded. One drawer that is allowed to be chaos. Lowering the bar is not giving up. It is the only way a house gets maintained by a real person with a job and a life.

Let one person in

This is the hardest suggestion and the one that changes things fastest. Tell one person the truth. It has got on top of me and I am embarrassed about it.

Almost everyone has had a version of this, and most people respond with their own confession rather than judgment. A friend who comes and sits and talks while you fill bags will get more done in an afternoon than three weeks of resolve, because company breaks the shame that was keeping you still.

Paid help, if it is possible, is worth considering for the first push rather than the ongoing upkeep. Getting over the hump is the expensive part.

Watch for the things that need more than tidying

Take it further if you cannot use your kitchen or bathroom, if the amount of stuff means rooms cannot be used at all, if you cannot discard things even when you want to, if there is anything rotting or a problem with pests, or if the state of the house is affecting a child's or an older person's safety.

Those are not failures of effort. They are specific situations with specific help, and they do not get better by trying harder in private.

When to get help

Please talk to your doctor if this came with low mood, pain, exhaustion or a life event you have not recovered from, because the house tends to follow those rather than cause them. If the difficulty is parting with things rather than energy, say that plainly to a doctor or therapist, since there are treatments aimed exactly at it. A therapist can also help with the perfectionism and the shame, which is usually where the lock is. You do not have to have a tidy house to deserve help with this.

If you are in crisis, call or text 988 (Suicide & Crisis Lifeline, US) or your local emergency number. Theraglee is a wellbeing tool, not a substitute for professional care.
