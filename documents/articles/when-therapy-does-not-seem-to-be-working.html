When Therapy Does Not Seem to Be Working

Eight sessions in and you are not sure anything has changed. How to tell slow progress from the wrong fit, and what to say before you quietly stop going.

CBTDecision Making Visitor 874 words

You have been going for two months. You like them, more or less. You talk for fifty minutes, you feel a bit lighter afterward, and by Tuesday you are back where you were.

And the thought has started arriving on the way there: is this doing anything, or am I paying to be listened to?

First, check what you are measuring

Progress in therapy rarely looks like feeling better every week. Quite often the first few weeks are worse, because you are deliberately turning toward things you have been avoiding.

The better questions are behavioral. Am I doing anything I was not doing two months ago? Does a bad day last two days rather than a week? Am I catching the pattern earlier, even if I still do it? Has one relationship or one habit shifted slightly?

Those are the signs that tend to come first, and they are easy to miss because they are not dramatic.

If you cannot answer any of them, that is worth taking seriously rather than waiting another two months.

Know roughly what you should expect by when

This depends a great deal on the kind of therapy and what you brought.

For a focused problem — panic, a specific fear, insomnia, low mood with clear patterns — the structured approaches usually show something by six to eight sessions. Not finished, but moving.

For longer-standing things — how you relate to people, something that happened years ago, who you are after a loss — it is slower, and the early work is often about trust rather than change.

What matters is that you were told which one you are in. If nobody has ever described a plan, that is a reasonable thing to ask about.

Say it in the room

This is the single most useful thing you can do, and almost nobody does it.

Tell them plainly: I am not sure this is working. I do not feel any different. Can we look at what we are doing?

A good therapist will not be hurt by that. It is one of the most productive conversations available, and it often changes the direction entirely: a clearer focus, a different approach, something to do between sessions, or a frank discussion about what is in the way.

If raising it feels impossible, notice that too, and consider telling them that instead: there is something I find hard to say to you.

Look at the possible reasons honestly

Sometimes the work is not landing because of the fit: a style that does not suit you, a therapist who mostly nods when you want structure, or someone who hands you techniques when you need to be heard first.

Sometimes it is the method. Talking about a trauma without a specific approach to it, or trying to think your way out of something that needs doing rather than discussing, can go round for a long time.

Sometimes it is what happens between sessions. Therapy that lives only in the hour tends to stay there; most of the change happens in the practicing.

And sometimes it is that something else needs attention first: sleep that has collapsed, drinking, a dangerous situation, untreated physical illness, or a mood low enough that a doctor should be involved.

What to do with each of those

If it is the fit, you are allowed to change therapist, and you do not need to justify it beyond saying it is not the right match. Good practitioners refer people on without taking offense.

If it is the method, ask directly: is there an approach designed for this particular thing? There usually is, and it is a fair question to put to a professional.

If it is the between-sessions part, ask for homework and actually do it. The people who get the most out of therapy are generally the ones doing something on Wednesday as well as Monday.

If something else needs treating first, say so to a doctor. Therapy on top of a collapsed foundation is hard work for small returns.

Before you quietly stop

Ending by not booking the next appointment is the most common way therapy finishes and the least useful.

Have one last session on purpose and say what did not work. You will learn something about what to look for next time, and so will they.

And if you are stopping because it brought something up that you would rather not look at, that is worth naming out loud before you go. That moment is often where the actual work was about to start.

When to get help beyond the room

Please talk to a doctor if your mood has got worse rather than better, if sleep or appetite have gone, if you are drinking more, or if you have been in therapy for months with nothing shifting at all; there may be a different diagnosis, a physical cause, or a medication question in it.

It is also entirely reasonable to ask for a second opinion, a different service, or a referral to someone who specializes in your particular thing. Therapy is a treatment rather than a test of character, and a professional who cannot help you with this should be helping you find someone who can.

If you are in crisis, call or text 988 (Suicide & Crisis Lifeline, US) or your local emergency number. Theraglee is a wellbeing tool, not a substitute for professional care.
