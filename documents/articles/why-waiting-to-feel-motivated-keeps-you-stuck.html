Why Waiting to Feel Motivated Keeps You Stuck

Most of us assume motivation comes first and action follows. Usually it is the other way around. How to start when you do not feel like it, and why that gets easier.

MotivationHabits Visitor 938 words

There is a story most of us believe about how things get done. First you feel motivated. Then you act. The motivation is the fuel, the action is the car, and if the tank is empty there is nothing to do but wait for a delivery.

The trouble is that the delivery rarely comes. You wait to feel like going to the gym, and the feeling does not arrive. You wait to feel ready to make the phone call, and days pass. Meanwhile the thing you are not doing gets bigger in your mind, and the less you feel like doing it, and the story seems more true every day.

Here is a different story, and it happens to be more accurate. Action usually comes first. Motivation follows.

How it actually works

Think about the last time you did not want to do something and did it anyway. Maybe you dragged yourself to a friend's party, or forced yourself to start a task at work. Notice what happened once you were in it. Often, five or ten minutes in, it was fine. Sometimes it was even good. The dread was worse than the thing.

That is not a coincidence. When you act, your brain gets new information. The task is not as bad as it looked. You are more capable than you felt. Something is getting done. Each piece of information gives you a little energy, and that energy is what we call motivation. It is a product of the action, not a precondition for it.

Waiting to feel like it, on the other hand, gives your brain nothing new. You stay in the same room with the same dread, and dread grows in the dark.

The wall is at the start

If motivation comes after action, the hard part is obviously the start. So make the start absurdly small.

Not, go for a run. Instead, put on your running shoes. Not, clean the kitchen. Instead, put one dish in the dishwasher. Not, write the report. Instead, open the document and type the title.

This can feel like cheating, and that is exactly the point. The goal is not to accomplish the task with the small step. The goal is to get past the wall at the front of it, because once you are past the wall, the rest is easier than you think. You may put on the shoes and not run. That is fine. Most days, though, the shoes lead to the door, and the door leads to the sidewalk, and the sidewalk leads to a run you did not feel like taking.

Shrink the time, not just the task

Another version of the same idea is to make a deal with yourself about time. Ten minutes. You will work on the thing for ten minutes, and then you are free to stop, guilt-free, no questions asked.

The deal only works if you keep it. If you promise ten minutes and then guilt yourself into an hour, your brain learns that ten minutes is a trick, and the next time it will refuse. So really stop if you want to at ten. What you will find is that you often do not want to, because by then you are in motion and stopping feels harder than continuing.

Plan the when and where

Vague intentions die quietly. I will exercise more. I should call her sometime. These have no handle to grab.

Give them one. I will walk after lunch on Tuesday. I will call her Saturday morning after coffee. Naming a time and a place does something useful: it moves the decision from the moment you least want to make it to a moment when you are calm. When Saturday morning comes, you do not have to decide. You already decided. You just have to follow through, which is a much smaller ask.

Expect the resistance

The feeling of not wanting to do something is not a signal that something is wrong with you or with the plan. It is just what starting feels like. Once you know that, you can stop reading it as a verdict. The resistance says, I do not feel like it. You can answer, I know. We are doing it anyway. Then do the smallest possible piece.

Some days even the smallest piece will not happen, and that is okay. Missing a day is not the end of a habit. Deciding that missing a day means you have failed is what ends the habit. Just start again with the next small step.

Notice what you did

Motivation grows on evidence. So give yourself the evidence. When you finish the ten minutes, or the one dish, or the one call, take a second to notice that you did it. Not a celebration, just a plain acknowledgment: that was hard to start, and I started it.

Over weeks, that record adds up. You become someone who does things whether or not they feel like it, and, oddly, that is when you start feeling like it more often. The tank fills from the driving.

When it is more than a slump

Sometimes the lack of motivation is not a habit problem. If nothing has felt worth doing for weeks, if you have stopped caring about things you used to love, if getting out of bed feels like lifting a car, that can be a sign of something heavier than a rough patch. Please talk to a therapist or your doctor. Small steps still help, but you deserve support in taking them, and a professional can help you figure out what is going on underneath.
