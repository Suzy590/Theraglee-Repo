Why Anxiety Makes Your Body Feel Ill

Chest tightness, dizziness, a stomach that will not settle, tingling hands. What anxiety actually does to the body, and why checking with a doctor is still the right move.

PanicBody Visitor 918 words

Your chest is tight. Your stomach has not been right for weeks. Your hands tingle, your head feels swimmy, and you are tired in a way sleep does not touch.

Somebody has probably told you it is just anxiety, which is one of the least useful sentences in medicine. It sounds like being told you are imagining it, and you are not. The sensations are real, physical, and produced by something your body is actually doing.

Here is what that is, and why it is still worth getting checked.

The alarm system runs the body, not just the mind

Anxiety is a whole-body response, not a mood. When your system reads a threat, it prepares you to fight or run, and that preparation is physical.

Adrenaline and cortisol go out. Your heart beats faster and harder. Breathing gets quicker and shallower. Muscles tense, ready to move. Blood is routed to the big muscles and away from your stomach and skin. Digestion slows down. Your senses sharpen.

That whole package is useful if you have to run. It is deeply unpleasant if you are sitting at a desk, and even more so if it has been running at a low level for months.

What the common symptoms actually are

Tight chest and breathlessness: the muscles across your chest are tense and your breathing has gone shallow and fast. Faster breathing also changes the balance of gases in your blood, which produces much of the rest of this list.

Tingling hands and face, light-headedness, a sense of unreality: these are classic effects of over-breathing, not signs of anything damaged. They can be reproduced in anyone in about a minute of fast breathing.

A stomach that will not behave: digestion is downregulated when you are in alarm mode, and the gut has a dense nerve supply that responds quickly to stress. Nausea, cramping, urgency and appetite changes are extremely common.

Aches, headaches, a sore jaw, a stiff neck: held tension, often all day, often without noticing. Jaw and shoulders are the usual sites.

Exhaustion: running an alarm system continuously is metabolically expensive, and anxiety fragments sleep even when you are in bed for eight hours.

Heart flutters and skipped beats: very common with adrenaline and caffeine, and worth mentioning to a doctor rather than assuming.

Why it keeps going round

Here is the loop that makes this so persistent: the symptom becomes the threat.

You notice your heart. You wonder if something is wrong with it. That wondering raises adrenaline, which raises your heart rate, which gives you more to notice. The same runs with chest tightness, dizziness and swallowing.

Checking feeds it too. Taking your pulse, searching symptoms, pressing on the sore spot, asking for reassurance: each one briefly settles and then sharpens the attention that keeps it running.

That loop is a large part of why physical anxiety symptoms outlast the stressful thing that started them.

Get it checked anyway

This is important and often missed in the rush to call something anxiety.

Several physical conditions produce exactly these sensations: thyroid problems, anemia, heart rhythm disturbances, asthma, diabetes, inner-ear problems, vitamin deficiencies, and the effects of medication, alcohol, nicotine and caffeine.

So see a doctor and describe it plainly. Getting a basic check is not a failure of nerve; it is how you rule things out, and it is also what lets you stop wondering, which is itself part of the treatment.

Treat some symptoms as urgent rather than familiar. Chest pain spreading to the arm or jaw, pain with sweating or vomiting, sudden severe breathlessness, fainting, weakness or drooping on one side, a sudden worst-ever headache, or trouble speaking: those need emergency care, not reassurance from an article.

What helps the body side

Slow the out-breath. Four in, six or seven out, for a few minutes, several times a day rather than only in a crisis. This is the most direct lever on the whole list above.

Unclench on purpose. Several times a day, drop your shoulders, unclench your jaw, let your hands go loose. Held tension is a habit and it responds to being interrupted.

Move most days. Movement spends the chemistry the alarm keeps producing, and it reliably lowers the background level.

Cut caffeine right back for two weeks and see. Caffeine produces a very large share of the heart flutters, jitteriness and stomach trouble people attribute to anxiety.

Protect sleep, and treat alcohol with suspicion: it settles things for an hour and fragments the second half of the night, which makes the next day worse.

And stop the checking where you can. Not pressing on it, not measuring it, not searching it. Leaving a symptom alone is the thing that lets it fade.

When to get proper help

Physical anxiety responds well to treatment, often in a short course of therapy aimed at exactly this loop, and sometimes with medication a doctor can discuss with you.

If this has been going on for weeks, if you are avoiding places or activities because of how your body feels, if panic attacks are arriving regularly, or if the fear that something is seriously wrong will not settle even after tests have come back clear, please take it to a doctor or a therapist rather than managing it alone.

And if things have got dark enough that you are not sure you want to be here, tell a doctor or someone you trust that same day. In the US you can call or text 988 at any time.

If you are in crisis, call or text 988 (Suicide & Crisis Lifeline, US) or your local emergency number. Theraglee is a wellbeing tool, not a substitute for professional care.
