Sending It Before It Feels Ready

The last ten percent of polish costs more than it returns, and the deadline passes anyway. How to tell real improvement from circling, and how to let something go on purpose.

PerfectionismDecision Making Visitor 762 words

The thing is basically done. It has been basically done for three days. You keep opening it, changing a sentence, moving a section, reading it through again, and it is not getting better, it is just getting more handled.

Meanwhile the actual cost is accruing somewhere else: the opportunity is passing, the other people are waiting, and the hours are coming out of everything else you had to do.

Where the extra effort goes

Early work is cheap and improves things fast. The first pass fixes real problems.

Then the returns flatten. Somewhere past the point where it is clearly good enough, each additional hour buys a change nobody but you will notice, and after that you start making lateral moves, swapping one acceptable phrasing for another acceptable phrasing.

The difficulty is that from inside, lateral motion feels exactly like progress. You are working. Things are changing. It is just that the thing is not getting better.

What the circling is usually about

Not standards. People with genuinely high standards ship things, because they can tell when something has met the standard.

The circling is more often about the moment of release. While it is unfinished it cannot be judged, and you are still the person who could have made it better. The second you send it, it stops being potential and becomes a thing with a verdict attached.

So polishing is a way of staying in the safe part. That is worth knowing, because if the problem is the release rather than the quality, no amount of further work will produce the feeling of readiness you are waiting for. It does not arrive.

Telling real improvement from circling

A few questions sort it quickly.

Can you name what is wrong with it. If you can state a specific defect, fix that. If the answer is a general unease, you are circling.

Would a reasonable person notice this change. Not you on the fourth read. Someone encountering it once.

Are you making changes or alternating between versions. If you have swapped the same paragraph back twice, that is the signal.

And what is the actual cost of being slightly less polished, said out loud and specifically. Usually it is small. Occasionally it is large, and knowing which is the point of asking.

Letting it go on purpose

Decide the endpoint before you start, not when you are tired. One more read for errors, then it goes. Writing that down beforehand is what makes it hold.

Set a time rather than a standard. This goes Thursday at four is enforceable. This goes when it is ready is not, because ready is the thing you cannot assess.

Do one defined last pass with a narrow brief. Spelling, numbers, names, broken links. Not quality. A constrained pass has an end.

Then send it without rereading. The final reread exists to give you one more chance to not send it.

And if letting go in one step is too much, shrink the audience rather than delaying. Send it to one person first. Publish the smaller version. Lowering the stakes works where willpower does not.

Afterward

Expect to feel worse briefly and then considerably better. The dip right after sending is the part people brace against, and it passes faster than the dread predicted.

Resist going back to fix something immediately. Note it for the next version instead. Reopening within the hour is the habit reasserting itself.

And write down what happened, because the prediction and the outcome rarely match. People who keep a short record of what actually followed from sending imperfect work find the evidence builds up quickly on one side.

Over time the useful shift is from how good is this to is this good enough for what it is for. A text to a colleague, a draft, a first version, and a final deliverable have different thresholds, and treating them all as the last one is most of the problem.

When to talk to someone about it

If this is costing you deadlines, promotions, or work you wanted to do, if you have abandoned projects rather than finish them imperfectly, or if the thought of releasing something brings real fear rather than mild reluctance, it is worth taking to a therapist. Perfectionism of this kind is common, well understood and quite responsive to treatment, and the work on it is practical rather than open-ended: not lowering your standards, which are usually worth keeping, but changing what it costs you to let a piece of work be finished, released and judged by other people.
