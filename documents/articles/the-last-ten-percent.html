The Last Ten Percent

The thing is nearly done and has been for weeks. Why finishing is its own distinct problem, separate from starting, and how to get something over the line.

MotivationGoals Visitor 938 words

The draft is written. The room is painted except for the trim. The application is complete apart from one form. The course has two modules left.

Nothing is stopping you. And it has been like this for three weeks, and every day it quietly costs you something.

Finishing is a separate skill from starting, and almost nobody is taught it.

Why the end is harder than the middle

Several things change as you approach the finish, and all of them push against completion.

The work gets less interesting. The last ten percent is nearly always the boring ten percent: the formatting, the trim, the admin, the second coat, the references.

The feedback stops. In the middle you could see progress. At the end you are grinding through small items that make no visible difference.

And, most importantly, finishing makes the thing real. While it is unfinished it is still potentially excellent. Once it is done it becomes a specific object that can be judged, and some part of you knows that.

That last one explains most stalled projects better than laziness does.

Name what finishing exposes

If a nearly complete thing has sat for weeks, it is worth asking plainly: what happens when this is done?

Someone reads it. The room is judged. The application gets an answer, possibly a no. The course ends and you have to decide what is next. People find out whether you are good at this.

Often the stall is not about the remaining work at all; it is a way of staying in the state where nothing has been decided. Naming that does not make it disappear, and it does make it much easier to act anyway.

Write down exactly what is left

A vague remainder expands. A written list shrinks it.

List every actual item, in physical terms, however small: find the receipt, write the covering email, buy a small brush, print one page, email two referees, delete the placeholder paragraph.

Most people discover the real remainder is six items and about ninety minutes, not the vast unspecified thing it felt like. That discovery is usually the turning point.

Then do them in order of size, smallest first, because momentum matters more than logic at this stage.

Define done, in advance

Half of all unfinished things are unfinished because nobody decided what finished means.

So write the definition before you resume: this is done when the trim is painted and the brushes are cleaned. When the form is submitted. When the draft has been read once for typos and sent.

Make it a standard you can actually hit. Done is not perfect; it is the agreed line. And having a line stops the last ten percent from quietly becoming another forty.

Set a deadline with a witness

A deadline you set alone is a preference. A deadline somebody expects is a different thing entirely.

So tell one person: I am sending this on Thursday. Book the appointment that requires the form. Invite someone to see the room on Saturday. Ask a colleague to expect the draft.

If nobody is available, make it costly in some other way: buy the tickets, book the collection, schedule the thing that only works if this is done.

External structure is not a weakness; it is the normal way adults finish things.

Use one unglamorous session

Set aside one block, ninety minutes, with the list in front of you, and do the remaining items badly rather than well.

The last ten percent rarely rewards care. The references do not need to be beautiful. The email does not need a fourth draft. The trim needs one decent coat.

If you catch yourself improving something that was already finished, stop; that is avoidance dressed as craft. Polishing the parts you enjoy while the admin sits undone is the most common way the end of a project dies.

Expect the flatness afterward

Finishing something often does not feel the way people expect. There is a drop: relief, then a strange emptiness, sometimes a day or two of feeling at a loose end.

That is normal, and it is one reason people avoid finishing things without knowing it. The unfinished project was also a companion and a source of identity.

So plan the hour after. Tell someone. Do something ordinary and pleasant. And do not immediately start the next large thing out of discomfort with the gap.

When the thing genuinely should not be finished

Sometimes the stall is information. The project no longer matters, the situation changed, or you have learned something that makes the original plan wrong.

That is a legitimate answer and it deserves a decision rather than a drift. Either finish it to the agreed line, or end it deliberately and say so, which costs far less than another year of a nearly-done thing sitting there.

What is not a legitimate answer is leaving it at ninety percent indefinitely, because that keeps all of the cost and delivers none of the benefit.

When this is a pattern rather than a project

If almost nothing gets finished, if you have a long history of things abandoned near the end, or if the dread around completion is strong enough to keep you from work you care about, that is worth looking at with someone.

Fear of being judged, perfectionism, and the ways attention and follow-through work differently for some people all produce exactly this, and all of them are workable. A doctor or a therapist is the right place to start, especially if the pile of unfinished things has begun to feel like evidence about who you are.
