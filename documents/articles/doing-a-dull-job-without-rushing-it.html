Doing a Dull Job Without Rushing It

The dishes, the laundry, the same drive again. Why hurrying through them costs more than it saves, how to do one of them with nothing playing, and what tends to surface when you do.

MindfulnessSelf-Care Visitor 855 words

There is a sink full of dishes, a basket of laundry, and a twenty-minute drive you have done four hundred times. None of it is interesting. All of it has to happen.

So you do it the way most of us do: as fast as possible, with a podcast in, while thinking about something else, hoping to get to the bit of the evening that counts. And then the evening arrives and you are oddly depleted, having rushed through an hour you do not remember.

The hidden cost of rushing

Hurrying through something does not save you much. The dishes take eleven minutes instead of fourteen, and you spend those eleven minutes slightly braced, resisting what you are doing.

That resistance is the expensive part. A whole day of lightly fighting the things you are doing is more tiring than the things themselves.

There is also the arithmetic nobody likes. These jobs are not an interruption to your life, they are a good few hours of every week of it. Treating them all as time to be gotten through means writing off a serious amount of your actual life.

Do one thing without a soundtrack

Not all of them. One.

Pick a single routine job, a few times a week, and do it with nothing playing. No podcast, no phone propped up, no radio. The washing up, the walk to the bus, folding the laundry, the ten minutes of tidying before bed.

It will feel boring at first, and boring is the point, because boredom is what you have been covering over. It usually settles into something else within a couple of minutes.

Put your attention on the thing itself

This is where it stops being a chore and starts being useful, and it is simpler than meditation advice usually makes it sound.

Use your hands and your senses. The temperature of the water. The weight of a wet towel. The smell of the clean laundry. The specific noise a knife makes going into the rack.

When your mind wanders, which it will constantly, bring it back to the sensation. Not with annoyance, just back. That is the entire practice, and the returning is the skill rather than the staying.

Slow down about ten percent. Not reverently slowly, just a little under your usual speed, which is enough to make the difference noticeable.

Expect your mind to object

For the first few minutes you may feel restless, irritated, or suddenly keen to check something. That is normal and it is information.

Often what surfaces is whatever you were avoiding by keeping the noise on. A worry, a conversation you did not like, a flat feeling. People sometimes discover that the podcast was doing more work than they realized.

If that happens, the answer is not to force it. Finish the job, notice what came up, and decide later whether it needs attention. You are not obliged to sit in silence with something difficult just because you turned the radio off.

Why it is worth the trouble

Two reasons, both practical.

The first is that it gives you a small amount of time in the day that is not spent somewhere else. Most people's attention is in the past or the future almost all day, and bringing it into a sink of water for ten minutes is genuinely restful in a way that scrolling is not.

The second is that it is training. Attention is a bit like strength, in that it responds to use. Practicing coming back to a dull thing is practice for coming back to a conversation, a piece of work, or a difficult evening.

And there is a third, less measurable one. People who do this often report liking their house more, and their life slightly more, which is a strange return on washing a pan with your full attention.

Keep it unambitious

This goes wrong when it becomes a project. You decide to do everything mindfully, fail by Tuesday, and conclude you are bad at it.

So keep it to one job, a few times a week. Make it the same job, so it becomes automatic. Let the rest of your chores be done any way you like, podcast and all.

And drop the idea of doing it well. There is no well. There is paying attention, noticing you have stopped, and coming back, which is all anybody does.

When to get help

Please talk to your doctor or a therapist if the reason you cannot be alone with a quiet task is that what arrives in the silence is too much, such as intrusive memories, a worry you cannot put down, or a low mood that has lasted weeks, because that is worth proper attention rather than being drowned out. If you have been through something frightening, quiet attention to your body can sometimes be unsettling rather than calming, and a professional who works with trauma can help you find a version that suits you. And if ordinary tasks have become overwhelming rather than just dull, say so plainly at an appointment, since that is a change worth looking at.
