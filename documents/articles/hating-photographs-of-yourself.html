Hating Photographs of Yourself

A photo comes up and your stomach drops. Why a picture lands so much harder than a mirror, what the checking afterward does, and how to stay in the pictures anyway.

Self-CompassionBody Visitor 939 words

Someone sends the photos from the weekend. You scroll to the one with you in it and your stomach drops, and then you spend ten minutes on it: the angle, the arms, the face you were apparently making.

By the end you are not thinking about the weekend at all. You are thinking about whether that is what everyone sees.

Why a photo lands harder than a mirror

A mirror is familiar and a photograph is not, and most of the shock is in that gap.

In a mirror you see yourself reversed, moving, from your own height, with your face arranged. A photo is a single frozen hundredth of a second, usually from someone else's angle, often from below, in whatever light was there, with a lens that flattens or stretches depending on how close it was.

It is not more accurate than the mirror. It is a different distortion, and an unfamiliar one, so it reads as the truth you had been hiding from yourself.

Add that you are the only person in the photo you examine. Everyone else gets a glance.

What you are comparing it to

Almost nobody compares a photo of themselves to other real photos. They compare it to pictures that have been chosen, posed, lit and smoothed, including the ones their friends post.

You are also comparing it to a version of yourself that updates slowly. Most people carry a mental picture that is a few years old, sometimes decades, and a current photograph argues with it. That stings in a way that has very little to do with how you actually look.

And mood changes the whole thing. The same photo on a flat, tired day looks worse than it does on a good one, which is a sign you are seeing your mood rather than your face.

The checking is what makes it stick

What people do after a bad photo is the part that does the damage.

Zooming in. Comparing it with an old picture. Looking in the mirror to check. Turning sideways. Asking someone whether it is accurate, then not believing the answer. Deleting every copy and then finding it again.

All of that narrows your attention down to the part you dislike, which makes it bigger and more distressing every time you look. Checking never produces reassurance that lasts, and the relief it gives is what keeps you doing it.

The useful move is to put the phone down and leave the room. Not forever, just now, before the twenty minutes happens.

What to do in the moment

Name what is happening plainly: I am having a bad reaction to a photograph. That sentence is small and it puts a little distance in.

Then widen your attention on purpose. Look at the whole picture rather than the one part: where you were, who was there, what you were laughing about. The photo is a record of a day, and you have been reading it as a verdict on your body.

Do something with your hands and your body for a few minutes, because this is a feeling that fades much faster when you move than when you sit with your phone.

And leave the deciding about the photo for later. Whether it gets posted, kept or deleted is a question for tomorrow, not for the ten minutes when your stomach is in your shoes.

Be in the pictures anyway

The avoidance is understandable and it has a real cost.

People who dislike photographs of themselves tend to stop being in them, which means years go by with no record of them with the people they love. Then they look back and the absence is the thing that hurts, not the arms or the chin.

Children in particular do not look at a photo of a parent and assess their body. They look for the person who was there.

So be in them, badly, often. The more ordinary pictures of yourself you have, the less power any single one has, partly because familiarity is what takes the shock out.

Change what you say out loud

Many people narrate their own appearance constantly in company, as a joke or a pre-emptive apology, and it does two things.

It rehearses the belief, so you hear your own worst line about yourself several times a day. And it teaches the people around you, including children, that this is how a person talks about their own body.

You do not have to swap it for compliments you do not believe. Dropping the commentary is enough. If something has to go in its place, make it factual and about the day: that was a good afternoon, I like that we are all in it.

When it is bigger than a photograph

If the photos are keeping you out of your own life, if you cannot be in a picture at all, or if you are spending a long time every day checking, comparing, or planning what to do about your body, that is worth taking to someone.

Please talk to a doctor or a therapist if how you feel about your body is driving what you eat, whether you leave the house, or whether you see people, or if it has been getting louder rather than quieter. Those feelings respond well to treatment, and the people who work with them hear this every day and will not find it vain or silly.

And if it has become dark enough that you are not sure you want to be here, please tell a doctor or someone you trust today rather than carrying it alone.
