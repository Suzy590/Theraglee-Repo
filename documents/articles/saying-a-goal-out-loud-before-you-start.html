Saying a Goal Out Loud Before You Start

Telling people what you are about to attempt can carry you or sink you, and which one depends on how you say it. What announcing does, who to tell, and what to say instead of the headline.

GoalsMotivation Visitor 891 words

You are about to start something. A course, a change at work, running again, finally dealing with the money. And there is a question underneath it that nobody talks about, which is whether to tell anyone.

Say it out loud and you have witnesses. Keep it quiet and you have an exit. Both have a real cost, and most people pick by temperament rather than by thinking about it.

What announcing actually does

It helps in one specific way and hurts in another, and knowing which is which is most of the decision.

It makes the thing real. A plan that exists only in your head stays negotiable, and saying it to another person moves it into the world where it has a shape.

It recruits help you would not otherwise get. People who know what you are doing make room for it, ask about it, and occasionally hand you exactly what you needed.

But it also pays you early. Being told that sounds great feels remarkably similar to having done something, and that small reward arrives before any of the work. For a lot of people that is enough to take the edge off the wanting.

And it adds an audience to the failing. A quiet change you abandon costs you nothing. An announced one you abandon comes with explaining, which is why people keep going with things they should have stopped.

Who to tell, which matters more than whether

The useful question is not whether to say it, but to whom, and most people get this backwards by telling the widest audience and the least useful one.

Tell one or two people who will actually be affected. The person you live with, the one who covers for you, the friend whose Saturday it changes. That is practical, not performative.

Tell someone who has done the thing. They will ask better questions than anyone else and will not be impressed by the announcement, which is the point.

Be careful with a broad audience. A post to everyone you know is the version that pays you most and helps you least, and it is the one that makes quitting expensive without making doing it any easier.

And be careful with anyone who will keep score. Some people turn a mentioned intention into a running question, and the pressure that creates tends to push people into hiding rather than into working.

What to say instead of the headline

How you word it changes what it does to you, more than people expect.

Say the next action, not the outcome. I am going to the first class on Tuesday is better than I am learning Spanish, because it is checkable, small, and does not cash in the whole ambition in one sentence.

Say it as a trial. I am trying this for six weeks. That gives you an honest exit that is not a failure, which removes most of the reason to overclaim in the first place.

Name what would help. I would find it easier if we did not plan anything on Thursday evenings. People generally want to help and almost never know how.

And leave the identity claims out. I am a runner now is a bigger thing to take back than I am running on Tuesdays, and the bigger the claim, the worse the abandoning feels.

If you would rather say nothing

That is a perfectly good choice, and it suits a lot of people better than the alternative.

Working in private removes the performance, and some things genuinely go better unobserved, particularly anything you expect to be bad at for a while.

The thing to replace it with is a record rather than an audience. A line in a notebook, a mark on a calendar, something that holds the commitment without another person in it.

And tell one person at the point you need something from them. Private does not have to mean secret, and most people only need an audience when they need help.

Afterward, whichever way you went

If it went well, notice that the result came from the work rather than from the announcement, which is worth knowing for next time.

If you stopped, say so plainly once, to the people you told. I am not doing that anymore. That sentence takes ten seconds and prevents months of being asked about it.

And if the stopping came with a verdict about yourself, separate the two. Abandoning a particular plan is information about the plan, the timing, or what else was happening, and it is almost never the summary of a person that it feels like at the time.

When the pattern is the thing to look at

Starting things and dropping them is ordinary, and most people do it more than they remember.

It is worth taking further if the starting and stopping has become a cycle that leaves you worse each time, if you cannot begin anything without first telling everybody, or if the gap between what you announce and what you do has started to feel like dishonesty.

A therapist can help with what is underneath that, and it usually turns out to be about what you believe the goal would prove rather than the goal itself. Your doctor can point you toward what is available if you are not sure where to start.
