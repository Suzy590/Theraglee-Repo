When an Apology Is Offered and You Are Not Ready

Somebody says sorry and you feel nothing, or you feel worse. Why an apology can arrive before you can use it, what you are allowed to say back, and what actually has to happen first.

AngerRelationships Visitor 818 words

They said it. Properly, apparently sincerely, maybe even well. And instead of the release you were supposed to feel, there is a flatness, or a spike of something hotter, and then guilt about that, because you are now the one holding it up.

An apology arriving does not mean you are ready to receive it. Those are two separate events and they are often weeks or years apart.

Why the timing is so often wrong

The apology is usually built around the other person's readiness rather than yours.

They have been working up to it. By the time they say it they have been through the whole thing privately and arrived somewhere. You are hearing it cold, at a moment they chose.

It can reopen rather than close. Being told in detail what was done to you is a reminder as much as a repair, and the first feeling is frequently the original injury rather than relief.

And it changes the social position instantly. The moment somebody apologizes, the pressure moves to you, and a lot of people accept an apology they have not absorbed simply to get out from under that.

What an apology can and cannot do

Separating these takes most of the confusion out of it.

It can tell you that they know. That is worth a great deal, particularly if you spent a long time wondering whether they understood at all.

It can establish a record. What happened is now agreed between you rather than disputed, which is the part people most often need.

It cannot undo the thing, and it cannot make the feeling stop on a schedule. Those are not what it is for, and expecting them is why people conclude the apology failed.

And it does not, by itself, make them safe. Whether they will do it again is a separate question answered by what happens next, not by what was said.

What you are allowed to say back

More than people think, and the options are better than the two most of us know about.

Thank you for saying that, and I am not there yet. That is a complete answer. It accepts the apology as an act without pretending to a feeling you do not have.

I need to think about it. Also complete. Nobody is owed an immediate verdict on something that took them months to prepare.

I believe you mean it and I am still angry. Both halves of that are true at once more often than not, and saying so is more honest than the version where you file the anger away and it comes out sideways in three weeks.

And if it was not actually an apology, you can say that too. I would rather you did not explain why you did it. A lot of what arrives as an apology is mostly justification, and you are allowed to decline the justification part.

What usually has to happen first

If you are waiting to feel something and it is not coming, it is often one of these.

A change, not a sentence. For most people the readiness arrives when they have seen different behavior for long enough to trust it, and no amount of wording substitutes.

The full account. Half the hurt is frequently in a part they have not mentioned, and an apology aimed at the wrong thing cannot land.

Time that is not negotiable. Some of this runs on its own clock, and being pushed makes it longer rather than shorter.

And occasionally, nothing. Sometimes you will not get there, and that is allowed. Not every relationship resumes, and deciding that is a legitimate outcome rather than a failure of generosity.

If they push

Which happens, and is worth having an answer ready for.

Name the move without a fight. I understand you want this settled, and it is not settled yet.

Watch for the version where their discomfort becomes your task. Someone who apologizes and then needs reassurance has handed you a second job, and noticing that is not cynicism.

And hold the line on the timing, which is the one piece that is genuinely yours. They chose when to say it. You get to choose when, or whether, you answer.

When it is sitting heavier than it should

If this has you going over the event constantly, if the anger is spilling into parts of your life that had nothing to do with it, or if you are being pressed to be finished with something you are not finished with, that is worth bringing to someone outside it.

A therapist can help with the part that is stuck, and the work is usually practical: what you actually need in order to put it down, which is often something you have never said out loud. Your doctor can point you to what is available if you are not sure where to start.
