Walking Into an Interview

Your mind empties, your voice shakes, and you leave sure you ruined it. What interviewers are actually doing, how to prepare in a way that holds under pressure, and how to steady yourself in the room.

Social AnxietySelf-Esteem Visitor 912 words

You have rehearsed. You know the job. And in the corridor outside, your heart is going hard enough that you can feel it in your throat, and the whole thing feels like a test of whether you are any good as a person.

Then afterward you replay the one question you fumbled, in detail, for three days, and conclude you have no chance.

What is actually happening in the room

It helps to know what the other side of the table is doing, because it is far less dramatic than it feels.

Interviewers are tired. They have a list of questions, often several candidates in a day, and they are trying to work out whether you can do the job and whether working with you would be all right. They are not assessing your worth as a human being, and they are not watching for the small things you are watching for.

Nerves themselves are expected. Everybody is nervous. A shaking voice or a long pause is so ordinary that it barely registers with the person asking, however loud it is inside your head.

Prepare the shape, not a script

A memorized answer is brittle. Lose one line and the whole thing collapses, and it sounds recited even when it survives.

What holds under pressure is a small set of stories. Four or five things you have actually done, each one roughly: what the situation was, what you did, how it turned out. Most interview questions are a door into one of those stories, and you can walk through whichever door opens.

Prepare your own ending, too. One sentence on why you want this job and one on what you would bring. If your mind empties, those are the two things you can always reach for.

Have something ready for the blank

If your mind goes empty, the silence is survivable, and the panic about the silence is what makes it long. So have lines ready.

Let me think about that for a second.

Can I come back to that one at the end.

Do you mean a time with a client, or within the team, so I answer the right thing.

Interviewers read these as considered. Nobody experiences them as failure, which is exactly what you cannot believe in the moment and is true anyway.

Steady the body, because the body is the problem

You will not think your way calm in a corridor. Use physical things.

Get there early enough to stop rushing. Walk around the block first, which burns off some adrenaline. Cold water on your wrists. Let your breath out slowly, longer out than in, a few times, which lowers your heart rate when nothing else will. Press your feet into the floor while you sit. Hold your hands in your lap rather than hiding them if they shake.

Once in the room, slow down deliberately: speak a little more slowly than feels natural, and take a sip of water between questions. Nerves make people speed up, and speeding up makes you feel more panicked, so the slowing is doing real work.

Turn your attention outward

The core of the misery is self-watching: tracking your voice, your face, how you are coming across. Attention spent on that is attention not spent on the question, which is why self-conscious people interview below their ability.

Put your attention on them instead. Listen properly to the question. Get curious about the job. Ask something you actually want to know. Curiosity and self-consciousness cannot occupy the same space, and the one you choose is the one that makes you sound like someone worth hiring.

Stop reading rejection as a verdict on you

This is where self-esteem gets mixed into something it does not belong in. A hiring decision is made out of fit, budget, internal candidates, one person's preference, and who they saw last. Plenty of good people are turned down for reasons that have nothing to do with their ability.

So treat an interview as a numbers exercise rather than a judgment. Ask for feedback, note anything useful, and get the next one in the diary. Nobody who hires you later will know or care that this one said no.

Review it once, briefly, then stop

Replaying it for days is not learning, it is punishment, and it makes the next interview harder. Give it ten minutes: two things that went well, one thing to do differently, one question to prepare better. Write those down. Then close it.

Practice out loud, with a person

Rehearsing in your head is far easier than speaking, which is why it does not transfer. Say the answers out loud, to a friend, to a camera, to the wall. The gap between knowing what you think and being able to say it is exactly what an interview tests, and it closes with practice rather than with more thinking.

When to get help

If interview dread is keeping you from applying, if you are turning down chances you want, or if the fear of being judged runs through much more of your life than hiring, it is worth talking to a therapist. Social anxiety is one of the most treatable things there is, and this is standard ground for someone who works with it. If a run of rejections has left you flat for weeks, please mention that to your doctor as well, because that is a different problem from nerves and it has its own help.

If you are in crisis, call or text 988 (Suicide & Crisis Lifeline, US) or your local emergency number. Theraglee is a wellbeing tool, not a substitute for professional care.
