What a Panic Attack Does to Your Body, and Why It Ends

A panic attack feels like an emergency. It is actually a false alarm, and understanding what your body is doing during one is the first step to being less afraid of the next.

PanicAnxiety Visitor 985 words

A panic attack is one of the most frightening experiences a body can produce, and one of the least dangerous. Both of those things are true. If you have had one, you know the first part. This article is about the second, because understanding what is actually happening makes the next attack smaller.

What it feels like

It usually comes fast, often out of nowhere. Your heart pounds or skips. You cannot get a full breath. Your chest is tight, your hands tingle, you feel dizzy or unreal, like you are watching yourself from a distance. Sweating, shaking, nausea. And on top of all of it, a wave of certainty that something terrible is about to happen: you are having a heart attack, you are going to pass out, you are losing your mind, you are going to die.

Then, within a few minutes to half an hour, it fades. You are left drained, shaky, and scared of when it will happen again.

What is actually happening

Your body has an alarm system. When it senses a threat, it floods you with adrenaline and prepares you to fight or run. Heart rate goes up to pump blood to your muscles. Breathing speeds up to bring in oxygen. Blood moves away from your hands and gut, which is why they tingle and churn. Your vision sharpens and your sense of the world can feel strange. This is a good system. It has kept humans alive for a very long time.

A panic attack is that system firing with no threat in the room. It is a false alarm. The sensations are real, and they are the same sensations you would have if you were running from actual danger. What is missing is the danger.

Here is the piece that turns a false alarm into a full attack. You feel your heart pound and you think, something is wrong with my heart. That thought is itself a threat, so the alarm fires harder. Your heart pounds more. You think, this is getting worse, I am going to die. The alarm fires harder still. The attack is a loop: sensation, scary interpretation, bigger sensation.

Why it always ends

Adrenaline has a short life. Your body cannot sustain that level of alarm for long, and it does not try to. The chemicals break down, the system resets, and the sensations fade whether or not you do anything. Every panic attack you have ever had has ended. Every one you will ever have will end too.

This is worth saying plainly because in the middle of an attack it feels like it could go on forever, or get worse forever. It cannot. There is a ceiling, and there is a clock.

The symptoms are not what they seem

Your heart pounding is not a heart attack. It is a healthy heart doing exactly what adrenaline tells it to do. The feeling of not getting enough air is not suffocation. You are actually breathing more than you need, and that over-breathing is what causes the dizziness and tingling. Feeling unreal is not losing your mind. It is a known effect of adrenaline on perception, and it passes with the rest.

If you have never had your heart checked and you are worried, see your doctor, get the reassurance, and then let yourself believe it. Many people who have panic attacks have had their hearts tested and know they are fine, and the reassurance helps.

What to do in the middle of one

The instinct is to fight it, to escape, to do something urgently. Try the opposite. Tell yourself, this is a panic attack. It is a false alarm. It will pass. Naming it interrupts the loop, because a panic attack is a known thing with a known ending, and a heart attack is not.

Slow your exhale. You do not need more air; you need less. Breathe in for a count of four and out for a count of six or longer. Do that for a minute. It will not stop the attack instantly, but it stops feeding it.

Let the sensations be there. This is the strange part, and the most effective. Instead of pushing the pounding heart away, notice it with something like curiosity. There is my heart, doing its thing. There is the tingling. You are not trying to enjoy it. You are just declining to treat it as an emergency, which is what the loop needs to keep going.

Stay where you are if you safely can. Leaving teaches your brain that the place was dangerous, which makes the next attack more likely there. Staying, and having the attack pass anyway, teaches it the opposite.

Between attacks

The fear of the next attack can shrink your life more than the attacks themselves. You start avoiding the store, the highway, the crowded room. Each avoided place is a small victory for the fear. Gently going back to those places, ideally with the tools above, is how the fear loses ground.

It also helps to look at what else is going on. Panic attacks often show up when stress has been building for a while, when sleep is short, or when you have been running on caffeine and adrenaline for weeks. The attack is sometimes the body's way of saying it cannot keep this up.

When to get help

Panic is very treatable, and you do not have to white-knuckle your way through it alone. If attacks are frequent, if you are reorganizing your life to avoid them, or if the fear between attacks never really lets up, please talk to a therapist or your doctor. There are specific, well-understood approaches for panic, and most people who use them get real relief. If you ever feel that you cannot keep yourself safe, reach out right away. In the US you can call or text 988 at any time.
