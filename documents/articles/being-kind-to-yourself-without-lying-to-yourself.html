Being Kind to Yourself Without Lying to Yourself

Positive affirmations feel false and self-criticism feels true. A middle option: saying the accurate thing about how you feel, which works better than either.

DBTSelf-Compassion Visitor 908 words

You have tried the kind version. I am doing my best, I am enough, be gentle with yourself. And something in you answers immediately: no I am not, that is nonsense, you dropped the ball on Tuesday.

So you go back to the other voice, the one that calls you useless, because at least that one sounds like it is telling the truth.

Why the nice sentences bounce off

Reassurance only lands when you already half believe it. Said against a strong belief, it starts an argument, and you lose arguments with yourself.

Worse, when the kind sentence is plainly untrue — I am great at this, when you have just made a mess of it — your own mind flags it as false, and the whole exercise feels embarrassing.

So the problem is not that kindness does not work. It is that most kind sentences people are handed are claims about your worth, and claims can be disputed.

The alternative is accuracy

There is a middle between flattery and attack, and it is simply describing what is actually happening.

This is hard, and it makes sense that I am struggling. I am exhausted, which is why this feels impossible. Anyone would find that conversation frightening. I did badly at this and I was also doing it with no sleep.

Notice what those have in common: nothing in them is deniable. They are not compliments. They name a situation and grant that your reaction fits it, which is the thing people are actually short of.

Validation, in the technical sense, is not saying good job. It is saying: that follows.

How to say it in practice

Three steps, and you can do them in thirty seconds.

Name the feeling plainly. I am ashamed. I am furious. I am frightened about Friday.

Then find the reason it makes sense, without arguing the facts. Of course I am ashamed, I care about doing this well and it went wrong in front of people.

Then say what is also true. And it was one meeting, and I can fix the part that was mine.

That last step is where the balance lives: it does not cancel the feeling, and it does not let the feeling have the whole floor.

What to do when it was your fault

This is where people get stuck, because self-compassion sounds like letting yourself off.

It is not. The accurate version includes the part you got wrong: I was short with him, that was not fair, and I was running on four hours of sleep. All three facts are true at once, and the middle one does not require the self-attack.

In fact the self-attack usually gets in the way of repair. People in a shame spiral avoid the person they hurt; people who can say I got that wrong, plainly, actually apologize.

So the test is not whether a sentence is nice. It is whether it moves you toward doing something useful.

Say it as you would to a friend

Not the words a friend would say to flatter you, the words a good friend would actually use.

A good friend does not say you are perfect. They say: of course you are upset, that was a horrible week, and you still managed to get the kids to school. Warmth and accuracy together.

If you cannot find that voice for yourself, borrow it. Write what you would say to somebody you like who was in exactly your position, then read it back addressed to you. People find this uncomfortable and effective.

Keep a few true sentences ready

Under pressure you will not compose anything, so have three or four lines prepared and written down.

This is hard and it makes sense that it is hard. I have got through worse than this. I do not have to feel better to do the next thing. Being tired is not a character flaw. This will look smaller on Thursday.

Flat, factual, no flattery. Say them out loud if you can; something about hearing them helps more than thinking them.

Expect it to feel odd before it feels useful

If the critical voice has run things for years, the accurate voice will sound weak at first, and you may notice a small panic that without the harshness you will stop trying.

That fear is common and it is not borne out. Self-criticism is a poor long-term motivator — it tends to produce avoidance rather than effort — while being able to say this is hard and I am still going is what keeps people working at things.

Give it a few weeks of unimpressive practice. It is a skill rather than a conversion.

When to get help with it

Please talk to a therapist or a counselor if the voice in your head is harsh in a way you would never use with anyone else, if nothing you do ever counts, or if the self-attack has gone on so long that you cannot remember another way of speaking to yourself.

This is one of the most practical areas in therapy, and it responds well: a counselor will work on exactly these sentences with you and keep you honest about the ones you skip. And if the self-criticism has come with a mood that has stayed low for weeks, mention that to a doctor too; it is common, treatable, and much easier to work on with help than alone.
