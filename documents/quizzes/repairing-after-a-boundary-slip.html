What your score suggests

0 – 8

One slip and it’s over

A single slip seems to convince you that the boundary, and maybe you, were never any good, and the line stays down after that. That harshness is learned, and it makes holding limits harder, not easier. Next time one slips, try saying the boundary again the very next day, out loud, with no speech about failure. A therapist can help soften the inner critic.

9 – 12

Hard on yourself afterward

You do reset your boundaries after a slip, but not before giving yourself a hard time, and sometimes not before it has slipped a few more times. That is ordinary and improvable. Try shortening the lecture: one sentence about what happened, one about what you will do next. Then stop.

13 – 16

Reset without much fuss

You treat a slip as information, reset fairly quickly, and mostly speak to yourself the way you would to a friend. That is a resilient way to hold limits. Notice which boundary tends to slip repeatedly, and ask whether it needs a new shape rather than more willpower.

17 – 20

Practiced, not perfect

You expect boundaries to wobble now and then, repair them without shame, and can even tell the other person you are changing course. That is mature and rare. Keep it by staying honest about slips that are becoming a pattern. And if someone you love collapses after one slip, you can show them what a reset looks like.

A self-assessment is a mirror, not a diagnosis. If anything here worries you, bring it to a licensed professional.
